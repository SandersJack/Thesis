    Over the past 75\,years the study of kaon physics has been integral to the development of the standard model. Since 2016, the NA62 experiment has been the leading experiment in the field of kaon physics. However, 
    with the NA62 experiment scheduled up to CERN Long Shutdown 3 (LS3), which is expected in autumn 2026, it is important to investigate future experiments. It was then required to investigate the future of kaon physics and the potential experiments that could succeed NA62. This chapter will discuss the potential future of kaon physics, the experiments that could be used to further the field, and the 
    work I contributed to these experiments.
    \section[HIKE]{High Intensity Kaon Experiments (HIKE)}
    The High-intensity kaon experiments (HIKE) was a proposed project to continue kaon physics at CERN in the ECN3 experimental hall and represented a long-phased program after LS3. HIKE would have profited from a four to six-fold increase in beam intensity compared to NA62 and included cutting-edge detector technologies that will be described in this 
    section. The beam intensity requirements for HIKE are described in Table \ref{tab:hikePhases}. To accommodate the higher intensities, upgrades were needed to the infrastructure that provided the beam to ECN3. A number of work groups such as the ECN3 beam delivery group within the Physics Beyond Colliders community at CERN \cite{Gatignon:2650989} was already conducting efforts to consolidate all the work required to upgrade infrastructure.
    Three phases were foreseen within the HIKE program, the first being a continuation of NA62 charged kaon physics with the high-intensity beam and detector upgrades to accommodate the rate increase with the goal of measuring the branching ratio of $K^+ \to \pi^+ \nu \bar\nu$ to 5\,\% precision. The second phase would have involved neutral kaon physics with charged secondary particle tracking with the goal to measure the branching ratio of $K_L \to \pi^0 l^+ l^-$. 
    The third phase would have been designed to observe the $K_{L} \to \pi^{0}\nu\bar{\nu}$ decay and measure its branching ratio to a 20\,\% precision.

    \subsection{Phase 1}
    The experimental design of NA62 has been proven as a successful setup for the precision measurement of the $K^{+} \to \pi^{+}\nu\bar{\nu}$ decay, described in Section \ref{sec:na62res}. The HIKE Phase 1 detector setup shown in Figure \ref{fig:Phase1}, is similar to the NA62 setup described in Section \ref{sec:na62} with new and upgraded detectors. The HIKE detectors were required to withstand a
    four times greater particle rate with respect to NA62 equating to $1.3\, \times 10^{13}$ protons per spill on the target. The expected beam had similar characteristics to NA62 with a  \SI{75}{\giga\electronvolt\per\clight} secondary beam produced from the  \SI{400}{\giga\electronvolt\per\clight} primary beam from the SPS accelerator onto a target at an angle of 0\,rad. 
    \begin{figure}[H]
        \centering
        \includegraphics[width=0.99\textwidth]{imgs/Phase1.png}
        \caption{Diagram of HIKE Phase 1 detector setup \cite{thehikecollaboration2023high}.}%
        \label{fig:Phase1}%
    \end{figure}  
    The detectors before the fiducial volume included an upgraded photon-detection system for the KTAG, described in Section \ref{sec:ktag_upgrade}, and a new GTK chip design. It was proposed to upgrade the existing NA62 Vetocounter with scintillating fibre (SciFi) technologies to improve the granularity 
    and to accommodate the increased beam intensity. 

    Major upgrades were envisioned to the LAV and STRAW detectors within the fiducial volume. A number of LAV stations were to be upgraded to a new lead scintillator design, similar to the Vacuum Veto System (VVS) planned for the CKM experiment \cite{frank2001ckm,ramberg2003photon}. The LAV detectors were crucial to the subsequent phases of HIKE, especially 
    Phase 3, and were designed to fulfil the requirements of the $K_{L} \to \pi^{0}\nu\bar{\nu}$ measurement even when used in Phase 1. The STRAW spectrometer was to be upgraded to use novel 5\,mm straw design with a layout shown in Figure \ref{fig:5mmstraw}. The reduction in the straw diameter would have decreased drift times while increasing granularity and resulting in an 
    expected improvement in the track angle and momentum resolution of 10-20\,\% compared to NA62.
    \begin{figure}[H]
        \centering
        \includegraphics[width=0.7\textwidth]{imgs/5mm_straw.png}
        \caption{Diagram of the new STRAW spectrometer layout with 5\,mm straw tubes \cite{thehikecollaboration2023high}.}%
        \label{fig:5mmstraw}%
    \end{figure} 
    Downstream of the fiducial volume, the PMT arrays of the RICH detector would have been upgraded, described in Section \ref{sec:rich_upgrade}. The HIKE timing detector would profit from the current NA62-CHOD detector with upgraded PMTs, which were to be chosen from the outcome of 
    studies from other detectors such as KTAG, RICH and MUVs. For HIKE Phase 1, the LKr was proposed as the primary electromagnetic calorimeter given its excellent energy resolution, but the required timing resolution was to be achieved by upgrading 
    its readout system. A number of other detectors would have been upgraded or replaced with higher granularity, such as the hadronic calorimeter, MUVs, IRC, and SAC.

    It was expected that Phase 1 would run between LS3 and LS4, with the exact years being undetermined. About $2.5 \times 10^{-6}$ kaon decays in the fiducial volume was expected per proton on target during HIKE phase 1. Given the expected protons per spill of $1.3 \times 10^{13}$ (four times NA62) and a nominal year of 200 data-taking days with an average of 3000 spills per day, would result in an 
    expected $2 \times 10^{13}$ kaon decays per year were foreseen during HIKE phase 1. As a consequence the Phase 1 goals could be completed in 5 years of data taking.
    \subsection{Phase 2}
    HIKE Phase 2 would have been a multi-purpose neutral $K_{L}$ decay experiment with the primary goal being to measure the branching ratios of the $K_{L} \to \pi^{0} l^{+} l^{-}$ decays. The detector setup for phase 2, shown in Figure \ref{fig:Phase2}, would have involved minimal modification to the experimental 
    setup of Phase 1, with the removal of detectors that were only applicable to the charged kaon beam. The KTAG, GTK, RICH, and SAC detectors would have been removed, and the STRAW spectrometer would have been shortened to account for a softer momentum of the secondary particles. The STRAW chambers would have been reconfigured to have 
    central beam holes to account for the neural beam. The MNP33 magnet required a reduction in strength by 20\,\% to account for the shorter spectrometer, resulting in a momentum kick of \SI{210}{\mega\electronvolt\per\clight}. The fiducial volume would have been increased to 90\,m compared to 75\,m in Phase 1.
    \begin{figure}[H]
        \centering
        \includegraphics[width=0.99\textwidth]{imgs/Phase2.png}
        \caption{Diagram of HIKE Phase 2 detector setup \cite{thehikecollaboration2023high}.}%
        \label{fig:Phase2}%
    \end{figure}  
    The design proposed a production angle of 2.4\,mrad on the target producing an expected $K_{L}$ yield of $5.4 \times 10^{-5}$ per proton. Assuming an integrated proton flux of $1.2 \times 10^{19}$ protons on target per year would have resulted in $3.8 \times 10^{13}$ $K_{L}$ decays in the fiducial volume per year. The mean beam momentum at the entry of the fiducial volume was expected to be 
    \SI{79}{\giga\electronvolt\per\clight} with a mean beam momentum for $K_{L}$ which decay of \SI{46}{\giga\electronvolt\per\clight}. 
    
    HIKE Phase 2 was planned to follow after Phase 1, which in turn was schedules after LS4 up to LS5. The expected protons per spill in Phase 2 was six times that of NA62 at $2 \times 10^{13}$. 
    Assuming 200 days of data taking and 3000 spills per day (as in Phase 1) and an $3.2 \times 10^{−6}$ $K_L$ per proton on target would result in $3.8 \times 10^{13}$ decays per year. The expected time to Phase 2 goals was 6 years of data taking. 
    \subsection{Phase 3 (KLEVER)}
    HIKE phase 3, previously known as KLEVER \cite{ambrosino2019klever}, was an experiment designed to measure the $K_{L} \to \pi^{0}\nu\bar{\nu}$ decay. The experimental setup is shown in Figure \ref{fig:Phase3} and differs from the design used at NA62 and HIKE Phase1/2. The setup consists of a large number of photon detectors located around a 160\,m long vacuum volume to produce a complete 
    coverage of photons produced from $K_{L}$ decays. The detectors include coverage for photons emitted up to 100\,mrad and allow for photons emitted with an angle up to 7.5\,mrad to be detected by the main electromagnetic calorimeter (MEC). 
    \begin{figure}[H]
        \centering
        \includegraphics[width=0.99\textwidth]{imgs/Phase3.png}
        \caption{Diagram of HIKE phase 3 detector setup \cite{thehikecollaboration2023high}.}%
        \label{fig:Phase3}%
    \end{figure}  
    Due to the significantly different experimental setup, Phase 3 was separated from the HIKE proposal submitted in 2023 \cite{thehikecollaboration2023high}. However, some detectors would have been shared between the two experiments as the granularity and efficiency requirements for Phase 3 were driven the detector requirements for the first two phases, reducing the overall costs. The LAVs, MEC, IRC, SAC and the hadronic calorimeters, which would have been built for HIKE Phase 1/2, 
    had all designs driven by their usage in Phase 3. The upstream veto, charged particle veto, and the pre-shower detectors are unique to Phase 3.
    \section{Flexible Software}\label{sec:na62fw}
    As briefly described in Section \ref{sec:introSoft}, the software for NA62 is split into three main \sloppy\texttt{C++} packages. 
    NA62MC is a GEANT4-based Monte Carlo simulation package that simulates the NA62 experiment. The MC utilises functions within GEANT4 to construct the sub-detectors of NA62, including all their materials and structures. It is possible to select a number of kaon decays to simulate in order to create signals similar to what are produced in reality at the experiment. After the simulation is complete, the software saves the signals in each detector in a ROOT file for use in reconstruction or analysis. 
    NA62Reconstruction is then used to reconstruct either the Monte Carlo data or collected data from the experiment, producing hits within the detectors and clustering them into candidates. NA62Analysis is used to analyse the data produced from the reconstruction to conduct all the physics studies that are performed at NA62. 

    The NA62FW was designed for use only with the NA62 experimental setup, resulting in minimal configurability and flexibility. Within the simulation, the only option for users to modify was to enable/disable sub-detectors. With the experiment foreseen to run up to CERN Long shutdown 3 (LS3), an upgraded software package was required to allow for the development of new detectors for future kaon experiments. 
    The software had to be flexible and configurable enough to allow for the simultaneous use for NA62 data taking and physics analysis, as well as the development of new experiments.
    To implement a flexible framework, all detector classes were refactored to inherit from a common class, which contained the essential functions and variables for all detectors. This allowed for all classes of the detectors to be parsed using the same machinery. The input to the simulation was then changed from a simple text file, to a single configuration file for all the detectors. The configuration file contained any arguments required for individual detectors which are defined in Table \ref{tab:config_variables}, including detectors upgraded later on in this Chapter. The X, Y, and Z placement of the back face of the detectors, along with the rotation, were also defined in this file. 
    An example entry in the configuration file for a STRAW station is shown in Figure \ref{fig:config}, where a name was given for the station "STRAW0", along with chamber ID, chamber type which defines the size of the straws and the chamber geometry which determines if the beam hole is symmetric or not.

    When constructed inside the simulation, each detector was contained within its own separate outer volume called the "Responsibility Region" (RR). 
    The creation of the RR was a simple task for most detectors apart from the STRAW and LAV which are made up of multiple stations that were not continuous, resulting in multiple responsibility regions for each station.

    \subsection{Testing: Geometry and Magnetic Fields}
    After the simulation was upgraded, all the geometries of each detector had to be validated to ensure that they were still correct with respect to the previous version. This was due to the complex nature of refactoring the detectors with a number of volume changes and the way the detectors were constructed. The validation was completed using a virtual particle within the GEANT4 framework called a Geantino. The Geantino is a particle that traverses the simulation in a straight line and interacts with all volumes 
    present in the simulation but will not be affected by magnetic fields. A large square beam of these particles was sent through the simulation, and every time the particle passed across a boundary between two volumes, its position was saved. Using this information it was possible to reconstruct the entire geometry of each detector in order to check the placement and geometry to mm precision to ensure that the simulation was still accurate. An example of the Geantino hits on a single view of STRAW chamber 1 is shown in Figure \ref{fig:geantino_plot} where 
    it is possible to see the features of the view, such as the missing straws within the centre, used to produce the chamber beam hole. For the full validation, a larger sample of Geantinos was used to achieve the required precision. However, if there was a discrepancy between the new version and the previous, a smaller area was selected to create a more precise picture of what was incorrect. This information was then used to modify the software to fix any geometry issues.
    This check was conducted for all sub-detectors.

    \begin{figure}[H]
        \centering
        \includegraphics[width=0.7\textwidth]{plots/straw_V0.pdf}
        \caption{Geantino hits on a single view in STRAW chamber 1.}%
        \label{fig:geantino_plot}%
    \end{figure} 

    To reduce the chance of overlaps and to clean up the simulation, the size of the unused space in all sub-detectors surrounding the physical geometry was reduced. The RR for all sub-detectors was reduced to just slightly larger than the physical detector. It is possible to see this change in figure \ref{fig:fields}, which depicts the direction of the magnetic fields. As the magnetic fields are only calculated in volumes, it is possible to see the reduction in RR size around the geometry. In \ref{fig:fields}(top) the field was calculated outside of the 
    actual physical sub-detector due to the larger RR, whereas in \ref{fig:fields}(bottom), the RR has been reduced to a few mm's larger than the actual physical detector. As well as reducing the chance of overlaps, reducing the RR would speed up the simulation slightly due to the reduction in unnecessary calculations for the magnetic field outside of the detector geometry.
    To improve the usability for users a geometry check is completed every time before the simulation starts. Each outer detector volume is checked for any overlaps with other detectors in all three dimensions, taking into account any rotations and the shape of the outer volumes. If an overlap of two sub-detectors is found, the simulation does not start, and an error is produced to the user describing where the overlap is and what sub-detectors are involved.

In NA62MC the magnetic field is split into three distinct regions: the Bluetube field within the decay region which is used for small corrections,
    the fringe field located at the edges of the MNP33 magnetic field and the MNP33 field which is the main magnetic field defined in the simulation from the MNP33 dipole magnet. In the previous version of the simulation, the position of the magnetic fields was written within the static code. However, with the flexible version, it is possible to move the MNP33 magnet to any position via the geometry input file. This required the magnetic field to be calculated relative to the central position of the MNP33 dipole magnet. An example of this flexibility is shown in Figure \ref{fig:moved_mag} where 
    the MNP33 magnet was moved from 198\,m to 163\,m, which is in front of the STRAW chamber 1. The MNP33 and fringe magnetic field was recalculated from the new position at 163\,m to account for the placement of the MNP33 magnet.

This was done using similar machinery to the geometry test completed previously but with a different GEANT particle, the charged Geantino. This is similar to a normal Geantino, where the particle traverses all volumes within its path but interacts with magnetic fields. As the charged Geantino enters a magnetic field, it is deflected as if it were a positively charged particle. This test ensured that the same tracks of the charged Geantinos between the previous version and the new flexible version were being produced within the magnetic fields. 
    An example of the tracks taken by the charged Geantinos within the simulation is shown in Figure \ref{fig:mag_check}, where it is possible to see the displacement of the particles within the magnetic field regions. The exact XY positions of the hits would then be validated against a sample of charged Geantinos with the exact same characteristics and starting parameters produced with the previous version of the software.

    With minimal modification it would also be possible to add the functionality to add multiple magnets within the simulation, producing a combined field. However, this was not implemented into the framework as its use was not foreseen, but the machinery was developed and is ready for use when required. 
    After the geometry and magnetic field tests were completed, full test productions of $K^+ \to \pi^+ \pi0$,  $K^+ \to \pi^+ \pi^- \pi^+$ and $K^+ \to \mu^+ \bar{\nu}$ were produced to test the validity of the existing NA62 setup along with the entire software chain (MC$\to$Reconstruction$\to$Analysis). 

    After testing, the software was ready to be used to develop new detectors and produce physics studies for HIKE.

    \section{The KTAG upgrades for HIKE} \label{sec:ktag_upgrade}
    To sustain the increased kaon rate, the KTAG required upgrades to the photon-detection and data acquisition systems, allowing to reach the required time resolution of 15-20\,ps and kaon identification efficiency of greater than 95\,\%. The expected kaon rate in HIKE Phase 1 was 200\,MHz, which corresponds to a maximum rate of \SI{10}{\mega\hertz\per\centi\meter\squared} for the detected photons at the PMT array. This rate takes into account the 
    production of Cherenkov photons in CEDAR-H and the resultant acceptance of the photons on the PMT plane. It was proposed that the NA62 PMT arrays were replaced with arrays of micro-channel plate photomultipliers (MCP-PMTs). The MCP-PMTs would provide a single photon time resolution of 50-70\,ps depending on the model, a low dark rate of \SI{1}{\kilo\hertz\per\centi\meter\squared} and the ability to sustain the required photon rate. 
    A downside of the MCP-PMTs is their limited lifetime from the ageing of the photocathode caused by feedback ions from the residual gas resulting in a rapid reduction in the Quantum Efficiency (QE) \cite{KISHIMOTO2006204}. This results in the rapid degradation of PMT quantum efficiency with the increasing integrated anode charge.
    %% Describe MCP-PMT with diagram
    MCP-PMTs are similar to normal PMTs except that the dynode section is replaced by a micro-channel plate. An example diagram is shown in Figure \ref{fig:mcppmt_dia}, where a photon hits the photocathode window, producing a single electron, which is then accelerated towards the MCP by an electric field provided by a voltage difference across the gap. The electron then collides with the walls of the MCP, producing secondary electrons, which results in a cascade collected by the anodes below.
    The dual layer design, or chevron, adds additional benefits compared to a single layer design such as better gains due to the increased surface for secondary electrons to be produced 
    %and improved lifetime due to the suppression of ions produced in the amplification from reaching the photocathode. 
    \begin{figure}[H]
        \centering
        \includegraphics[width=0.7\textwidth]{imgs/mcp_diag.pdf}
        \caption{Diagram describing the workings of a MCP-PMT.}%
        \label{fig:mcppmt_dia}%
    \end{figure}  
    Major breakthroughs to improve the lifetime of the MCP-PMT have come with the method of Atomic Layer Deposition (ALD) \cite{BEAULIEU200981}, where the channels of the MCP are coated with a ultra-thin layer of \ce{Al2O3} or \ce{MgO}. The layer prevents the out-gassing from the MCP glass substrate and drastically reduces the heavy ions reaching the photocathode resulting in significant increases in lifetime of the MCP-PMTs \cite{Lehmann_2018}. 
    Utilising the method of ALD, MCP-PMTs from Photonis have been demonstrated to withstand an Integrated Anode Charge (IAC) greater than \SI{20}{\coulomb\per\centi\meter\squared} \cite{LEHMANN2020161821}, corresponding to a lifetime improvement of a factor of 100 compared to standard MCP-PMTs, shown in Figure \ref{fig:mcppmt_life}.

    \section{RICH Upgrade} \label{sec:rich_upgrade}
    The overall design of the NA62 RICH described in section \ref{sec:RICH} was well suited for re-use within HIKE with an upgrade to the photon-detection system. The NA62 RICH photon detection system comprises two arrays on either side of the beam pipe, equipped with Hamamatsu R7400-U03 PMTs placed in a pattern described in Figure \ref{fig:richPMTArray}. 
    The PMTs have a single hit time resolution of 240\,ps and a quantum efficiency of 20\,\%. Due to the placement of the PMTs, the distance between the centre of each PMT is 18\,mm, which is constrained by the sensor size and the geometry of the PMT itself. This distance between sensors was the main contributor to the single hit position resolution of 4.7\,mm, 
    resulting in an overall ring radius resolution of 1.5\,mm. The number of hits per ring candidate and the single hit time resolution produced a time resolution of 80\,ps. Due to the increased beam intensity, the main requirement of the RICH for HIKE was a time resolution of 20 - 30\,ps.
    \begin{figure}[H]
        \centering
        \includegraphics[width=0.7\textwidth]{plots/NA62_RICH.pdf}
        \caption{Diagram of RICH PMT array}%
        \label{fig:richPMTArray}%
    \end{figure}  
    The proposed RICH photon detection system upgrade would replace the current PMTs with a smaller sensor size, allowing for a smaller distance between sensor centres and reducing the hit position resolution. An excellent candidate for a sensor replacement was silicon photomultipliers (SiPMs). SiPMs with small sensor sizes, high quantum efficiency of 40\,\% and a single hit time resolution 
    of at least 100\,ps are available from major manufacturers. Three sensor sizes were selected for further investigation, $9 \times 9\,mm$, $6 \times 6\,mm$, and $3 \times 3\,mm$. Each SiPM sensor size had apparent benefits and downsides, and a balance between performance and complexity/price had to be taken into account. The smaller sensors would produce a better hit position resolution but at an increased cost and complexity. Whereas the larger sensors would have worse hit position resolution but at reduced complexity and cost. 
    A study was conducted to determine the optimal sensor size for the SiPMs, balancing overall detector performance with cost and complexity. 

    Using the upgraded NA62 software package described in Section \ref{sec:na62fw}, it was possible to implement the new PMTs into the RICH Monte Carlo simulation with the implemented arrays shown in Figure \ref{fig:RICH_PMTs}. Compared to the PMT arrays currently used in NA62, the PMs have been aligned on the vertical axis to simply the placement because of the square geometry of the new PMTs. The area of the expected light spot, shown in Figure \ref{fig:RICH_PMTs}, as a grey circle, is completely covered by the SiPMs with 
    no gaps between the sensors which was a clear improvement compared to the NA62 setup shown in Figure \ref{fig:richPMTArray}.

    \section{Detector Rates}
    After the new GTk and RICH detectors were developed, it was necessary to determine the expected particle rate in the upgraded and non-upgraded detectors. The upgraded STRAW detector utilising 5\,mm diameter straws was also included in the HIKE simulations.  
    It was possible to estimate the expected rate for Phase 1 by scaling the known NA62 rates by the expected increase in beam intensity by a factor of 4 compared to NA62. 
    The rates for Phase 2 were calculated using the new simulation software described in the previous sections. 
    
    An array of input files were produced in BDSIM \cite{Nevay2020BDSIM} by the CERN beam group which contained the simulated HIKE beam components at a Z of 120\,m, which was the start of the fiducial volume.
    The files were then used as an input for the NA62MC software and used as the primary beam instead of the turtle beam, which is the default for NA62. Each file contained 10000 events, equaling 10000 protons on target, and there were 18K files produced in total, which was equivalent to $1.8 \times 10^8$ protons on target. 
    As this wouldn't be enough to get a reasonable estimate of the rates, each file was used 11 times as an input to the simulation with random seeds to increase the statistics. The simulation was run with the unforced flag which allows for four main $K_L$ decays: $ K_L \to \pi^{\pm} e^{\mp} \nu_e$ ($K_{e3}^{0}$), $ K_L \to \pi^{\pm} \mu^{\mp} \nu_{\mu}$ ($K_{\mu 3}^{0}$), $ K_L \to \pi^{0} \pi^{0} \pi^{0}$ and $ K_L \to \pi^{+} \pi^{-} \pi^{0}$.
    After the simulation, the files were then reconstructed using the NA62 Reconstruction software, and the rates were calculated by scaling the numbers in the output by the expected HIKE Phase 2 beam conditions, a spill time of 4.8 seconds and a beam intensity of $2 \times 10^{13}$ protons per spill.

    For the detector design, it was also important to calculate the total rates produced by different particles, which is shown in Table \ref{tab:TotalRates}. It's clear that the majority of the rates at the detector planes were from photons, especially in the spectrometer. However, the rate from charged particles, which the detectors would be mainly designed for, is reasonable, suggesting that the detectors could be designed and built with current technologies.
    
    With all rates information from the simulation shown in this section, it would then be possible to design the detectors that were not already upgraded within the simulation. Table \ref{tab:TotalRates} was used in the HIKE proposal to demonstrate the requirement for upgrading the detectors to handle the increased rates. The particle rates are collected not from the geometrical acceptance of the corresponding sub-detectors but from the cross-section of the outer volume for the sub-detector found in Monte Carlo. 
    For the Straw-1\&3, a circle of radius of 141.5\,mm, NCHOD a square of length 330\,mm, CHOD a square of length 244\,mm, IRC a square of length 122\,mm, MEC a circle of radius 182.5\,mm, MUV-1\&2 a square of length 320\,mm,  MUV-3 a square of length 281\,mm and HAC a square of length 281\,mm. A geometrical cut has been made to remove particles that pass through the checkpoints within the expected beam dispersion radius calculated to be $0.4\times10^{-3}\times Z$, where Z is the position of the checkpoint.
