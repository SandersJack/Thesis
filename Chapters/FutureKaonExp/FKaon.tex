    Over the past 75\,years the study of kaon physics has been integral to the development of the standard model. Since 2016, the NA62 experiment has been the leading experiment in the field of kaon physics. However, 
    with the NA62 experiment scheduled up to CERN Long Shutdown 3 (LS3), which is expected in autumn 2026, it is important to investigate future experiments. It was then required to investigate the future of kaon physics and the potential experiments that could succeed NA62. This chapter will discuss the potential future of kaon physics, the experiments that could be used to further the field, and the 
    work I contributed to these experiments.
    \section[HIKE]{High Intensity Kaon Experiments (HIKE)}
    The High-intensity kaon experiments (HIKE) was a proposed project to continue kaon physics at CERN in the ECN3 experimental hall and represented a long-phased plan after LS3. HIKE would have profited from a four to six-fold increase in beam intensity compared to NA62 and included cutting-edge detector technologies that will be described in this 
    section. The beam intensity requirements for HIKE are described in Table \ref{tab:hikePhases}. To accommodate the higher beam intensities, upgrades were needed to the beam-lines that provided the beam to ECN3. A number of work groups such as the ECN3 beam delivery group, Physics Beyond Colliders was already conducting efforts to consolidate all the work required to upgrade infrastructure.
    There were three phases within the HIKE program, the first being a continuation of NA62's charged kaon physics with the high-intensity beam and detector upgrades to accommodate the rate increase. The second would have involved neutral kaon physics with charged secondary particle tracking, and the third phase would have been designed to measure the $K_{L} \to \pi^{0}\nu\bar{\nu}$ decay.

    \subsection{Phase 1}
    The experimental design of NA62 has been proven as a successful setup for the precision measurement of the $K^{+} \to \pi^{+}\nu\bar{\nu}$ decay \ref{sec:na62res}. The HIKE Phase 1 detector setup is shown in Figure \ref{fig:Phase1} with new and upgraded detectors with respect to the NA62 setup described in Section \ref{sec:na62}. The new and upgraded detectors were required due to the 
    four times greater particle rate with respect to NA62 equating to $1.3\, \times 10^{13}$ protons per spill on the target. The expected beam had similar characteristics to NA62 with a $75\,Gev / c$ secondary beam produced from the $400\,GeV / c$ primary beam from the SPS accelerator onto a target at an angle of $0\,rad$. 
    \begin{figure}[H]
        \centering
        \includegraphics[width=0.99\textwidth]{imgs/Phase1.png}
        \caption{Diagram of HIKE Phase 1 detector setup \cite{thehikecollaboration2023high}}%
        \label{fig:Phase1}%
    \end{figure}  
    The upgraded detectors before the fiducial volume included an upgraded photon-detection system for the KTAG, described in Section \ref{sec:ktag_upgrade}, and a new GTk chip design described in section \ref{sec:gtk_upgrade}. It was proposed to upgrade the existing NA62 Vetocounter with scintillating fibre (SciFi) technologies to improve the granularity 
    and to accommodate the increased beam intensity. 

    Major upgrades were envisioned to the LAV and STRAW detectors within the fiducial volume. A number of LAV stations would have been upgraded to a new design proposed for a lead scintillator design, similar to the Vacuum Veto System (VVS) planned to be used at the CKM experiment \cite{frank2001ckm,ramberg2003photon}. The LAV detectors were important for the subsequent phases of HIKE, especially for 
    Phase 3, as they would have been designed for the requirements of the $K_{L} \to \pi^{0}\nu\bar{\nu}$ measurement even when used in this Phase. The STRAW spectrometer would have been upgraded to use a new 5\,mm straw design shown in Figure \ref{fig:5mmstraw}. The reduction in straw diameter would have decreased drift times and increased granularity resulting in an 
    expected improvement in the track angle and momentum resolution of 10-20\,\% compared to NA62.
    \begin{figure}[H]
        \centering
        \includegraphics[width=0.7\textwidth]{imgs/5mm_straw.png}
        \caption{Diagram of the expected layout of the 5\,mm straws \cite{thehikecollaboration2023high}}%
        \label{fig:5mmstraw}%
    \end{figure} 
    Downstream of the fiducial volume, the PMT arrays of the RICH detector would have been upgraded, described in Section \ref{sec:rich_upgrade}. The timing detector would profit from the current NA62-CHOD detector with upgraded PMTs, which would have been chosen from the number of 
    studies from other detectors such as KTAG, RICH and MUV. For Phase 1, the primary electromagnetic calorimeter would have used the LKr from NA62, which had excellent energy resolution but did not satisfy the timing requirements. The required timing resolution would have been achieved by upgrading 
    the readout system. A number of other detectors would have been upgraded or replaced with higher granularity, such as the hadronic calorimeter, MUVs, IRC, and SAC.

    It was expected that Phase 1 would run between LS3 and LS4, with the exact years being undetermined. During Phase 1, the expected protons per spill was $1.3 \times 10^{13}$ (four times NA62) with a nominal year consisting of 200 data-taking days with an average of 3000 spills per day. With $2.5 \times 10^{-6}$ kaon decays in the fiducial volume per proton on target would result in an 
    expected $2 \times 10^{13}$ kaon decays per year. At this rate, it would be expected that the Phase 1 goals could be completed in 5 years of data taking.
    \subsection{Phase 2}
    HIKE Phase 2 would have been a multi-purpose neutral $K_{L}$ decay experiment with the primary goal being to measure the branching ratios of the $K_{L} \to \pi^{0} l^{+} l^{-}$ decays. The detector setup for phase 2, shown in Figure \ref{fig:Phase2}, would have involved minimal modification to the experimental 
    setup of Phase 1, with the removal of detectors that were only applicable to the charge kaon beam. The KTAG, GTk, RICH, and SAC detectors would have been removed, and the STRAW spectrometer would have been shortened to account for the softer momentum of the secondary particles. The chambers would have been reconfigured to have 
    central beam holes to account for the neural beam. The MNP33 magnet required a reduction in strength by 20\,\% to account for the shorter spectrometer, resulting in a momentum kick of $210 MeV / c$. Due to the compact detector, the fiducial volume would have been increased to 90\,m compared to 75\,m in Phase 1.
    \begin{figure}[H]
        \centering
        \includegraphics[width=0.99\textwidth]{imgs/Phase2.png}
        \caption{Diagram of HIKE Phase 2 detector setup \cite{thehikecollaboration2023high}}%
        \label{fig:Phase2}%
    \end{figure}  
    The design proposed a production angle of 2.4\,mrad on the target producing an expected $K_{L}$ yield of $5.4 \times 10^{-5}$ per proton. Assuming an integrated proton flux of $1.2 \times 10^{19}$ protons on target per year would have resulted in $3.8 \times 10^{13}$ $K_{L}$ decays in the fiducial volume per year. The mean momentum at the entry of the fiducial volume was expected to be 
    $79\,GeV/c$ with a mean beam momentum for $K_{L}$ which decay of $46$\,GeV/c. 
    
    Phase 2 would be expected to follow after Phase 1, which would have been expected after LS4 up to LS5. The expected protons per spill was six times that of NA62 at $2 \times 10^{13}$. With a similar standard year as Phase 1 and an expected $3.2 \times 10^{−6}$ $K_L$ per proton on target would result in $3.8 \times 10^{13}$ decays per year. The expected time to Phase 2 goals was 6 years of standard data taking. 
    \subsection{Phase 3 (KLEVER)}
    HIKE phase 3, previously known as KLEVER \cite{ambrosino2019klever}, was an experiment designed to measure the $K_{L} \to \pi^{0}\nu\bar{\nu}$ decay. The experimental setup is shown in Figure \ref{fig:Phase3} and differs from the design used at NA62 and HIKE Phase1/2. The setup consists of a large number of photon detectors located around a 160\,m long vacuum volume to produce a complete 
    coverage of photons produced from $K_{L}$ decays. The photon-detectors include coverage for photons emitted up to 100\,mrad and allow for a free path of photons to the main electromagnetic calorimeter (MEC) emitted up to 7.5\,mrad. 
    \begin{figure}[H]
        \centering
        \includegraphics[width=0.99\textwidth]{imgs/Phase3.png}
        \caption{Diagram of HIKE phase 3 detector setup \cite{thehikecollaboration2023high}}%
        \label{fig:Phase3}%
    \end{figure}  
    Due to the differing experimental setup, Phase 3 was separated from the HIKE proposal submitted in 2023 \cite{thehikecollaboration2023high}. However, some detectors would have been shared between the two experiments as the granularity and efficiency requirements for Phase 3 would have driven the detector requirements for the first two phases, reducing overall costs. The LAVs, MEC, IRC, SAC and the hadronic calorimeters, which would have been built for HIKE Phase 1/2, would have been 
    designed for use in Phase 3. The upstream veto counter, charged particle veto, and the pre-shower detector are unique to Phase 3. 
    \section{KTAG Photon Detection System Upgrade} \label{sec:ktag_upgrade}
    To sustain the increased kaon rate, the KTAG required upgrades to the photon-detection system and data acquisition system, with an expected time resolution of 15 - 20\,ps and a kaon identification efficiency of greater than 95\,\%. The expected kaon rate was 200\,MHz which corresponds to a maximum particle rate of $10$\,MHz/c$m^{2}$ for the photons at the PMT array. This took into account the 
    production of Cherenkov photons in CEDAR-H and the resultant acceptance of the photons on the PMT plane. It was proposed that the NA62 PMT arrays would be replaced with arrays of micro-channel plate photomultipliers (MCP-PMTs). The MCP-PMTs would provide a single photon time resolution of 50 - 70\,ps depending on the model, a low dark rate of $1$\,kHz/c$m^{2}$ and the ability to sustain the required photon rate. 
    A downside of the MCP-PMTs is their limited lifetime from the ageing of the Photo-Cathode caused by heavy feedback ions from the residual gas. This results in the rapid degradation of PMT quantum efficiency with the increasing integrated anode charge.
    %% Describe MCP-PMT with diagram
    MCP-PMTs are similar to normal PMTs except that the dynode section is replaced by a micro-channel plate. An example diagram is shown in Figure \ref{ig:mcppmt_dia}, where a photon hits the photocathode window, producing a single electron, which is then accelerated towards the MCP by an electric field provided by a voltage difference across the gap. The electron then collides with the walls of the MCP, producing secondary electrons, which results in a cascade which is then collected by the anodes below.
    The duel layer design, or chevron, adds additional benefits compared to a single layer design: better gains due to the increased surface for secondary electrons to be produced and improved lifetime due to the suppression of ions produced in the amplification from reaching the photocathode. 
    \begin{figure}[H]
        \centering
        \includegraphics[width=0.7\textwidth]{imgs/mcp_diag.pdf}
        \caption{Diagram describing the workings of a MCP-PMT.}%
        \label{fig:mcppmt_dia}%
    \end{figure}  
    An example of the expected MCP-PMT model to be used in the KTAG manufactured by Photonics is shown in Figure \ref{fig:mcppmt}. The module consists of an 8x8 array of pixels which can individually be read out.
    \begin{figure}[H]
        \centering
        \includegraphics[width=0.7\textwidth]{imgs/mcp_pmt_dia.png}
        \caption{Schematic of Photonics MCP-PMT \cite{RONZHIN201552}}%
        \label{fig:mcppmt}%
    \end{figure}  
    Each of the eight sectors would consist of four of these MCP-PMT, shown in Figure \ref{fig:mcp_ktag}. The four MCP-PMT per sector were required in order to have full coverage of the light spot produced when the light exiting the CEDAR was 
    reflected by the spherical mirror. The schematic in Figure \ref{fig:mcppmt} shows there was some unused space around the active area of each MCP-PMT, which would result in reduced efficiency, especially in the centre, where the maximum amount of light would be expected. To mitigate this, reflective surfaces were attached to each side of the individual MCP-PMTs shown in Figure \ref{fig:mcp_ktag} to reflect the light onto the active surface.

The position on the MCP-PMT in the KTAG sector was optimised to produce the maximum number of photo-electrons per kaon candidate.

The distribution for the Hi-QE Blue was implemented into the Monte Carlo, but further studies at The University of Birmingham are planned to take place to characterise the MCP-PMTs by measuring the lifetimes, QE, and other characteristics in order to decide which type could be used

    \section{RICH Upgrade} \label{sec:rich_upgrade}
    The overall design of the NA62 RICH described in section \ref{sec:RICH} was well suited for re-use within HIKE with an upgrade to the photon-detection system. The NA62 RICH photon detection system comprises two arrays on either side of the beam pipe, equipped with Hamamatsu R7400-U03 PMTs placed in a pattern described in Figure \ref{fig:richPMTArray}. 
    The PMTs have a single hit time resolution of 240\,ps and a quantum efficiency of 20\,\%. Due to the placement of the PMTs, the distance between the centre of each PMT is 18\,mm, which is constrained by the sensor size and the geometry of the PMT itself. This distance between sensors was the main contributor to the single hit position resolution of 4.7\,mm, 
    resulting in an overall ring radius resolution of 1.5\,mm. The number of hits per ring candidate and the single hit time resolution produced a time resolution of 80\,ps. Due to the increased beam intensity, the main requirement of the RICH for HIKE was a time resolution of 20 - 30\,ps.
    \begin{figure}[H]
        \centering
        \includegraphics[width=0.7\textwidth]{plots/NA62_RICH.pdf}
        \caption{Diagram of RICH PMT array}%
        \label{fig:richPMTArray}%
    \end{figure}  
    The proposed RICH photon detection system upgrade would replace the current PMTs with a smaller sensor size, allowing for a smaller distance between sensor centres and reducing the hit position resolution. An excellent candidate for a sensor replacement was silicon photomultipliers (SiPMs). SiPMs with small sensor sizes, high quantum efficiency of 40\,\% and a single hit time resolution 
    of at least 100\,ps are available from major manufacturers. Three sensor sizes were selected for further investigation, $9 \times 9\,mm$, $6 \times 6\,mm$, and $3 \times 3\,mm$. Each SiPM sensor size had apparent benefits and downsides, and a balance between performance and complexity/price had to be taken into account. The smaller sensors would produce a better hit position resolution but at an increased cost and complexity. Whereas the larger sensors would have worse hit position resolution but at reduced complexity and cost. 
    A study was conducted to determine the optimal sensor size for the SiPMs, balancing overall detector performance with cost and complexity. 

    Using the upgraded NA62 software package described in Section \ref{sec:na62fw}, it was possible to implement the new PMTs into the RICH Monte Carlo simulation with the implemented arrays shown in Figure \ref{fig:RICH_PMTs}. Compared to the PMT arrays currently used in NA62, the PMs have been aligned on the vertical axis to simply the placement because of the square geometry of the new PMTs. The area of the expected light spot, shown in Figure \ref{fig:RICH_PMTs}, as a grey circle, is completely covered by the SiPMs with 
    no gaps between the sensors which was a clear improvement compared to the NA62 setup shown in Figure \ref{fig:richPMTArray}.

The Hamamatsu S13360-**50CS series was selected as the preliminary candidate for the RICH PMT upgrade to achieve this requirement. Hamamatsu offers a number of SiPMs in the S13360 series, with the **50CS selected for its improved Ultra Violet (UV) sensitivity and high quantum efficiency of 40\,\% at 460\,nm, 
    shown in Figure \ref{fig:RICH_PMT_QE}.

    Along with the QE efficiency, a single hit time resolution of 100\,ps was required \cite{thehikecollaboration2023high}. The manufacturer stated the Hamamatsu S13360-**50CS series has excellent time resolution, but no definite value was given. The time resolution would also depend on the final design of the RICH readout electronics and would have to be measured in a test setup. The SiPMs from Hamamatsu have previously been tested and have reached a single hit time resolution below 100\,ps \cite{CARNESECCHI2020164484}. 
    For use in the simulation a conservative simple Gaussian time response curve with a sigma of 100\,ps was used for the simulations, which would be updated later on for a specific model to be implemented when chosen.

With the increased quantum efficiency, the expected number hits per $\pi^{+}$ between 15 and 45\,$GeV/c$ was expected to increase by a factor of two. This, along with the improved time resolution of the new PMTs resulted in an expected time $\pi^+$ time resolution of 27\,ps at 15\,$GeV/c$ and 20\,ps at 45\,$GeV/c$.

    \section{Detector Rates}
    After the new GTk and RICH detectors were developed, it was necessary to determine the expected particle rate in the upgraded and non-upgraded detectors. The upgraded STRAW detector utilising 5\,mm diameter straws was also included in the HIKE simulations.  
    It was possible to estimate the expected rate for Phase 1 by scaling the known NA62 rates by the expected increase in beam intensity by a factor of 4 compared to NA62. 
    The rates for Phase 2 were calculated using the new simulation software described in the previous sections. 
    
    An array of input files were produced in BDSIM \cite{Nevay2020BDSIM} by the CERN beam group which contained the simulated HIKE beam components at a Z of 120\,m, which was the start of the fiducial volume.
    The files were then used as an input for the NA62MC software and used as the primary beam instead of the turtle beam, which is the default for NA62. Each file contained 10000 events, equaling 10000 protons on target, and there were 18K files produced in total, which was equivalent to $1.8 \times 10^8$ protons on target. 
    As this wouldn't be enough to get a reasonable estimate of the rates, each file was used 11 times as an input to the simulation with random seeds to increase the statistics. The simulation was run with the unforced flag which allows for four main $K_L$ decays: $ K_L \to \pi^{\pm} e^{\mp} \nu_e$ ($K_{e3}^{0}$), $ K_L \to \pi^{\pm} \mu^{\mp} \nu_{\mu}$ ($K_{\mu 3}^{0}$), $ K_L \to \pi^{0} \pi^{0} \pi^{0}$ and $ K_L \to \pi^{+} \pi^{-} \pi^{0}$.
    After the simulation, the files were then reconstructed using the NA62 Reconstruction software, and the rates were calculated by scaling the numbers in the output by the expected HIKE Phase 2 beam conditions, a spill time of 4.8 seconds and a beam intensity of $2 \times 10^{13}$ protons per spill.

    However, as the rates are collected from the cross-section at the expected detector position, the rates are higher than what would be seen due to the fact that all the detectors would eventually have a beam hole to accommodate beam particles passing through the detector. An example cross-section at the entry of the first STARW chamber is shown in Figure \ref{fig:STRAW1_rate}, where it is clear that the highest rate from the decay particles was found at the centre. As the detectors were not designed and the beam hole size was unknown, it was possible to estimate the size by calculating the beam divergence at the detector position.
    This was calculated using Equation \ref{eq:beamDivergence}.
    \begin{equation}\label{eq:beamDivergence}
        \delta_{beam} = 0.4\,mrad \times Z
    \end{equation}
