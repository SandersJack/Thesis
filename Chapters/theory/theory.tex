    \section{The Standard Model of Particle Physics}

    The \gls{SM} of particle physics is a \gls{QFT} that describes the fundamental matter and three of the four forces in the universe. The matter content of the SM comprises 12 elementary fermions (and their anti-particles), 
    five vector bosons that mediate the three fundamental forces, and a scalar boson responsible for particle masses (Figure~\ref{fig:SM}). 
    There are six quark flavours: up, down, charm, strange, top and bottom. Depending on their charge, they are separated into three generations or two types. There are also three lepton flavours, each with a corresponding neutrino.
    It is important to note that the SM is not a complete theory. An example of this is the gravitational force, which is not included in the \gls{SM}. However, at the small scales of particle physics, this force is neglected due to its weaker coupling compared to the other three forces (strong, electromagnetic and weak).
    \begin{figure}[t]
        \centering
        \includegraphics[width=0.5\textwidth]{imgs/SM.pdf}
        \caption{The particle content of the Standard Model of particle physics with the 12 fermions shown in purple and green, the five vector bosons shown in red and the Higgs scalar boson shown in yellow.}%
        \label{fig:SM}%
    \end{figure}
    
    The \gls{SM} has a total non-Abelian gauge symmetry group of SU(3)×SU(2)×U(1). 
    The SU(3) group governs the strong interactions between quarks in the form of \gls{QCD}. The strong force is mediated by the gluon.
    The SU(2)×U(1) groups describe the electroweak interactions \cite{GLASHOW1961579}, which are mediated by the photon and $W^{\pm}$, $Z^{0}$ bosons. The SU(3) is an exact gauge symmetry while the electroweak symmetry is spontaneously broken by the Higgs field \cite{Higgs:1964pj}, resulting 
    in the massive electroweak bosons ($W^{\pm}$ and $Z^{0}$). 
    \section{Quark Mixing and the CKM Matrix}
    Electroweak symmetry breaking gives rise to massive quarks, with the strong and weak eigenstates diverging. The weak eigenstates ($d'$, $s'$, $b'$) of down, strange and bottom quarks, which are associated with the weak interaction, are related to the mass
    eigenstates ($d$, $s$, $b$) through the \gls{CKM} matrix $V_{\text{CKM}}$ \cite{PhysRevLett.10.531,10.1143/PTP.49.652}, with the following relation:
    \begin{equation}
    \begin{bmatrix} d' \\ s' \\ b' \end{bmatrix} =
        V_{\text{CKM}}
        \begin{bmatrix} d \\ s \\ b \end{bmatrix} = 
        \begin{bmatrix} 
            V_{ud} & V_{us} & V_{ub} \\ 
            V_{cd} & V_{cs} & V_{cb} \\ 
            V_{td} & V_{ts} & V_{tb} 
        \end{bmatrix}
        \begin{bmatrix} d \\ s \\ b \end{bmatrix}.
    \end{equation}
    The elements of the CKM matrix determine the strength of the interaction between quark flavours. Interaction vertices involving the transition from a quark type $i$ to $j$ introduce a coupling factor of $V_{ij}$ and a corresponding transition probability given by $|V_{ij}|^2$.
    %Utilising the elements of the \gls{CKM} matrix, it is possible to calculate the probability that a quark type i will transition into type j with $|V_{ij}|^2$.

    As a unitary $3\times3$ matrix, the \gls{CKM} matrix can be described by four parameters: three mixing angles ($\theta_{12}$, $\theta_{13}$, $\theta_{23}$) which dictate the couplings between each generation of quarks and one phase ($\delta$) which introduces \gls{CP} violation \cite{10.1143/PTP.49.652}. 
    A number of different parametrisations of the matrix are possible, but the three-angle and one-phase formalism is the standard choice \cite{PhysRevLett.53.1802}, 
    \begin{equation}\label{eq:angles}
    V_{\text{CKM}} =
    \begin{bmatrix}
    c_{12} c_{13} & s_{12} c_{13} & s_{13} e^{-i\delta} \\
    - s_{12} c_{23} - c_{12} s_{23} s_{13} e^{i\delta} & c_{12} c_{23} - s_{12} s_{23} s_{13} e^{i\delta} & s_{23} c_{13} \\
    s_{12} s_{23} - c_{12} c_{23} s_{13} e^{i\delta} & - c_{12} s_{23} - s_{12} c_{23} s_{13} e^{i\delta} & c_{23} c_{13}
    \end{bmatrix}
    \end{equation}  
    where $c_{ij} = \cos{\theta_{ij}}$ and $s_{ij} = \sin{\theta_{ij}}$. The mixing angles $\theta_{ij}$ can be selected to satisfy $0 \leq \theta_{ij} \leq \frac{\pi}{2}$ such that $c_{ij}, s_{ij} \geq 0$.

    Experimental data shows that there is a hierarchical structure to the components of the \gls{CKM} matrix, 
    \begin{equation}
        s_{12} \ll s_{23} \ll s_{13}.
    \end{equation}
    Based on this hierarchy, the Wolfenstein parametrisation \cite{PhysRevLett.51.1945} describes the \gls{CKM} using the parameters:
    \begin{equation}
        \begin{split}
            s_{12} &= \lambda, \\
            s_{23} &= A\lambda^2,\\
            s_{13} &= A\lambda^3(\rho + i\eta),
        \end{split}
    \end{equation}
    resulting in
    \begin{equation}\label{eq:wolf}
        V_{\text{CKM}} =
        \begin{bmatrix}
        1 - \frac{\lambda^2}{2} & \lambda & A\lambda^3 (\rho - i\eta) \\
        -\lambda & 1 - \frac{\lambda^2}{2} & A\lambda^2 \\
        A\lambda^3 (1 - \rho - i\eta) & -A\lambda^2 & 1
        \end{bmatrix}
        + O(\lambda^4).
    \end{equation}
    
    The \gls{CKM} matrix is expanded in terms of $\lambda$ and provides a hierarchical structure which reflects the pattern where transitions between quarks of the same/adjacent generations are stronger compared to distant ones.
    Expanding the \gls{CKM} matrix in powers up to $O(\lambda^3)$ results in the loss of unitary. However, unitary can be restored by including $O(\lambda^5)$ \cite{PhysRevD.50.3433} correction to the bottom left component of Equation~\ref{eq:wolf},
    \begin{equation}
        V_{td} = A\lambda^3 (1 - \bar{\rho} - i\bar{\eta}),
    \end{equation}
    where 
    \begin{equation}
        \begin{split}
            \bar{\rho} = \rho \left(1 - \frac{\lambda^2}{2}\right), \\
            \bar{\eta} = \eta \left(1 - \frac{\lambda^2}{2}\right).
        \end{split}
    \end{equation}

    The unitary nature of the CKM matrix imposes conditions on the rows and columns:
    \begin{equation}
        \begin{split}
            \sum_{i} V_{ij}V^*_{kj} = \delta_{ik} \\ 
        \end{split}
    \end{equation}
    where $\delta_{ik}$ denotes the Kronecker symbol, which equals 1 when elements $j = k$ and 0 otherwise. This results in six vanishing combinations of the \gls{CKM} matrix, which can be represented as triangles in the complex plane. 
    The most commonly used, with elements at leading order contributions of $A\lambda^3$ are,
    \begin{equation}
        V_{ud}V^*_{ub} + V_{cd}V^*_{cb} + V_{td}V^*_{tb} = 0.
    \end{equation}
    In the Wolfenstein parameterization (Equation \ref{eq:wolf}), the equation takes the form,  
    \begin{equation}\label{eq:wolf_uni}
        1 + \frac{V_{td}V^*_{tb}}{V_{cd}V^*_{cb}} = - \frac{V_{ud}V^*_{ub}}{V_{cd}V^*_{cb}} = \bar{\rho} + i\bar{\eta} \\ 
    \end{equation}
    leading to the triangle in Figure~\ref{fig:triange}.
    \begin{figure}[t]
        \centering
        \includegraphics[width=0.7\textwidth]{imgs/unitary.pdf}
        \caption{Sketch of the most common unitary triangle $V_{ud}V^*_{ub} + V_{cd}V^*_{cb} + V_{td}V^*_{tb} = 0$.}%
        \label{fig:triange}%
    \end{figure}

    The \gls{CKM} matrix components are fundamental parameters of the \gls{SM}. 
    A large number of experiments have contributed to the measurements of the \gls{CKM}, with a global fit yielding \cite{Charles:2004jd}:
    \begin{equation}
    \begin{bmatrix} 
        |V_{ud}| & |V_{us}| & |V_{ub}| \\ 
        |V_{cd}| & |V_{cs}| & |V_{cb}| \\ 
        |V_{td}| & |V_{ts}| & |V_{tb}| 
    \end{bmatrix} = 
    \begin{bmatrix}
        0.97436^{+0.00014}_{-0.00019} & 0.22498^{+0.00081}_{-0.00062} & 0.00373^{+0.00019}_{-0.00015} \\
        0.22484^{+0.00081}_{-0.00061} & 0.97351^{+0.00016}_{-0.00019} & 0.04160^{+0.00066}_{-0.00121} \\
        0.00857^{+0.00018}_{-0.00037} & 0.04088^{+0.00066}_{-0.00119} & 0.999125^{+0.000052}_{-0.000027}
    \end{bmatrix}.
    \end{equation}
    
    Through measurement of the \gls{CKM} matrix components, it is possible to constrain the unitary triangle of Figure~\ref{fig:triange}, as depicted in Figure~\ref{fig:excluded_tri},
    \begin{figure}[t]
        \centering
        \includegraphics[width=0.7\textwidth]{imgs/rhoeta_large.png}
        \caption{Constraints for the most common unitary triangle in the ($\bar{\rho}$,$\bar{\eta}$) plane \cite{Charles:2004jd}.}%
        \label{fig:excluded_tri}%
    \end{figure}

    Including the four \gls{CKM} components discussed above, there are nineteen free parameters of the \gls{SM}:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Four \gls{CKM} matrix parameters: three mixing angles and one CP-violating phase.
        \item Six quark masses: up, down, charm, strange, top, and bottom: $M_u$, $M_d$, $M_c$, $M_s$, $M_t$ and $M_b$.
        \item Three charged lepton masses: $M_e$, $M_\mu$, and $M_\tau$.
        \item Three gauge couplings associated with the Standard Model gauge group $\mathrm{SU}(3)_C \times \mathrm{SU}(2)_L \times \mathrm{U}(1)_Y$, which correspond to the strong, weak, and hypercharge interaction and are denoted by, $g_s$, $g$, and $g'$.
        \item Two Higgs sector parameters: the vacuum expectation value ($v$) and the Higgs boson mass ($M_H$).
        \item One \gls{QCD} vacuum angle ($\theta_{\text{QCD}}$), related to CP violation in the strong interaction.
    \end{itemize}
    Each of these parameters must be determined through experiment to the highest possible precision as the \gls{SM} does not predict their values. However, relations between the parameters, such as the unitary \gls{CKM} matrix, allows for internal consistency tests within the \gls{SM}.
    Any statistically significant deviations between experimental results and theoretical understanding or inconsistencies between different determinations of the parameters could be a hint of \gls{BSM} physics. 

    As mentioned previously, a feature of the \gls{CKM} matrix is that the diagonal components which describe the transitions within the quark generations dominate. The off-diagonal elements describing  
    flavour-changing charged current transitions are suppressed between one generation and doubly suppressed for transitions between two generations. This suppression between two generations leads to rare processes, such as decay $K^+ \to \pi^+ \nu \bar{\nu}$, discussed in Section \ref{sec:pinn_theory}. 
    \section{Kaon Physics}
    The first possible observation of a meson, a particle composed of a quark-antiquark pair, was made in 1943 by Leprince-Ringuet and L'heritier \cite{Cahn_Goldhaber_2009, 10.1063/PT.3.1989} and predated the discovery of the pion in 1947 \cite{Lattes1947}. Utilising a cloud chamber to measure cosmic rays, they found the first evidence of a particle with a mass of 
    990 times the mass of the electron \cite{Navas:2024}. However, given the uncertainty of the observation it was possible that the upper limit on the measured mass could reach the proton mass, therefore it was not credited for the discovery. In 1947, while similarly studying cosmic rays with cloud chambers, G. D. Rochester and C. C. Butler published a result which included
    two pictures showing forked tracks \cite{Rochester1947}. One of the tracks showed the decay of a neutral particle into two charged daughter particles, which turned out to be a decay of the neutral kaon to two charged pions. The other track showed the decay of a charged particle into charged and neutral daughter particles.
    This result was later confirmed in 1950 by a group in Cal-tech who observed multiple instances of these decays \cite{Seriff:1950ajp}. Between these publications, a group in Bristol used emulsion chambers to observe the decay of a charged particle into three charged particles \cite{Brown1949}, known then as the tau decay. 
    This resulted in what was known as the tau-theta problem, where seemingly the same particle was decaying into final states with different parities: Theta to two pions (+1) and Tau to three pions (-1). This eventually led to the discovery that parity is not conserved in weak interactions \cite{PhysRev.104.254}. 

    In the early 1950s, these particles would be described as K-mesons, for which there are four interaction (weak) eigenstates ($K^+$, $K^-$, $K^0$ and $\bar{K^0}$). These particles are produced via the strong interaction but decay via the weak interaction. Experiments continued to measure the characteristics of the $K$ mesons and found features such as unusually long lifetimes ($10^{-10}$ seconds) despite their rapid production \cite{PhysRev.86.663}. These features arise from the fact the production of $K$ mesons occurs through the strong interaction, whereas their decays proceed through the weak interaction.
    These deviations from the other previously discovered mesons, such as the pion, suggested the existence of a new quantum number named strangeness \cite{PhysRev.92.833, Gell-Mann:1956iqa}. 
    The strangeness quantum number applies to all strongly interacting particles and is conserved in strong interactions but violated in weak ones.

    Into the late 1950s and early 1960s, the neutral kaon became the centre of a number of studies because their relative ease of production and long lifetimes made them easy to study. Despite the fact that parity is violated in weak interactions, it was still believed that the combination of \gls{CP} would be conserved. 
    Through experiment, the two mass (strong) eigenstates of the neutral kaon showed to have different lifetimes, which resulted in their names $K_L$ for long and $K_S$ for short~\cite{BARDON1958156}. Each of these eigenstates had different \gls{CP} values with the $K_S$ having $CP = +1$ decaying to two pions and the $K_L$ having $CP = -1$ decaying to three pions.

    However, in 1964, it was observed that the $K_L$ also decayed to two pions \cite{PhysRevLett.13.138} proving that the \gls{CP} symmetry is indeed violated in weak decays. This decay is an example of indirect \gls{CP} violation as it occurs through neutral kaon mixing. The first evidence of direct \gls{CP} violation, where the violation occurs within the decay, was provided by the NA31 experiment in 1993 \cite{BARR1993233}. The process was conclusively observed by the KTeV and NA48 collaborations in the late 1990s \cite{1999335, PhysRevLett.83.22}.

        \begin{split}
            \mathcal{B}(K^+ \to \pi^+ \nu \bar{\nu}) &= \kappa_+(1+\Delta_{EM}) \Bigg[ \left( \frac{\mathfrak{Im}(\lambda_t)}{\lambda^5} X(x_t) \right)^2 \\
            &\quad + \left( \frac{\mathfrak{Re}(\lambda_c)}{\lambda} P_c(X) + \frac{\mathfrak{Re}(\lambda_t)}{\lambda^5} X(x_t) \right)^2 \Bigg]
        \end{split}    
    \end{equation}
    with 
    \begin{equation}
        \begin{split}
            \kappa_+ = (5.173 \pm 0.025) \times 10^{-11} \left(\frac{\lambda}{0.225}\right)^8, \\
            \Delta_{EM} = -0.003,
        \end{split}
    \end{equation}    
    where $x_t = M_t^2 / M_W^2$ is the ratio of the mass of the top quark and the mass of the $W^\pm$ boson, $\lambda$ originates from the Wolfenstein parametrisation of the \gls{CKM} matrix and $\lambda_i = V_{is}^*V_{id}$ where $V_{ij}$ are CKM matrix elements. The $\Delta_{EM}$ takes into account the correction from radiative photon exchanges, and $\kappa_+$ condenses the relevant hadronic matrix elements. 
    The terms $X(x_t)$ and $P_c(X)$ are the loop functions which describe contributions from the top and charm quark. The $X(x_t)$ can be described by the equation \cite{PhysRevD.83.034030, Buras2015}, 
    \begin{equation}
        X(x_t) = X^{(0)} + \frac{\alpha_s}{4\pi}X_t^{(1)} + \frac{\alpha}{4\pi}X_t^{(EW)} = 1.481 \pm 0.005 \pm 0.008
    \end{equation}
    where $X^{(0)}$ is the leading order contribution, $X_t^{(1)}$ is the next-to-leading order (NLO) \gls{QCD} correction and $X_t^{(EW)}$ is the electroweak correction.
    The $P_c(X)$ term describes the contribution from the charm quark which is defined as \cite{Buras_2015},
    \begin{equation}
        P_c(X) = P_c^{SD}(X) + \delta P_{c,u} = 0.404 \pm 0.024
    \end{equation}
    where $\delta P_{c,u}$ is the long distance contribution arising from non-local effects involving intermediate hadronic states and $P_c^{SD}(X)$ is the short distance contribution arising from loop diagrams with internal charm quarks \cite{Buras_2015}, 
    \begin{equation}
        P_c^{SD}(X) = \frac{1}{\lambda^4} \left(\frac{2}{3}X^e_{NNL} + \frac{1}{3} X^{\tau}_{NNL} \right)
    \end{equation}
    where $X^{l}_{NNL}$ are loop functions from the charm quark interacting with electron and tau neutrinos, produced from NLO \gls{QCD} and NNLO calculations .
    It is possible to represent $\lambda_t$ and $\lambda_c$ from Equation \ref{eq:pinn_theory_br} as \cite{Buchalla:1995vs}, %\cite{Buras2015}, 
    \begin{equation}
        %\mathfrak{Re}(\lambda_t) \simeq |V_{ub}||V_{cb}| \cos{\gamma}(1-2\lambda^2) + (|V_{ub}|^2 - |V_{cb}|^2)\lambda \left(1 - \frac{\lambda^2}{2}\right),
        \mathfrak{Re}(\lambda_t) = -(1-\frac{\lambda^2}{2})A^2\lambda^5(1-\bar{\rho}),
    \end{equation}
    \begin{equation}
        %\mathfrak{Im}(\lambda_t) \simeq |V_{ub}||V_{cb}|\sin{\gamma},
        \mathfrak{Im}(\lambda_t) = A^2\lambda^5\eta,
    \end{equation}
    \begin{equation}
        %\mathfrak{Re}(\lambda_c) \simeq -\lambda \left(1-\frac{\lambda^2}{2}\right),
        \mathfrak{Re}(\lambda_c) = -\lambda \left(1-\frac{\lambda^2}{2}\right),
    \end{equation}
    yielding the following prediction for the $K^+ \to \pi^+ \nu \bar{\nu}$ branching fraction (\ref{eq:pinn_theory_br}) in terms of the Wolfenstein parameters
    %and can be used to parametrise the $K_{\pi\nu\bar{\nu}}$ branching fraction (\ref{eq:pinn_theory_br}) with the CKM Wolfenstein parameters as \cite{Buchalla:1998ba}
    \begin{equation}\label{eq:branch_kpinunu}
        %\mathcal{B}(K^+ \to \pi^+ \nu \bar{\nu}) = \frac{\kappa_+}{\lambda^8}|V_{cb}|^4X(x_t)^2 \left(\sigma R_t^2\sin{\beta}^2 + 1/\sigma \left(R_t\cos{\beta} + \frac{\lambda^4P_c(X)}{|V_{cb}|^2 X(x_t)} \right)\right).
        \mathcal{B}(K^+ \to \pi^+ \nu \bar{\nu}) = \kappa_+ A^4X(x_t)^2\frac{1}{\sigma}((\sigma\bar{\eta})^2 + (\rho_0 - \bar{\rho})^2),
    \end{equation}
    where $\sigma = (1 / (1-\frac{\lambda^2}{2}))^2$ and $\rho_0 = 1 + \frac{P_0(X)}{A^2 X(x_t)}$. Equation \ref{eq:branch_kpinunu} represents an ellipse in the unitary triangle plane ($\bar{\rho},\bar{\eta}$) shown in Figure~\ref{fig:traingles_SM}, with centre at ($\rho_0, 0$), a semi-major axis equal to $\sqrt{\frac{\mathcal{B}}{\kappa_+ A^4X(x_t)^2}}$ and a semi-minor axis of $\sqrt{\frac{\mathcal{B}}{\sigma\kappa_+ A^4X(x_t)^2}}$. The ellipse constrains the parameters ($\bar{\rho},\bar{\eta}$) and is depicted in Figure~\ref{fig:traingles_SM_elip}. 
    
    The $K_L \to \pi^0 \nu \bar{\nu}$ decay is dominated by contributions from Feynman diagrams similar to Figure~\ref{fig:fey_pinn}, but with a down quark spectating the decay compared to an up quark for the charged mode. The decay is theoretically clean and almost entirely \gls{CP} violating, dominated by top-quark loop contributions \cite{PhysRevD.39.3322}. This gives a branching fraction depending only on the imaginary part of the top loop from Equation \ref{eq:pinn_theory_br}, which is \gls{CP} violating,
    \begin{equation}
        \mathcal{B}(K_L \to \pi^0 \nu \bar{\nu}) = \kappa_L \left(\frac{\mathfrak{Im}(\lambda_t)}{\lambda^5} X(x_t) \right)^2
    \end{equation}
    with
    \begin{equation}
        \kappa_L = (2.231 \pm 0.013) \times 10^{-10} \left(\frac{\lambda}{0.225}\right)^8.
    \end{equation}
    In the Wolfenstein parametrisation, the equation takes the form, 
    \begin{equation}
        \mathcal{B}(K_L \to \pi^0 \nu \bar{\nu}) = \kappa_L \eta^2 A^4 X(x_t)^2.
    \end{equation}
    The decay can be used to further constrain the parameter $\bar{\eta}$ and is shown in Figure~\ref{fig:traingles_SM} and \ref{fig:traingles_SM_elip}.

    \begin{equation}\label{eq:PNN_theory_val}
        \mathcal{B}(K^+ \to \pi^+ \nu \bar{\nu}) = (8.60 \pm 0.42) \times 10^{-11}
    \end{equation}
    and 
    \begin{equation}
        \mathcal{B}(K_L \to \pi^0 \nu \bar{\nu}) = (2.94 \pm 0.15) \times 10^{-11}.
    \end{equation}

    \subsection{The $K^0_L \to \pi^0 l^+l^-$ decay}\label{sec:kllep_theory}
    The decay $K^0_L \to \pi^0 l^+l^-$ is a \gls{FCNC} transition of a strange quark into a down quark and a lepton-lepton pair. The prediction of the branching fraction is less theoretically clean compared to $K \to \pi \nu \bar{\nu}$ with contributions through three processes: direct \gls{CP} violating, indirect \gls{CP} violating and \gls{CP} conserving components.
    The SM branching fraction for the $K^0_L \to \pi^0 l^+l^-$ decay can be described as \cite{Buchalla:2003sj,Isidori2004}
    \begin{equation}
        \mathcal{B}(K^0_L \to \pi^0 l^+l^-) = \left(C^l_{mix} \pm C^l_{int} \left(\frac{\mathfrak{Im}(\lambda_t)}{10^{-4}}\right) + C^l_{dir} \left(\frac{\mathfrak{Im}(\lambda_t)}{10^{-4}}\right) + C^l_{CPC} \right) \times 10^{-12},
    \end{equation}
    where $C^l_{mix}$ represents the contributions due to $K_S - K_L$ mixing, $C^l_{int}$ the interference of the indirect and direct \gls{CP} violating decays. The $\pm$ represents the constructive and destructive interference. The $C^l_{dir}$ is the contribution of direct \gls{CP} violating and $C^l_{CPC}$ is the long distance \gls{CP} conserving contribution. 
    This results in the branching fractions for the $K^0_L \to \pi^0 e^+e^-$ and $K^0_L \to \pi^0 \mu^+\mu^-$ decays, 
    \begin{equation} \label{eq:kl_piee}
        \mathcal{B}(K^0_L \to \pi^0 e^+e^-) = \left(15.7|a_S|^2 \pm 6.2 |a_S| \left(\frac{\mathfrak{Im}(\lambda_t)}{10^{-4}}\right) + 2.4 \left(\frac{\mathfrak{Im}(\lambda_t)}{10^{-4}}\right) \right) \times 10^{-12}
    \end{equation}
    and 
    \begin{equation} \label{eq:kl_pimumu}
        \mathcal{B}(K^0_L \to \pi^0 \mu^+\mu^-) = \left(3.7|a_S|^2 \pm 1.6 |a_S| \left(\frac{\mathfrak{Im}(\lambda_t)}{10^{-4}}\right) + 1.0\left(\frac{\mathfrak{Im}(\lambda_t)}{10^{-4}}\right) + 5.2 \right) \times 10^{-12}.
    \end{equation}
    The $|a_S|$ term depicts the decay form factor calculated from the measurement of the branching fraction of $K_S \to \pi^0 l^+l^-$ at NA48/1 experiment as $|a_S| = 1.2 \pm 0.2$ \cite{NA481:2003cfm, NA481:2004nbc}.
    Equations \ref{eq:kl_piee} and \ref{eq:kl_pimumu} show the main difference between the two decay modes. The \gls{CP} conserving contribution due to the two-photon intermediate state only occurs in the muon decay as it is negligible in the helicity-suppressed electron mode.

    Utilising the above equations, the expected SM branching fractions can be described as \cite{Mescia:2006jd},
    \begin{equation}
        \mathcal{B}(K^0_L \to \pi^0 e^+e^-) = 3.54^{+0.98}_{-0.85}(1.56^{+0.62}_{-0.49}) \times 10^{-11}
    \end{equation}  
    and 
    \begin{equation}
        \mathcal{B}(K^0_L \to \pi^0 \mu^+\mu^-) = 1.41^{+0.28}_{-0.26} (0.95^{+0.22}_{-0.21}) \times 10^{-11}.
    \end{equation}
    The separate values for the branching ratios represent the constructive vs destructive interference introduced in Equations \ref{eq:kl_piee} and \ref{eq:kl_pimumu}.  
    The state-of-the-art experimental constraints on the branching fraction of $K^0_L \to \pi^0 l^+l^-$ decays at a 90\,\% \gls{CL} are the upper limits obtained by the KTeV experiment \cite{PhysRevLett.93.021805, PhysRevLett.84.5279} 
    \begin{equation}
        \mathcal{B}(K^0_L \to \pi^0 e^+e^-) < 28 \times 10^{-11}
    \end{equation}  
    and 
    \begin{equation}
        \mathcal{B}(K^0_L \to \pi^0 \mu^+\mu^-) < 38 \times 10^{-11}.
    \end{equation}
    \section{Dark Photon} \label{sec:DarkP_th}
    As mentioned above, the \gls{SM} is not a complete theory. A significant component of the universe remains unexplained. For instance, the observed rise in the cosmic-ray positron fraction with energy, along with the anomaly in the muon's magnetic moment $(g-2)$ \cite{Muong-2:2023cdq}, suggest the possible existence of hidden sectors. 
    The hidden sectors is a collection of theories that propose the existence of new particles and forces that are not part of the \gls{SM}.
    A possible extension of the \gls{SM} foresees a new $U(1)$ gauge symmetry with an associated boson, the \gls{DP}, which kinematically mixes with the SM hypercharge $U(1)_Y$ \cite{Fabbrichesi:2020wbt}. There are two separate benchmark cases of the \gls{DP} specified by the Physics Beyond Colliders group for future study \cite{Beacham_2020}: 
    \begin{itemize}
        \item BC1 - The massive DP decays into only SM particles.
        \item BC2 - The DP is massive and decays mostly into dark-sector particles.
    \end{itemize}
    For the BC1 case, the hypothetical \gls{DP} (denoted $A'$) with a \si{\giga\electronvolt} scale mass could resolve some of the discrepancies arising from the observations of the $(g-2)$ muon gyromagnetic ratio measurement and the rise of cosmic-ray positron fraction with energy. The addition of the \gls{DP} introduces two unknown parameters: the mass $M_{A'}$ and a kinetic mixing parameter $\epsilon$ denoting the strength of the \gls{DP} interaction with SM particles. A \gls{DP} with a mass that is less than a neutral pion
    can be produced in the $\pi^0 \to \gamma A'$ decay. The branching fraction for the $\pi^0$ decay is \cite{PhysRevD.80.095024}
    \begin{equation}\label{eq:DP_branch}
        \mathcal{B}(\pi^0 \to \gamma A') = 2\epsilon^2 \left(1-\frac{M_{A'}^2}{M_{\pi^0}^2}\right)^3 \mathcal{B}(\pi^0 \to \gamma \gamma).
    \end{equation}
    
    Within the mass range considered for the \gls{DP}, the only decay into \gls{SM} particles that is both kinematically allowed and occurs at leading order in the coupling constant is $A' \to e^+ e^-$. 
    The $A'$ would decay via $A' \to \gamma \gamma \gamma$ and $A' \to \nu \bar{\nu}$ proceeding through higher-order processes, which are highly suppressed for low $\epsilon$ values. Hence, it is safe to assume that $\mathcal{B}(A' \to e^+e^- = 1$). This assumption allows the expected decay width to be described as \cite{PhysRevD.80.095024}
    \begin{equation}
        \Gamma_{A'} \approx \Gamma (A' \to e^+e^-) = \frac{1}{3} \alpha \epsilon^2 M_{A'} \sqrt{1 - \frac{4M_e^2}{M_{A'}^2}} \left( 1 + \frac{4M_e^2}{M_{A'}^2} \right),
    \end{equation}
    where $\alpha$ is the fine structure constant. For the \gls{DP} mass range $2M_e < M_{A'} < M_{\pi^0}$, the life time of the \gls{DP} is calculated, 
    \begin{equation}\label{eq:DP_lifetime}
        c \tau_{A'} = \frac{\hbar c}{\Gamma_{A'}} \approx \SI{0.8}{\micro\meter} \times \left(\frac{10^{-6}}{\epsilon^2}\right) \left(\frac{\SI{100}{\mega\electronvolt\per\clight}}{M_{A'}}\right)
    \end{equation}
    Through experiment it is possible to set limits on the branching ratio of the \gls{DP} production, with the final goal of setting limits on the mixing parameter. Within the \gls{DP} mass range $2M_e < M_{A'} < M_{\pi^0}$, the leading result for the upper limit of $\epsilon^2$ is from the NA48/2 experiment \cite{2015178}, shown in Figure~\ref{fig:na48_dp_lim}.
    The study described in Section \ref{sec:darkP} sets out to improve the limits on the \gls{DP} mixing parameter set by NA48/2 with data collected from the NA62 experiment.
