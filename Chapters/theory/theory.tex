    \section{Standard Model of Particle Physics}

    The Standard Model (SM) of particle physics is a framework which contains our current understanding of the matter and three of the four four forces found in the universe. Within the SM there are 12 elementary fermions, 
    5 vector bosons which mediate the three fundamental forces and a scalar boson responsible for particle masses (Figure \ref{fig:SM}). It is important to note that the SM is not a complete model of the universe. A great example of this is the gravitational force 
    which is not described by the theory, however at the small scales of of particle physics this force is neglected due to it's insignificant strength when compared to the other three forces (strong, electromagnetic and weak).
    \begin{figure}[H]
        \centering
        \includegraphics[width=0.7\textwidth]{imgs/SM.pdf}
        \caption{The Standard Model of particle physics with the 12 fermions shown in purple and green, the five vector bosons shown in red and the Higgs scaler boson shown in yellow.}%
        \label{fig:SM}%
    \end{figure}
    The SM comprises of a quantum field theory (QFT) and is based on the non-Abelian gauge symmetry groups SU(3)×SU(2)×U(1). The SU(3) governs the strong interactions and the SU(2)×U(1) the electroweak interactions. 
    Each group contains their respective gauge bosons: gluon for the strong and W, Z and photon for the electroweak. The SU(3) is an exact gauge symmetry where as the electroweak symmetry is spontaneously broken by the Higgs field, resulting 
    in the massive electroweak bosons ($W^{\pm}$ and $Z^{\pm}$). Due to this broken symmetry which gives rise to massive quarks, the strong and weak eigenstates diverge. The weak eigenstates (d', s', b') of down, strange and bottom quarks which are associated to the weak interaction are related to the mass
    eigenstates (d, s, b) through the Cabibbo-Kobayashi-Maskawa (CKM) matrix $V_{\text{CKM}}$ \cite{PhysRevLett.10.531,10.1143/PTP.49.652}. 
    \begin{equation}
    \begin{bmatrix} d' \\ s' \\ b' \end{bmatrix} =
        V_{\text{CKM}}
        \begin{bmatrix} d \\ s \\ b \end{bmatrix} = 
        \begin{bmatrix} 
            V_{ud} & V_{us} & V_{ub} \\ 
            V_{cd} & V_{cs} & V_{cb} \\ 
            V_{td} & V_{ts} & V_{tb} 
        \end{bmatrix}
        \begin{bmatrix} d \\ s \\ b \end{bmatrix}
    \end{equation}
    Utilising the elements of the CKM matrix it is possible to calculate the probability that a quark type i will transition into type j with $|V_{ij}|^2$.

    For a unitary 3x3 matrix the CKM can be reduced from nine parameters to four: three mixing angles ($\theta_{12}$, $\theta_{13}$, $\theta_{23}$) which dictate the couplings between each generation of quarks and one phase ($\delta$) which introduces CP violation \cite{10.1143/PTP.49.652}. 
    A number of different parametrisations are possible but the standard choice is \cite{PhysRevLett.53.1802}, 
    \begin{equation}\label{eq:angles}
    V_{\text{CKM}} =
    \begin{bmatrix}
    c_{12} c_{13} & s_{12} c_{13} & s_{13} e^{-i\delta} \\
    - s_{12} c_{23} - c_{12} s_{23} s_{13} e^{i\delta} & c_{12} c_{23} - s_{12} s_{23} s_{13} e^{i\delta} & s_{23} c_{13} \\
    s_{12} s_{23} - c_{12} c_{23} s_{13} e^{i\delta} & - c_{12} s_{23} - s_{12} c_{23} s_{13} e^{i\delta} & c_{23} c_{13}
    \end{bmatrix}
    \end{equation}  
    where $c_{ij} = \cos{\theta_{ij}}$ and $s_{ij} = \sin{\theta_{ij}}$. The mixing angles $\theta_{ij}$ can be selected to satisfy $0 \leq \theta_{ij} \leq \frac{\pi}{2}$ such that $c_{ij}, s_{ij} \geq 0$.

    Through experiments it is known that there is hierarchical structure to the components of the CKM matrix, 
    \begin{equation}
        s_{12} \ll s_{23} \ll s_{13}
    \end{equation}
    and it is useful to further parametrise through the Wolfenstein parametrisation \cite{PhysRevLett.51.1945} (Equation \ref{eq:wolf}). For this parametrisation the following definitions are used,
    \begin{equation}
        \begin{split}
            s_{12} &= \lambda \\
            s_{23} &= A\lambda^2 \\
            s_{13} &= A\lambda^3(\rho + i\eta)
        \end{split}
    \end{equation}
    resulting in, 
    \begin{equation}\label{eq:wolf}
        V_{\text{CKM}} =
        \begin{bmatrix}
        1 - \frac{\lambda^2}{2} & \lambda & A\lambda^3 (\rho - i\eta) \\
        -\lambda & 1 - \frac{\lambda^2}{2} & A\lambda^2 \\
        A\lambda^3 (1 - \rho - i\eta) & -A\lambda^2 & 1
        \end{bmatrix}
        + O(\lambda^4).
    \end{equation}
    Reducing the CKM to $O(\lambda^3)$ results in the loss of unity. However, unity can be restored by including $O(\lambda^5)$ \cite{PhysRevD.50.3433} correction to the bottom left component of Equation \ref{eq:wolf},
    \begin{equation}
        V_{td} = A\lambda^3 (1 - \bar{\rho} - i\bar{\eta})
    \end{equation}
    where 
    \begin{equation}
        \begin{split}
            \bar{\rho} = \rho(1 - \frac{\lambda^2}{2}) \\
            \bar{\eta} = \eta(1 - \frac{\lambda^2}{2})
        \end{split}
        .
    \end{equation}

    The unitary nature of the CKM matrix imposes conditions on the row and columns
    \begin{equation}
        \begin{split}
            \sum_{i} V_{ij}V^*_{ik} = \delta_{jk} \\ 
            \sum_{j} V_{ij}V^*_{kj} = \delta_{ik}
        \end{split}
    \end{equation}
    where $\delta_{jk}$ denotes the Kronecker delta function, which equals 1 when j = k and 0 otherwise. This results in six vanishing combinations of the CKM matrix which can be represented as triangles, with the most common being
    \begin{equation}
        V_{ud}V^*_{ub} + V_{cd}V^*_{cb} + V_{td}V^*_{tb} = 0
    \end{equation}
    and can be modified with the Wolfenstein parametrisation (Equation \ref{eq:wolf_uni}) which is depicted in Figure \ref{fig:triange}.
    \begin{equation}\label{eq:wolf_uni}
        1 + \frac{V_{td}V^*_{tb}}{V_{cd}V^*_{cb}} = - \frac{V_{ud}V^*_{ub}}{V_{cd}V^*_{cb}} = \bar{\rho} + i\bar{\eta} \\ 
    \end{equation}
    \begin{figure}[H]
        \centering
        \includegraphics[width=0.7\textwidth]{imgs/unitary.pdf}
        \caption{Sketch of the most common unitary triangle $V_{ud}V^*_{ub} + V_{cd}V^*_{cb} + V_{td}V^*_{tb} = 0$.}%
        \label{fig:triange}%
    \end{figure}

    The CKM matrix components are fundamental parameters of the SM. Through a large number of experiments the elements of the CKM matrix can be measured and are combined to form a global fit \cite{Charles:2004jd}:
    \begin{equation}
    \begin{bmatrix} 
        |V_{ud}| & |V_{us}| & |V_{ub}| \\ 
        |V_{cd}| & |V_{cs}| & |V_{cb}| \\ 
        |V_{td}| & |V_{ts}| & |V_{tb}| 
    \end{bmatrix} = 
    \begin{bmatrix}
        0.97436^{+0.00014}_{-0.00019} & 0.22498^{+0.00081}_{-0.00062} & 0.00373^{+0.00019}_{-0.00015} \\
        0.22484^{+0.00081}_{-0.00061} & 0.97351^{+0.00016}_{-0.00019} & 0.04160^{+0.00066}_{-0.00121} \\
        0.00857^{+0.00018}_{-0.00037} & 0.04088^{+0.00066}_{-0.00119} & 0.999125^{+0.000052}_{-0.000027}
    \end{bmatrix}
    \end{equation}
    A feature of the CKM matrix is that the diagonal components which describe the transitions within the quark generations dominate, compared to the off-diagonal elements which are suppressed for 
    transitions between one generation and doubly suppressed for transitions between two generations. This suppression between two generations produces rare decays, such as $K^+ \to \pi^+ \nu \bar{\nu}$ described in Section __. 

    Through measurement of the CKM matrix components it is possible to constrain the unitary triangle (Figure \ref{fig:triange}) shown in Figure  
    \begin{figure}[H]
        \centering
        \includegraphics[width=0.7\textwidth]{imgs/rhoeta_large.png}
        \caption{Constraints for the most common unitary triangle in the ($\bar{\rho}$,$\bar{\eta}$) plane \cite{Charles:2004jd}.}%
        \label{fig:excluded_tri}%
    \end{figure}

    Including the four CKM components discussed above there are nineteen free parameters of the SM:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Four CKM parameters discussed above.
        \item Six Quark masses (Up, down, charm, strange, top and bottom).
        \item Three Lepton masses (Electron, muon and tau).
        \item Three Gauge Couplings (U(1), SU(2) and SU(3))
        \item Two Higgs parameters (Vacuum expectation value and mass).
        \item Quantum chromodynamics (QCD) vacuum angle.
    \end{itemize}
    Each one of these parameters needs to be measured through experiment to the highest precision so that any discrepancies can be resolved. Any deviations from theoretical understanding could be hint of 
    physics beyond the Standard Model (BSM). 
    \section{Kaon Physics}
    The first possible observation a heavy meson was in 1943 by Leprince-Ringuet and L'heritier \cite{Cahn_Goldhaber_2009, 10.1063/PT.3.1989} and predated even the discovery of the pion in 1947 \cite{Lattes1947}. Utilising a cloud chamber to measure cosmic rays they found the first evidence of a particle with a mass of 
    990 times the mass of the electron (\SI{0.511}{\mega\electronvolt} \cite{Navas:2024}). However, it was possible that the upper limit on the measured mass could reach the proton mass so they were not credited with the discovery. In 1947 while similarly studying cosmic rays with cloud chambers G. D. Rochester and C. C. Butler published a result which included
    two pictures showing forked tracks \cite{Rochester1947}. One of the tracks proved to show the decay of a neutral particle into two charged daughter particles, which turned out to be decay of the neutral kaon to two pions. The other track showed the decay of a charged particle into charged and neutral daughter particles.
    This result was later confirmed in 1950 by a group in Cal-Tech who observed multiple instances of these decays \cite{Seriff:1950ajp}. However, between these publications a group in Bristol which included physicist Rosemary Brown, used emulsion chambers to observe the decay of a charged particle into three charged particles \cite{Brown1949}, known then as the tau decay. 
    This resulted in what was known as the tau-theta problem, where the seemingly the same particle was decaying into two different final states with different parities: Theta to two pions (+1) and Tau to three pions (-1). This eventually led to the discovery that parity is not conserved in weak interactions \cite{PhysRev.104.254}. 

    In the early 1950s these particles would be described as K-mesons, for which there are four types ($K^+$, $K^-$, $K^0$ and $\bar{K^0}$). Experiments continued to measure the characteristics of the K mesons and found features such as long lifetimes ($10^{-10}$ seconds) when compared to the fact there production was much faster \cite{PhysRev.86.663}. These deviations from the other mesons, mainly the pion suggested the existence of a new quantum number named strangeness \cite{PhysRev.92.833, Gell-Mann:1956iqa}. 
    The strange quantum number applies to all strongly interacting particle and is conserved in strong interactions but violated in weak interactions.

    Into the latter 1950s and early 1960s the neutral kaon became the center of number of studies. Despite the fact that parity is violated in weak interactions it was still believed that the combination of charge-parity (CP) would be conserved. 
    Through experiment the two weak eigenstates of the neutral kaon showed to have different lifetimes, which resulted in their names $K_L$ and $K_S$ \cite{BARDON1958156}. Each of these eigenstates had different CP values with the $K_S$ with $CP = +1$ decaying to two pions and the $K_L$ with $CP = -1$ observed decaying to three pions.

The first evidence of direct CP violation, where the violation occurs within the decay was provided by the NA31 experiment in 1993 \cite{BARR1993233}. The process was eventually discovered by KTeV and NA48 collaborations in the latter 1990s \cite{1999335, PhysRevLett.83.22}.

    The $K^+ \to \pi^+ \nu \bar{\nu}$ decays through Flavour Changing Neutral Current (FCNC) which are extremely suppressed in the SM due to CKM and GIM suppression \cite{PhysRevD.2.1285}. The decay process is described by the box and penguin Feynman diagrams shown in Figure \ref{fig:fey_pinn}. The decay is the transformation of a anti-strange quark into an anti-down quark and an neutrino pair, leaving the up quark unchanged.
    The branching ratio of $K_{\pi\nu\bar{\nu}}$ is dominated by the $Z^0$ penguin diagrams and after summing over the three neutrino flavours can be described by \cite{Buras2015},
    \begin{equation}\label{eq:pinn_theory_br}
        \begin{split}
            \mathcal{B}(K^+ \to \pi^+ \nu \bar{\nu}) &= \kappa_+(1+\Delta_{EM}) \Bigg[ \left( \frac{\mathfrak{Im}(\lambda_t)}{\lambda^5} X(x_t) \right)^2 \\
            &\quad + \left( \frac{\mathfrak{Re}(\lambda_c)}{\lambda} P_c(X) + \frac{\mathfrak{Re}(\lambda_t)}{\lambda^5} X(x_t) \right)^2 \Bigg]
        \end{split}    
    \end{equation}
    with 
    \begin{equation}
        \begin{split}
            \kappa_+ = (5.173 \pm 0.025) \times 10^{-11} \left(\frac{\lambda}{0.225}\right)^8, \Delta_{EM} = -0.003
        \end{split}
    \end{equation}    
    where $x_t = m_t^2 / m_W^2$ which is the ratio of the mass of the top quark and the mass of the W boson, $\lambda$ originates from the Wolfenstein parametrisation shown above and $\lambda_i = V_{is}^*V_{id}$ where $V_{ij}$ are CKM matrix elements. $\Delta_{EM}$ takes into account the correction from radiative photon exchanges and $\kappa_+$ condenses the relevant hadronic matrix elements. 
    The terms $X(x_t)$ and $P_c(X)$ are the loop functions which describe contributions from the top and charm quark. The $X(x_t)$ can be described by the equation \cite{PhysRevD.83.034030, Buras2015}, 
    \begin{equation}
        X(x_t) = X^(0) + \frac{\alpha_s}{4\pi}X_t^{(1)} + \frac{\alpha}{4\pi}X_t^{(EW)} = 1.481 \pm 0.005 \pm 0.008.
    \end{equation}
    It is possible to represent $\lambda_t$ and $\lambda_c$ from Equation \ref{eq:pinn_theory_br} as \cite{Buchalla:1995vs}, %\cite{Buras2015}, 
    \begin{equation}
        %\mathfrak{Re}(\lambda_t) \simeq |V_{ub}||V_{cb}| \cos{\gamma}(1-2\lambda^2) + (|V_{ub}|^2 - |V_{cb}|^2)\lambda \left(1 - \frac{\lambda^2}{2}\right),
        \mathfrak{Re}(\lambda_t) = -(1-\frac{\lambda}{2})A^2\lambda^5(1-\bar{\rho})
    \end{equation}
    \begin{equation}
        %\mathfrak{Im}(\lambda_t) \simeq |V_{ub}||V_{cb}|\sin{\gamma},
        \mathfrak{Im}(\lambda_t) = A^2\lambda^5\eta
    \end{equation}
    \begin{equation}
        %\mathfrak{Re}(\lambda_c) \simeq -\lambda \left(1-\frac{\lambda^2}{2}\right),
        \mathfrak{Re}(\lambda_c) = -\lambda(1-\frac{\lambda}{2})
    \end{equation}
    and can be used to parametrise the $K_{\pi\nu\bar{\nu}}$ branching fraction (\ref{eq:pinn_theory_br}) with the CKM Wolfenstein parameters as \cite{Buchalla:1998ba}
    \begin{equation}\label{eq:branch_kpinunu}
        %\mathcal{B}(K^+ \to \pi^+ \nu \bar{\nu}) = \frac{\kappa_+}{\lambda^8}|V_{cb}|^4X(x_t)^2 \left(\sigma R_t^2\sin{\beta}^2 + 1/\sigma \left(R_t\cos{\beta} + \frac{\lambda^4P_c(X)}{|V_{cb}|^2 X(x_t)} \right)\right).
        \mathcal{B}(K^+ \to \pi^+ \nu \bar{\nu}) = \kappa_+ A^4X(x_t)^2\frac{1}{\sigma}((\sigma^2\bar{\eta})^2 + (\rho_0 - \bar{\rho})^2)
    \end{equation}
    where $\sigma = (1 / (1-\frac{\lambda^2}{2}))^2$ and $\rho_0 = 1+ \frac{P_0(X)}{A^2 X(x_t)}$. Equation \ref{eq:branch_kpinunu} represents an ellipse in the unitary triangle plane ($\bar{\rho},\bar{\eta}$) with a centre of ($\rho_0, 0$), a semi-major axis equal to $\sqrt{\frac{\mathcal{B}\sigma}{\kappa_+ A^4X(x_t)^2}}$ and a semi-minor axis of $\sqrt{\frac{\mathcal{B}}{\sigma\kappa_+ A^4X(x_t)^2}}$. The ellipse constrains the parameters ($\bar{\rho},\bar{\eta}$) and is depicted in Figures \ref{fig:traingles_SM} and \ref{fig:traingles_SM_elip}.
    
    
    The $K_L \to \pi^0 \nu \bar{\nu}$ ($K_{\pi^0\nu\bar{\nu}}$) decays through similar feynman diagrams shown in Figure \ref{fig:fey_pinn} but with a down quark on top which does not change during the decay compared to an up quark for the charged mode. The $K_{\pi^0\nu\bar{\nu}}$ decay is almost entirely CP violating and is dominated by top loop contributions \cite{PhysRevD.39.3322}, resulting in the branching fraction depending on imaginary part of the top loop from Equation \ref{eq:pinn_theory_br} which is CP violating,
    \begin{equation}
        \mathcal{B}(K_L \to \pi^0 \nu \bar{\nu}) = \kappa_L \left(\frac{\mathfrak{Im}(\lambda_t)}{\lambda^5} X(x_t) \right)^2
    \end{equation}
    with
    \begin{equation}
        \kappa_L = (2.231 \pm 0.013) \times 10^{-10} \left(\frac{\lambda}{0.225}\right)^8.
    \end{equation}
    It is also possible to parametrise the equation with the Wolfenstein parameters similar to Equation \ref{eq:branch_kpinunu} as \cite{Buchalla:1998ba}, 
    \begin{equation}
        \mathcal{B}(K_L \to \pi^0 \nu \bar{\nu}) = \kappa_L \eta^2 A^4 X(x_t)^2.
    \end{equation}
    The decay can be used to further constrain the parameter $\bar{\eta}$ and is shown in Figures \ref{fig:traingles_SM} and \ref{fig:traingles_SM_elip}.
    
    
    Inputting experimental values into the equations for the branching fractions produces the SM predictions \cite{Buras2022},
    \begin{equation}
        \mathcal{B}(K^+ \to \pi^+ \nu \bar{\nu}) = (8.60 \pm 0.42) \times 10^{-11}
    \end{equation}
    and 
    \begin{equation}
        \mathcal{B}(K_L \to \pi^0 \nu \bar{\nu}) = (2.94 \pm 0.15) \times 10^{-11}.
    \end{equation}

    The first observation and measurement of the branching fraction for the $K^+ \to \pi^+ \nu \bar{\nu}$ decay has been obtained by NA62 in 2024 and is described in Section \ref{sec:na62res}. The current best experimental result for the branching fraction for the $K_L \to \pi^0 \nu \bar{\nu}$ decay is an upper limit obtained by the 
    KOTO experiment \cite{PhysRevLett.122.021802}, 
    \begin{equation}
        \mathcal{B}(K_L \to \pi^0 \nu \bar{\nu}) < 300 \times 10^{-11} (90\% \text{CL}).
    \end{equation}

    \subsection{$K^0_L \to \pi^0 l^+l^-$}\label{sec:kllep_theory}
    The decay $K^0_L \to \pi^0 l^+l^-$ is a FCNC transition of a strange quark into a down quark and a lepton-lepton pair and are theoretically clean with contributions through three processes: direct CP violating, indirect CP violating and CP conserving components.
    The SM branching fraction for the $K^0_L \to \pi^0 l^+l^-$ decay can be described as \cite{Buchalla:2003sj,Isidori2004},
    \begin{equation}
        \mathcal{B}(K^0_L \to \pi^0 l^+l^-) = \left(C^l_{mix} \pm C^l_{int} \left(\frac{\mathfrak{Im}(\lambda_t)}{10^{-4}}\right) + C^l_{dir} \left(\frac{\mathfrak{Im}(\lambda_t)}{10^{-4}}\right) + C^l_{CPC} \right) \times 10^{-12}
    \end{equation}
    where $C^l_{mix}$ represents the contributions due to $K_S - K_L$ mixing, $C^l_{int}$ the interference of the indirect and direct CP violating decays, $C^l_{dir}$ the contribution of direct CP violating and $C^l_{CPC}$ is the long distance CP conserving contribution. 
    The $|a_S|$ term depicts the decay form factor which has been calculated from results at NA48/2 from the measurement of the branching fraction of $K_S \to \pi^0 l^+l^-$ as $|a_S| = 1.2 \pm 0.2$ \cite{NA481:2003cfm, NA481:2004nbc}.
    This results in the branching fractions for the $K^0_L \to \pi^0 e^+e^-$ and $K^0_L \to \pi^0 \mu^+\mu^-$ decays, 
    \begin{equation} \label{eq:kl_piee}
        \mathcal{B}(K^0_L \to \pi^0 e^+e^-) = \left(15.7|a_S|^2 \pm 6.2 |a_S| \left(\frac{\mathfrak{Im}(\lambda_t)}{10^{-4}}\right) + 2.4 \left(\frac{\mathfrak{Im}(\lambda_t)}{10^{-4}}\right) \right) \times 10^{-12}
    \end{equation}
    and 
    \begin{equation} \label{eq:kl_pimumu}
        \mathcal{B}(K^0_L \to \pi^0 \mu^+\mu^-) = \left(3.7|a_S|^2 \pm 1.6 |a_S| \left(\frac{\mathfrak{Im}(\lambda_t)}{10^{-4}}\right) + 1.0\left(\frac{\mathfrak{Im}(\lambda_t)}{10^{-4}}\right) + 5.2 \right) \times 10^{-12}.
    \end{equation}
    Equations \ref{eq:kl_piee} and \ref{eq:kl_pimumu} show the main difference between the two decay modes, that the CP conserving contribution due to the two-photon intermediate state only occurs in the muon decay as it is negligible in the helicity suppressed electron mode.

    Utilising the above equations, set out in \cite{Mescia:2006jd} the expected SM branching fractions can be described as,
    \begin{equation}
        \mathcal{B}(K^0_L \to \pi^0 e^+e^-) = 3.54^{+0.98}_{-0.85} (1.56^{+0.62}_{-0.49}) \times 10^{-11}
    \end{equation}  
    and 
    \begin{equation}
        \mathcal{B}(K^0_L \to \pi^0 \mu^+\mu^-) = 1.41^{+0.28}_{-0.26} (0.95^{+0.22}_{-0.21}) \times 10^{-11}.
    \end{equation}
    The current best results experimentally for the branching fraction of $K^0_L \to \pi^0 l^+l^-$ decays are the upper limits obtained by the KTeV experiment \cite{PhysRevLett.93.021805, PhysRevLett.84.5279} 
    \begin{equation}
        \mathcal{B}(K^0_L \to \pi^0 e^+e^-) < 28 \times 10^{-11} (90\% \text{CL})
    \end{equation}  
    and 
    \begin{equation}
        \mathcal{B}(K^0_L \to \pi^0 \mu^+\mu^-) < 38 \times 10^{-11} (90\% \text{CL}).
    \end{equation}
    \section{Dark Photon} \label{sec:DarkP_th}
    As mentioned above the SM is not a complete theory of the universe. A large component of the universe is not yet understood such as the origin of the observed rise in cosmic-ray positron fraction with energy and the anomalous muon magnetic moment (g-2) \cite{Muong-2:2023cdq} suggest the existence of a hidden sector. 
    A possible extension of the SM is the interaction of a new $U(1)$ gauge symmetry with an associated boson, the Dark Photon (DP) which kinematically mixes with the SM hypercharge $U(1)_Y$ \cite{Fabbrichesi:2020wbt}. There are three separate benchmark cases of the DP which have been set out by the Physics Beyond Colliders group for future study \cite{Beacham_2020}: 
    \begin{itemize}
        \item BC1 - The DP is massive and decays into only SM particles.
        \item BC2 - The DP is massive decays into mostly Dark Matter.
        \item BC3 - The DP is massless and involves the existence of so called milli-charged particles.
    \end{itemize}
    In this study the BC1 case is the only scenario accounted for. The hypothetical DP (denoted $A'$) with a \si{\giga\electronvolt\per\clight} scale mass could be possible explanations for these deviations from the SM. The DP has two unknown parameters: the mass $m_{A'}$ and a kinetic mixing parameter $\epsilon$ denoting the strength of the DP interaction with SM particles. For DP of mass less than a pion 
    it is possible for it to produced in the $\pi^0$ decay $\pi^0 \to \gamma A'$ and then subsequently decay to SM particles $A' \to e^+e^-$. The resultant branching fraction for the $\pi^0$ decay is \cite{PhysRevD.80.095024},
    \begin{equation}\label{eq:DP_branch}
        \mathcal{B}(\pi^0 \to \gamma A') = 2\epsilon^2 \left(1-\frac{m_{A'}^2}{m_{\pi^0}^2}^3\right) \mathcal{B}(\pi^0 \to \gamma \gamma).
    \end{equation}
    Within the DP mass range for the decay the only allowed tree level decay is the $A' \to \e^+e^-$ decay. As the loop decays to three photons and two neutrinos are highly suppressed compared to the electron pair, it is safe to assume that the branching fraction for $A' \to \e^+e^- \approx 1 $. This assumption allows the expected decay width to be described as \cite{PhysRevD.80.095024}, 
    \begin{equation}
        \Gamma_{A'} \approx \Gamma (A' \to \e^+e^-) = \frac{1}{3} \alpha \epsilon^2 m_{A'} \sqrt{1 - \frac{4m_e^2}{m_{A'^2}}} \left( 1 + \frac{4m_e^2}{m_{A'^2}} \right)
    \end{equation}
    By utilising Equation \ref{eq:DP_branch} and constructing a study to search for the DP to measure it branching fraction, it is possible to measure the mixing parameter. Within the DP mass range $2m_e < m_{A'} < m_{\pi^0}$ the world leading result for the upper limit of $\epsilon^2$ is from the NA48/2 experiment \cite{2015178} and is shown in Figure \ref{fig:na48_dp_lim}.
    \begin{figure}[H]
        \centering
        \includegraphics[width=0.7\textwidth]{imgs/na48_limits.png}
        \linespread{1.0}
        \caption{Obtained upper limits on the DP mixing parameter from the NA48/2 \cite{2015178} and other experiments.}%
        \label{fig:na48_dp_lim}%
    \end{figure}
    The study set out in Section \ref{sec:darkP} sets out to improve the limits on the DP mixing parameter set by NA48/2 with data collected from the NA62 experiment.
