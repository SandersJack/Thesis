    %% Intro with background on NA48/2
    This chapter describes the search for the \gls{DP}, also denoted $A'$, at NA62 with the data collected in 2022--2024. It outlines the selection criteria for the analysis and provides the acceptances at NA62. The search strategy for the \gls{DP} is then presented, followed by two different methods used to estimate the background. The first method relies 
    on \gls{MC} simulations to model the background. However, due to large statistical uncertainties associated with the \gls{MC}, a second method is introduced, which uses the side bands of the observed data to model the background. Both background methods are used as inputs in the \gls{DP} method to produce two separate results for the analysis.


    The analysis aims to set limits on the mixing parameter of the \gls{DP}, utilising production through the decay of $\pi^0 \to \gamma A'$ and subsequent prompt decay into \gls{SM} particles $A' \to e^+e^-$. The theoretical background for this hypothetical \gls{DP} is described in Section \ref{sec:DarkP_th}. 
    The NA62 experiment, described in Section \ref{sec:na62}, provides a clean sample of $\pi^0$ particles through the decay \mbox{$K^+ \to \pi^+\pi^0$} with a branching ratio of 20.67\,\%. With the assumption that the \gls{DP} decays promptly, the decay signature is identical to the $\pi^0$ Dalitz decay 
    $\pi^0_D \to \gamma e^+e^-$. The Dalitz decay represents the background for the \gls{DP} search and determines the sensitivity. 

    %Using the Dalitz decay to model the signal of the \gls{DP} requires an assumption of the quantum numbers of $A'$. 
    By definition, the dark photon is the mediator of the vector portal~\cite{Beacham_2020} and therefore shares the quantum numbers of the \gls{SM} photon, $J^{PC} = 1^{--}$. As a result the Dalitz decay is used to model the signal.
    The spin of the $A'$ directly affects the kinematics and angular distribution of the final-state particles.

    \section{\boldmath $\pi^0_D$ \unboldmath Event Selection}
    %The analysis started using the same procedure used in the NA48/2 \cite{2015178} %ADD na48 context
    The selection for the \gls{DP} search originates from the search at NA48/2 \cite{2015178}. The \gls{kpid} decay has the same event signature as the production and prompt decay of the \gls{DP}. The event selection comprises the identification of the $\pi^+$, $e^-$, $e^+$ and $\gamma$.
    \subsection{Vertex Selection}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item It is required that at least one of the following trigger conditions is satisfied: the Control Trigger (also referred to as the Minimum Bias Trigger), the Multi-Track (MT) trigger, or the electron Multi-Track (eMT) trigger, described in Chapter~\ref{sec:na62}.
        \item A single three-track vertex is required. The vertex must have a total momentum below \SI{78}{\giga\electronvolt\per\clight}, a total positive charge of $Q = 1$, a vertex fit $\chi^2 < 40$, and a position within the range $\SI{105}{\meter} < Z_{\text{vtx}} < \SI{180}{\meter}$.
        \item The tracks within the vertex are checked if they are in acceptance of all four STRAW spectrometer chambers and the CHOD. The track momentum must be $\SI{5}{\giga\electronvolt\per\clight} < p < \SI{60}{\giga\electronvolt\per\clight}$ with $\chi^2 < 30$. The tracks must traverse the first STRAW chamber with separations of 20\,mm from each other.
        \item A selected photon is required. The missing vertex momentum of the vertex is used to extrapolate a straight line from the vertex position to the Z-plane of the LKr calorimeter. The missing momentum is the difference between the kaon 3-momentum and the total vertex 3-momentum. Within a radius of 15\,cm of the extrapolated position, a single \gls{LKr} energy deposit
        with a minimum energy of \SI{5}{\giga\electronvolt} is required and is considered as the photon candidate. The photon energy must be within \SI{5}{\giga\electronvolt} of the missing vertex momentum.
    \end{itemize}

    \subsection{Kinematic PID}    
    The three-track vertex is required to consist of a positively charged pion ($\pi^+$), an electron and a positron ($e^{\pm}$). The pion is identified among the two positively charged tracks using the missing mass between the kaon 4-momentum and the track 4-momentum, 
    \begin{equation}
        M_{miss}^2 = (P_{K^+} - P_{\pi^+})^2.
    \end{equation}
    If the missing mass is consistent with the $\pi^0$ mass hypothesis, the track is identified as a charged pion. 
    The cut on $M_{miss}$ is calculated using a \gls{kpid} \gls{MC} sample and is shown in Figure~\ref{fig:kaonpion}.
    The width of the distribution is found to be
    \begin{equation}
        %\sigma = 3.984 \pm 0.007_{Stat}\, \SI{}{\mega\electronvolt}.
        \sigma = (10.30 \pm 1.43)\,\SI{}{\mega\electronvolt\per\clight\squared},
    \end{equation} 
    which results in the cut $| M_{miss} - M_{\pi^0} | < 5\,\sigma = \SI{51.5}{\mega\electronvolt\per\clight\squared}$.

    The elliptical cut is defined as
    \begin{equation}\label{eq:elipse}
        \frac{((x - x_c)\cos{\theta} + (y-y_c)\sin{\theta})^2}{a^2} + \frac{((y - y_c)\cos{\theta} - (x-x_c)\sin{\theta})^2}{b^2} \leq 1
    \end{equation}
    where $x_c$ and $y_c$ are the coordinates of the ellipse centre. The $\theta$ is the angle of rotation of the ellipse, and $a$ and $b$ are the lengths of the semi-major and the semi-minor axis, respectively. 
    For the candidate event $x = M_{\pi e e \gamma}$ and $y = M_{ee \gamma}$.
    The parameters are selected to produce a selection efficiency of 95\,\%. The parameters for the ellipse are defined below: 
    \begin{itemize}
        \item $x_c = \SI{493.677}{\mega\electronvolt\per\clight\squared}$: the x-coordinate of the ellipse centre (nominal $K^+$ mass).
        \item $y_c = \SI{134.977}{\mega\electronvolt\per\clight\squared}$: the y-coordinate of the ellipse centre (nominal $\pi^0$ mass).
        \item $a = \SI{40}{\mega\electronvolt\per\clight\squared}$: the semi-major axis of the ellipse.
        \item $b = \SI{6}{\mega\electronvolt\per\clight\squared}$: the semi-minor axis of the ellipse.
        \item $\theta = \SI{0.4887}{\radian}$: the angle of rotation of the ellipse.
    \end{itemize}

    \section{\gls{DP} Search Method}\label{sec:dp_method}
    %%%% Include DP search method and DP acceptance
    This section describes the \gls{DP} search method used by both the \gls{MC} and data-driven background estimation methods.
    To conduct the \gls{DP} search, the $M_{ee}$ values must be converted to ${A'}$ mass hypotheses. The mass resolution is considered when selecting the search window centres and the size of the windows. Each centre corresponds to a specific $A'$ mass hypothesis with a window of a narrow mass range around that centre where a signal would be expected to appear.
    The mass resolution is calculated using \gls{kpid} \gls{MC}, where the difference between the reconstructed and true $M_{ee}$ values is calculated as shown in Figure~\ref{fig:true_reco}.
    \begin{figure}[t]
        \centering 
        \includegraphics[width=0.7\textwidth]{plots/true_reco.png}
        \linespread{1.0}
        \caption{Reconstructed invariant mass minus true invariant mass $M_{ee}$ vs true invariant mass.}
        \label{fig:true_reco}
    \end{figure}
    A Gaussian fit is conducted for the $M_{ee}^{Reco} - M_{ee}^{True}$ distribution in each $M_{ee}^{True}$ bin. The width of the Gaussian function at each $M_{ee}$ value is used to produce Figure~\ref{fig:mass_res}. A linear function is fitted, resulting in a parametrisation for the mass resolution:
    \begin{equation}\label{eq:mass_res}
        \sigma_m(M_{ee}) = 3.76 \times 10^{-2}\, \SI{}{\mega\electronvolt\per\clight\squared} + 5.54 \times 10^{-3} \times M_{ee}.  
    \end{equation}
    Due to the increased spectrometer length and increased B-field, there is a significant improvement in the NA62 resolution compared to NA48/2. The improvement in mass resolution allows for a narrower search mass window compared to the previous study at NA48/2.
    \begin{figure}[t]
        \centering 
        \includegraphics[width=0.9\textwidth]{plots/mass_res.png}
        \linespread{1.0}
        \caption{Resolution of the reconstructed $M_{ee}$ against $M_{ee}^{True}$.}
        \label{fig:mass_res}
    \end{figure}
    The window centres are separated by $1\sigma_m(M_{A'})$, with a window width of $\pm 1.5 \sigma_m(M_{A'})$ rounded to the nearest 0.01\,\si{\mega\electronvolt\per\clight\squared}.

    The \gls{DP} selection acceptance is calculated using the \gls{kpid} \gls{MC} sample in $M_{ee}$ mass bins of \SI{1}{\mega\electronvolt\per\clight\squared}. Each event is checked if it fulfils 
    \begin{equation}
        |M^{Reco}_{ee} - M^{True}_{ee}| < 1.5 \sigma_m(M^{True}_{ee})
    \end{equation}
    and that it passes the \gls{kpid} selection. The acceptance is shown in Figure~\ref{fig:accept_dp} as a function of $M_{A'}$. 
    The acceptance is parametrised using a polynomial for later use in the analysis.% The total acceptance for the \gls{DP} selection is $0.45\,\%$.
    \begin{figure}[t]
        \centering 
        \includegraphics[width=0.9\textwidth]{plots/accept_dp.png}
        \linespread{1.0}
        \caption{Acceptance for \gls{DP} selection for NA62 across the $M_{A'}$ spectrum.}
        \label{fig:accept_dp}
    \end{figure}

    For each \gls{DP} mass hypothesis, the number of data events ($N_{obs}$) is compared to the number of expected background events ($N_{exp}$). The error on the number of observed events $\delta N_{obs}$, used for the significance calculation is defined as 
    \mbox{$\delta N_{obs} = \sqrt{N_{obs}}$}. The error on the number of expected events ($\delta N_{exp}$) is dependent on the background estimation method. 
    For each \gls{DP} mass hypothesis, the local significance of the \gls{DP} signal is calculated as 
    \begin{equation}\label{eq:localSig}
        Z = \frac{N_{obs} - N_{exp}}{\sqrt{\delta N_{obs}^2 + \delta N_{exp}^2}}.
    \end{equation} 
    
    If the local significance is not greater than $ 3 \sigma$, no \gls{DP} signal is observed.
    However, as there are a large number of \gls{DP} mass hypotheses, it is necessary to account for the \gls{LEE}. The \gls{LEE} is essential, as when searching across many mass hypotheses statistical fluctuations can produce locally significant results. 
    To correct for this, toy \gls{MC} simulations are generated under the background only hypothesis. For each toy dataset, the maximum local significance across all the tested mass hypotheses is calculated. This yields a distribution of expected fluctuations in the absence of any signal. 
    The observed maximum local significance, calculated using Equation \ref{eq:localSig}, is compared against the distribution of fluctuations and a global p-value is computed. The p-value quantifies the probability of observing the maximum local significance from the analysis.
    This p-value is then converted into a global significance by evaluating the inverse of the standard normal distribution. If the global significance does not exceed $3\,\sigma$, the maximum deviation is not statistically significant and no \gls{DP} signal is observed.
    
    The \gls{UL} for the number of \gls{DP} decays ($A' \to e^+e^-$) at a 90\% \gls{CL} for each \gls{DP} mass value is calculated with $N_{obs}$, $N_{exp}$ and $\delta N_{exp}$ using the CLs method. This method is recommended by the Particle Data Group \cite{Cowan:2018} and employs the process set out in \cite{Junk:1999kv, Read:2000ru}.
    For setting an \gls{UL} of the signal $s$ from a counting experiment where $N$ events are observed ($N_{obs}$), and there is a known expected background $b$, it is a hypothesis test between the two cases: signal+background and just background. According to the Neyman-Pearson lemma \cite{NeymanPearson1933}, the most powerful test statistic for the hypothesis is the likelihood ratio, 
    \begin{equation}\label{eq:test_stat}
        X = L_{s+b} / L_b = \frac{e^{-(s+b)}(s+b)^N}{N!} / \frac{e^{-b} b^N}{N!} = e^{-s}\left(1+\frac{s}{b}\right)^N.
    \end{equation} 
    It is beneficial to denote the test statistic for the observed data as $X_{obs}$.
    A confidence level for the exclusion of the background hypothesis ($\text{CL}_{s+b}$) is the probability that the test statistic defined in Equation~\ref{eq:test_stat} is less than or equal to the observed test statistic, 
    \begin{equation}\label{eq:prob_test}
        CL_{s+b} = P_{s+b}(X \leq X_{obs}).
    \end{equation}
    To improve the computational efficiency, the test statistic defined in Equation~\ref{eq:test_stat} used for comparison is simplified to, 
    \begin{equation}
        log(X) = N log\left(\frac{s+b}{b}\right).
    \end{equation}
    The \gls{CL} is calculated using a \gls{MC} integration method where a large number of trials are used to calculate the probability defined in Equation~\ref{eq:prob_test}. For each trial, the value N is sampled from a Poisson distribution with a mean of $s+b$. Where $b$ is the background sampled from a normal distribution with a mean equal to the expected number of events ($N_{exp}$) and width equal to the error on the expected value ($\delta N_{exp}$).
    The test statistic is computed and compared with $X_{obs}$. The confidence level for the exclusion of the $s+b$ hypothesis ($\text{CL}_{b}$) is calculated, with N sampled from a Poisson distribution with a mean of $b$ instead of $s+b$. 
    After the trials the modified frequentist confidence level ($\text{CL}_s$) is calculated as,
    \begin{equation}
        CL_s = CL_{s+b} / CL_b.
    \end{equation}
    A binary search is then completed using the above method to find the value of $s$, which satisfies the relation
    \begin{equation}
        CL_s(s) = 1 - \alpha,
    \end{equation}
    which would be the \gls{UL} of the signal for a confidence level of $\alpha$.

    \subsection{The \boldmath $\pi^0$ \unboldmath Transition Form Factor}
    The simulation of the $\pi^0_D$ decay uses the next-to-leading order (NLO) differential decay width \cite{Lurkin:2280295} 
    \begin{equation}
        \frac{d^2\Gamma^}{dxdy} = \frac{d^2\Gamma^{LO}}{dxdy} (1 + \delta(x,y)),
    \end{equation}
    with $\delta(x,y)$ being a function encoded from the NLO corrections \cite{PhysRevD.3.1122, PhysRevD.5.1763, PhysRevD.92.054027}
    \begin{equation}
        \delta = \frac{d^2\Gamma^{NLO}}{dxdy} / \frac{d^2\Gamma^{LO}}{dxdy} = \delta^{virt} + \delta^{1 \gamma IR} + \delta^{BR},
    \end{equation}
    where $\delta^{virt}$ is from virtual radiative corrections, $\delta^{1\gamma IR}$ one-photon irreducible contribution and $\delta^{BR}$ is the bremsstrahlung contribution.
    The leading-order differential decay width is defined as \cite{PhysRevD.5.1763}
    \begin{equation}\label{eq:partDecay}
        \frac{d^2\Gamma^{LO}}{dxdy} = \Gamma_0 \frac{\alpha}{\pi}|F(x)|^2\frac{(1-x)^3}{4x} \left(1+y^2+\frac{r^2}{x}\right),
    \end{equation}
    where $\Gamma_0$ is the $\pi^0 \to \gamma \gamma$ decay width, $\alpha$ is the fine-structure constant, $F(x)$ is the pion \gls{TFF}, $x$ and $y$ are dimensionless kinematic variables,
    \begin{equation}
        x = \frac{(Q_{e^-} + Q_{e^+})^2}{M_{\pi^0}^2} \quad \text{and} \quad y = \frac{2Q_{\pi^0}(Q_{e^-} - Q_{e^+})}{M_{\pi^0}^2 (1-x)}.
    \end{equation}
    The variables $Q_{e^-}$, $Q_{e^+}$ and $Q_{\pi^0}$ are the four-momenta of the electron, positron and pion. Within the kinematic region of \gls{kpid} decay, the \gls{TFF} is approximated as $F(x) = 1 + ax$. The parameter $a$ is the slope factor, which has been set through theoretical predictions and a number of experiments. 
    The Vector Meson Dominance (VMD) model expects that \mbox{$a = M_{\pi^0}^2 (M_{\rho}^{-2} + M_{\omega}^{-2}) / 2 = 0.03$ \cite{PhysRevD.83.037503}}.
    The \gls{PDG} average for all experiments is \mbox{$a = 0.0335 \pm 0.0031$} \cite{Navas:2024}.
    In the NA62 framework, the slope is defined as \mbox{$a_{Fw} =  0.032$} by default. 
    It is not possible to rely on external values for the slope factor as this would assume that the \gls{MC} perfectly models effects such as detector responses, resolutions and kinematic distributions. 
    Relying on external slope factor can result in discrepancies between data and \gls{MC}. To mitigate this and ensure an accurate background model an effective slope is defined using the data itself.
    %due to unknown biases within the simulation.  
    
    Figure~\ref{fig:MCDATAFF} shows the ratio of data and \gls{MC} from Figure~\ref{fig:mee_mc} in bins of $x$. %The ratio shows a slight discrepancy between the expected background and data. The dependence along $x$ suggests that the TFF found in the framework is incompatible with the background estimate for this data set.
    A correction is applied to modify the \gls{MC} sample with an effective \gls{TFF} slope. %The \gls{MC} is re-weighted using the events passing the selection compared against the expected number of events from \gls{MC} across the reconstructed mass range.

    \subsection{Fitting the Data Distribution}
    The method described in Section~\ref{sec:dp_method} is used to convert bins of $M_{ee}$ to \gls{DP} candidate windows.
    The \gls{DP} mass range is defined as $6 \leq M_{A'} \leq 121.6$\,\si{\mega\electronvolt\per\clight\squared}. The mass range is extended compared to the previous \gls{MC} study. This is due to the large number of events in the mass bins at the edges of the distribution provided by using the data sample rather than the MC. 
    %Each \gls{DP} mass hypothesis is defined by steps of $1\sigma_m(M_{A'})$
    Using the method defined in Section~\ref{sec:dp_method} results in 428 \gls{DP} mass hypotheses tested. 
    %This deviated from the \gls{MC} study where the window's width is the size of the mass centre step. 
    %This is  compared to the \gls{MC} study. %How many?
    A 4th-order polynomial is fitted to the data distribution shown in Figure~\ref{fig:22_23_data}, using a least $\chi^2$ method. The fitting region for each mass hypothesis is defined as $\pm 15\sigma_m(M_{A'})$ from the mass hypothesis, and the distribution is re-binned with a bin width of $\sigma_m(M_{A'}) / 5$. 
    Data events in the mass bins above \SI{125}{\mega\electronvolt\per\clight\squared} are removed. This is because the small counts at the edge of the mass range cause instability with the fitting.
    Events in the mass bins $\pm 1.5 \sigma_m(M_{A'})$ from the mass hypothesis are excluded from the fit. This reduces the chance of a biased background estimate from potential signal contamination while preserving sensitivity across the scanned mass range.
    
    The number of expected events $N_{exp}$ is evaluated as the integral of the fitted function within the $\pm 1.5 \sigma_m(M_{A'})$ mass window divided by the bin width. The error on the number of expected events $\delta N_{exp}$ combines statistical and systematic uncertainties. The statistical component $\delta N_{exp}^{stat}$ is computed from the propagation of the 
    statistical errors of the polynomial parameters, provided in the form of a covariance matrix and calculated using an error propagation method available in the ROOT package (TF1::IntegralError()) \cite{Brun1997} . 
    The systematic error $\delta N_{exp}^{sys}$ is estimated by fitting each distribution defined above with 3rd and 5th order polynomials. The resultant integrals are evaluated and compared to the integral calculated using the 4th order polynomial. The systematic error is calculated as the largest of the two differences from the 4th order polynomial.

    \section{Validation of the Prompt Decay Assumption}\label{sec:PromptLim}
    Key to this analysis is the assumption of the prompt $A' \to e^+e^-$ decay, where \mathcal{B}($A' \to e^+e^-$) = 1. A check on the \gls{DP} maximum mean path length is required to ensure that the above assumption holds and the calculated limits above are correct. 
    %Using the maximum energy of the \gls{DP}, the maximum mean path of the \gls{DP} is calculated. From a nominal beam energy at NA62 of \SI{75}{\giga\electronvolt\per\clight}
    The energy of the $\pi^0$ from the $K_{2\pi}$ decay in the kaon rest frame is
    \begin{equation} \label{eq:2body}
        E_{\pi^0}^{rest} = \frac{M_K^2 + M_{\pi^0}^2 - M_{\pi^+}^2}{2M_K} = \SI{0.246}{\giga\electronvolt},
    \end{equation}
    where $M_K$, $M_{\pi^0}$ and $M_{\pi^+}$ are the kaon, neutral pion and charged pion masses, with the equation derived from basic two-body decay kinematics. The $\pi^0$ momentum in the kaon rest frame is computed as
    \begin{equation} \label{eq:pi0mom}
        p_{\pi^0}^{rest} = \sqrt{(E_{\pi^0}^{rest})^2 - M_{\pi^0}^2} = \SI{0.205}{\giga\electronvolt\per\clight}.
    \end{equation}
    With a nominal beam energy at NA62 of $E_{K} = \SI{75}{\giga\electronvolt\per\clight}$, using Equations \ref{eq:2body} and \ref{eq:pi0mom} the maximum energy of the $\pi^0$ in the laboratory reference frame via a Lorentz boost is,
    \begin{equation}
        E_{\pi^0} = \gamma_K (E_{\pi^0}^{rest} + \beta_K p_{\pi^0}^{rest}) = \SI{68.5}{\giga\electronvolt},
    \end{equation}
    with $\gamma_K = E_{K} / M_K$ and $\beta_K^2 = 1 - 1/\gamma_K^2$. In the decay $\pi^0 \to \gamma A'$ the maximum energy of the \gls{DP} occurs when the \gls{DP} is emitted in the direction of the $\pi^0$ motion and the photon is emitted backwards in the $\pi^0$ rest frame, resulting in a maximum \gls{DP} energy
    \begin{equation}
        E^{max}_{A'} = \gamma_{\pi^0} (E_{A'}^{rest} + \beta_{\pi^0} p_{A'}^{rest}) = \SI{68.5}{\giga\electronvolt},
    \end{equation} 
    where $\gamma_{\pi^0} = E_{\pi^0} / M_{\pi^0}$ and $\beta_{\pi^0} = p_{\pi^0} / E_{\pi^0}$. The maximum \gls{DP} energy and momentum in the $\pi^0$ rest frame is when the \gls{DP} mass tends to zero, resulting in \mbox{$E_{A'}^{rest} \approx M_{\pi^0} / 2$} and \mbox{$p_{A'}^{rest} \approx E_{A'}^{rest}$}.
    The maximum \gls{DP} mean free path is
    \begin{equation}
        \lambda^{max}_{A'} \approx \frac{E^{max}_{A'}}{M_{A'} c} \tau_{A'} = \SI{0.55}{\milli\metre} \times \frac{10^{-6}}{\epsilon^2} \times \left(\frac{\SI{100}{\mega\electronvolt\per\clight\squared}}{M_{A'}}\right)^2,
    \end{equation}
    where \gls{DP} mean free path is inversely dependent on $\epsilon^2 M_{A'}^2$, and $\tau_{A'}$ is defined by Equation~\ref{eq:DP_lifetime}. The lowest \gls{UL} of $\epsilon^2$ from the analysis is found as 
    $\epsilon^2 M_{A'}^2 = 4.59 \times 10^{-6}$\,\si{\mega\electronvolt^2\per\clight^4}  %$\epsilon^2 m_{A'}^2 = 6.80 \times 10^{-6} MeV^2 /c^4$ 
    corresponding to $\lambda^{max}_{A'} = \SI{1.19}{\meter}$. %$0.81\m$.
    Due to the design of the three-track trigger and offline reconstruction, the resolution of a decay vertex position is about 1\,m. As the distance between the production vertex and the $A'$ decay point increases, the probability of reconstructing a single three-track vertex decreases. This results in the event being removed due to the requirement of a single three-track vertex during the selection, reducing the acceptance and reducing the sensitivity of the analysis.
    For a \gls{DP} with the maximum energy, a dashed line corresponding to a decay length of $\lambda = \SI{1}{\meter}$ is shown in Figure~\ref{fig:prompt}. The fluctuations of the \gls{UL} of the mixing parameter are just below the line for the maximum energy, suggesting that the prompt assumption is still valid except in the most extreme cases.

    \section{Conclusions}
    The search for \gls{DP} at NA62 is expanded from the study conducted at NA48/2, utilising the 2022--2024 dataset. Two methods for the background estimation are presented: one using \gls{MC} samples and the other using the data.
    The \gls{MC} background estimation method produces uncompetitive results due to the limited size of \gls{MC} samples produced and potential systematic errors in the background estimate. The data-driven background estimation produces improved limits compared to previously published results for the mixing parameter across the 
    \gls{DP} mass search range. The result sets limits in a region of phase space not yet covered by any other experiment.
