    Differential Cherenkov counters with achromatic ring focus (CEDAR) were designed and built at CERN to detect secondary beam particles at the Super Proton Synchrotron (SPS) \cite{Bovet:1982xf}. Two types of CEDAR were developed at CERN:
    CEDAR-West (CEDAR-W) and CEDAR-North (CEDAR-N), corresponding to the areas of use. The two designs differ in terms of the energy range in which they can detect beam particles, with CEDAR-N 
    able to separate kaons and pions up to \SI{300}{\giga\electronvolt\per\clight} but not detect protons below \SI{60}{{\giga\electronvolt\per\clight}}. Whereas CEDAR-W can detect protons down to $12 GeV / c$ 
    and separate kaons and pions up to \SI{150}{{\giga\electronvolt\per\clight}}. 
    %% Describe the previous performance of CEDAR-W with Nitrogen
    \newline\indent As described in Section \ref{sec:na62}, since 2016 NA62 has utilised a CEDAR-W filled with Nitrogen (\NN) at a nominal gas pressure of 1.71\,bar (absolute pressure).
    In this configuration, the CEDAR contributed to the most significant amount of material in the path of the beam, corresponding to $39 \times 10^{-3}\,X_0$ of material, 
    where $X_0$ is one radiation length. The CEDAR vessel contributed $3.9 \times 10^{-3}\,X_0$ and the \NN gas contributed $35 \times 10^{-3}\,X_0$.
    From simulations, the material from the \CW\ with \NN\ resulted in the multiple scattering of beam particles, introducing an angular divergence in the beam of $32\,\mu rad$. Scattered beam particles 
    contribute to the backgrounds for the searches for ultra-rare decays. In order to reduce these backgrounds, it was possible to minimise the amount of material in the path of the beam. 
    It was proposed that a CEDAR filled with Hydrogen (\HH) could reduce the material due to the radiator gas's lower mass. Using \HH\ as a radiator gas would require a pressure of 3.8\,bar 
    to produce a similar Cherenkov angle as \NN gas at \SI{1.71}{\bar}. Hydrogen at this pressure would introduce $3.4 \times 10^{-3}\,X_0$ of material, which would result in a factor of five reduction in material compared to \NN, resulting in a total 
    material of $7.3 \times 10^{-3}\,X_0$. The expected angular beam divergence is $13\,\mu rad$ in both the horizontal and vertical dimensions. 

    \newline\indent Taking into account that the four GTK stations contribute $20 \times 10^{-3}\,X_0$, the total beam material along the beam-line reduces from $59 \times 10^{-3}\,X_0$ 
    to $27 \times 10^{-3}\,X_0$. Using the full NA62 simulation based on GEANT4 \cite{Agostinelli2003Geant4}, it was possible to estimate that the number of particles interacting inelastically and elastically upstream 
    of the decay volume would reduce from $2.1\,\%$ to $0.9\,\%$. The reduction in scattering would result in higher kaon transmission (number of kaons reaching the
    decay volume) and reduced signal rates in the downstream detectors. %%% May be we can add things about trigger rates but I think we should do a quick study

    \section{CEDAR Detector}\label{sec:cedar_desc}

    A CEDAR comprises a thermally insulated gas vessel separated into two sections: a 4.3\,m long cylinder with an inner diameter of 53\,cm and a vessel cap 
    which can differ in length, depending on the CEDAR variant. The CEDAR-W has a vessel cap length of 34\,cm compared to 28\,cm for the CEDAR-N variant. Both attached to the main cylinder 
    at the upstream end. The gas vessel is separated from the vacuum of the beam-pipe by two aluminum windows at the upstream and downstream end. A diagram of the CEDAR vessel, along with the optics enclosed, is depicted in Figure \ref{fig:CEDAR_DIA} 
    
    The beam is designed to pass through the CEDAR vessel along the longitudinal axis through the radiator gas and aluminium windows. When the beam particles pass through the vessel Cherenkov at a velocity $v$ between,
    \begin{equation}
        \frac{c}{n} < v < c
    \end{equation}
    where $c$ is the speed of light in a vacuum, and $n$ is the refractive index of the radiator gas, Cherenkov light is produced.

    %light is produced with an angle dependent on the mass and momentum of the beam particle and is defined as equation \ref{eq:Cherenkov2}.
    %\begin{equation}\label{eq:Cherenkov2}
    %cos(\theta) = \frac{1}{\beta n}
    %\end{equation}
    The CEDAR contains optics inside the vessel to allow the reflection and focusing of the produced Cherenkov light to reach the diaphragm at a radius of 100\,mm, as displayed in Figure \ref{fig:CEDAR_DIA} as the orange lines. 
    The light produced is reflected back upstream by a Mangin mirror \cite{Riedl:74}. A mirror comprising two spherical surfaces with the downstream lens cemented onto a concave mirror. The 
    mirror is designed to reduce spherical aberrations, an effect intrinsic to lenses and curved mirrors where the light that strikes the spherical surface off-centre is refracted/reflected
    to different points compared to the light that would strike the centre of the mirror. After the mirror, the light goes through a plano-convex lens named the Chromatic Corrector, which is designed in such a way that it corrects for the chromatic dispersion of the radiator gas
    and directs the light towards the diaphragm.
    Chromatic dispersion happens because different wavelengths of light refract differently in a medium, causing the Cherenkov ring to disperse, which would result in some of the light being lost when it reaches the small diaphragm opening. Both the Mangin mirror and the 
    Chromatic corrector have a hole in the centre to allow for the beam to pass through without impacting the optics. 
    
    \newline\indent The diagram is shown in Figure \ref{fig:cedarpics}, as a disk with eight separate apertures. Each aperture has an inner and outer element which allows the aperture to open to  
    20\,mm and close to 0\,mm. These elements can be seen in Figure \ref{fig:cedarpics} (right), where the aperture was set at a small value, used only to select a single Cherenkov ring of radius 100\,mm.  
    The mechanism is operated by a stepper motor connected to the shaft shown in Figure \ref{fig:cedarpics} (left). Turning the shaft clockwise opens the diaphragm, and counter-clockwise closes it. The 
    shaft is connected to a potentiometer that measures the rotation, which is used to convert to an aperture in millimetres. A pair of end switches are in place at the extreme ends of the rotation to alert the user if the aperture 
    is fully open or fully closed, which is essential not to cause any damage to the mechanical system.

    In the original CEDAR design \cite{Bovet:1982xf}, light exiting the CEDAR through the exit windows was detected by eight ET-9820QB photomultiplier tubes (PMTs), with one attached to each window. 
    A particle was identified when there was a coincidence in at least six PMTs.
    However, this setup would not be applicable for NA62, which requires strict timing requirements on the selection of kaons of less than 100\,ps, a kaon identification efficiency greater than 95\,\% 
    and a pion miss-identification probability of less than $10^{-4}$. To achieve these strict requirements, a purpose-built photon detection system was designed \cite{GOUDZOVSKI201586} and attached to the 
    upstream end of the CEDAR. The light exiting the quartz windows traversed through optical caps attached to the exit windows, which focused the light onto spherical mirrors instead of directly after the window. The eight spherical mirrors, one for each exit 
    window, reflect the light 90 degrees onto eight PMT arrays and broaden the light to increase the resultant light spot. The spherical mirrors were manufactured from off-the-shelf optical lenses, then coated in aluminium and are shown in Figure \ref{fig:spherical-mirror}. Each light spot is defined as a sector 
    which will be used from now on. Each sector contains a light guide before a PMT array equipped with 48 Hamamatsu PMTs (32 of type R9880U-110 and 16 of type R7400U-03). 
    The light guide consists of a matrix of cones coated by aluminised mylar to maximise their reflectivity and direct the light onto the active area of each PMT.

    % Explain how particles are detected
    The Cherenkov light from the beam particles is produced with an angle dependent on the mass and momentum of the beam particle,
    \begin{equation}\label{eq:Cherenkov2}
    cos(\theta) = \frac{1}{\beta n}
    \end{equation}
    with $n$ being the refractive index of the radiator gas and $\beta$ the beam particle velocity defined as $\beta = p / E$. The momentum ($p$) is constant for all beam particles in NA62, and for a constant refractive index, different particles produce slightly different Cherenkov angles due to their differing mass. The CEDAR is designed to allow the light emitted from a specific angle to pass through a 
    diaphragm aperture by selecting the pressure of the radiator gas. When the pressure of the gas is raised, the refractive index increases, which results in an increase in the Cherenkov angle and the Cherenkov ring radius. This allows a pressure to be
    selected where a kaon at \SI{75}\giga\electronvolt\per\clight produces a Cherenkov ring radius of 100\,mm at the diaphragm, matching the centre of the CEDAR aperture. Selecting a aperture of less than 2.5\,mm allowed only the light from the kaon to pass through the diaphragm, with the 
    central radius of the pion ring being 2\,mm larger and the central radius of the proton ring being 6\,mm smaller, shown in Figure \ref{fig:WestNitrogen}. The selection of a single particle Cherenkov ring depends on the beam divergence being less than $100\,\mu m$ and the axis of the CEDAR vessel being aligned parallel
    to the beam. The CEDAR alignment to the beam is performed using a pair of motors at the downstream end of the detector, which displaces the downstream end by $\pm 5\,mm$ in both the XY dimension, pivoting the CEDAR vessel around the fixed upstream end. 
    At a temperature of 293\,K, the nominal gas pressure to select kaons at \SI{75}{\giga\electronvolt\per\clight} is defined at \SI{1.71}{\bar} for \NN\ in CEDAR-W and \SI{3.85}{\bar} for \HH\ in CEDAR-H, with the values fluctuating by 0.34\,\% for every 1\,K of temperature difference.
    The CEDAR vessel is sealed and insulated in order to operate as a closed volume with a fixed amount of gas. It is also expected to be insensitive to fluctuations in the environmental temperature due to the extensive insulation around the pressure vessel.

    %Due to the different chromatic dispersion effects in Hydrogen, it was not possible to use the CEDAR-W using \HH. From simulations (Figure \ref{fig:WestHyrogen}), it was apparent the light was distorted 
    %when compared to the CEDAR-W with \NN\ shown in Figure \ref{fig:WestNitrogen}. 
    Simulations of the \CW filled with \HH radiator gas show that the correction of the chromatic dispersion is inefficient, and photons of different wavelengths still display different radii at the diaphragm after the correction.
    As in Figure \ref{fig:WestHyrogen}, in this configuration, it is not feasible to only select the light from kaons using the CEDAR diaphragm aperture without sacrificing light yield and operating at a lower detection efficiency. 
    It was decided that a new vessel had to be developed with updated optics optimised for use with \HH. The vessel was called CEDAR-H.
    
    An available CEDAR-N vessel was chosen to be used as the CEDAR-H structure, which reduced the costs when most of the mechanical components, such as the diaphragm and mirror supports, were not envisioned to change due to their complexity. This 
    reduced the degrees of freedom for optimisation to two: the radius of curvature of the chromatic corrector lens and the two surfaces of the Mangin mirror. Initial parameters of the optical components were found using an analytical ray-tracing 
    procedure that considered the point of origin of the Cherenkov light and the wavelength spectrum \cite{Bache:2845388, Henshaw:2915320}. The parameters were iterated to achieve a ring radius of 100\,mm at the diaphragm and to minimise the width of the ring at the diaphragm. 
    Due to the pressure-dependent effects of the chromatic dispersion and spherical aberration, each computation was completed at pressures between 3.8 to 4.1\,bar in steps of 0.1\,bar.

    Once the initial parameters had been found, a full GEANT4 \cite{Agostinelli2003Geant4} simulation was employed to validate and optimise the results from the analytical computation. The GEANT4 simulation allowed to further trace the light coming out of the CEDAR vessel through the diaphragm aperture 
    , and being reflected by the spherical mirrors onto the PMT arrays, as shown by the light path in Figure \ref{fig:CEDAR_DIA}. The simulation was then used to modify the optical parameters 
    to maximise the number of photons reaching the KTAG PMTs. The radius of the beam hole of the Mangin mirror was reduced with respect to the original CEDAR design, resulting in a greater reflective surface and increasing number of 
    photons reaching the PMTs. The spherical mirrors outside the vessel were updated to improve the light distribution on the PMT arrays. It was found that using the condenser lenses from the CEDAR-W as opposed to the CEDAR-N resulted in more significant photon yield. 

    The optimisation studies produced results with negligible differences between 3.8 and 4.1\,bar as the increase of Cherenkov photons at high pressure was counteracted by the improved light distribution on the PMT array at low pressures.
    Due to \HH\ safety considerations, the lower pressure of 3.8\,bar was selected. The optics sufficiently correct the chromatic dispersion at this pressure setting, producing a Gaussian width of 0.4\,mm for the kaon and pion rings at the diaphragm (see left Figure \ref{fig:cedarHsim}). This configuration allowed for the selection of 
    kaons with a 2\,mm CEDAR diaphragm, shown in Figure \ref{fig:cedarHsim} with minimal light from the pion passing through.

    the requirements set for operation at NA62.

    \section{CEDAR-H Test-beam}
    \begin{figure}[H]
        \centering 
        \includegraphics[width=0.7\textwidth]{imgs/test_beamInstall.png}
        \linespread{1.0}
        \caption{Image of CEDAR-H being installed on the H6 beam-line at CERN in 2022.}
        \label{fig:CEDARHTest}

    After construction of the CEDAR-H, the detector was tested and characterised in 2022 during a test at the H6 beam-line at CERN, shown in Figure \ref{fig:CEDARHTest}.
    The test beam had three main aims: to familiarise with the safety procedures related to \HH gas operations, to ensure that the optics had been installed and aligned correctly, and to measure the kaon identification efficiency and pion miss-identification probability for several working points.
    The H6 beam-line was located at the CERN north area and was derived from \SI{400}{\giga\electronvolt\per\clight} primary protons from the SPS interacting with a target consisting of a beryllium rod, producing an unseparated \SI{75}{\giga\electronvolt\per\clight} hadron beam similar to the NA62 beam. 
    CEDAR-H was installed 440\,m downstream of the target, compared to 70\,m when installed in NA62, resulting in a different beam composition when compared to NA62, with 4\,\% kaons, 25\,\% protons, and 71\,\% pions. 
    The beam divergence at the entrance of the CEDAR was $80\,\mu m$ in the H6 beam-line compared to \SI{70}{\micro\meter} in NA62, well within the operating divergence of the CEDAR. 

    For the test beam, an ET 9820QB PMT was installed directly onto each exit window without the optical caps previously described and operated at a voltage of 2\,kV with a 30\,mV discriminator threshold voltage to achieve single photo-electron sensitivity. The test setup is described in Figure \ref{fig:CEDARHTest_dia} where a pair of scintillating 
    counters were installed at either end of the detector (one downstream and one upstream) to provide a trigger when each beam particle passed through the detector vessel. For a typical SPS beam pulse (spill), there were $2.8\times 10^{5}$ particles, and for each triggered particle within the spill, the six, seven and eight-fold 
    coincidences were recorded in a database for later analysis. The N-fold coincidences were defined where N PMTs were in coincidence within a given time window.

As described in Section \ref{sec:cedar_desc}, the CEDAR is attached to XY motors, which are used to align the vessel to the beam. The diagram in Figure \ref{fig:alignment_dia}
    depicts how moving the CEDAR vessel would have impacted the position of the Cherenkov ring at the diaphragm. The alignment aims to centre the ring directly within the diaphragm aperture to maximise the light yield.

    It was possible to quantify the alignment in two ways: to maximise the number of detected photons per beam particle and to balance the number of detected photons per trigger on each PMT. For the first alignment, the gas pressure was set at 4.2\,bar and the diaphragm at a wide aperture of 19\,mm to 
    allow the light from all beam particles to pass through the diaphragm, with the results shown in Figure \ref{fig:alignment_res1}. The motors were moved until the counts on the PMT per trigger were around 100 and were balanced (shown on the right of Figure \ref{fig:alignment_res1}).
    A large aperture allowed for the rough calculation of the PMT efficiency, as a fully efficient detector would expect the number of photo-electrons on each PMT to equal the number of triggers. From Figure \ref{fig:alignment_res1}
    it was clear that the PMTs are at least 95\,\% efficiency with the average being 98.3\,\%.

    After the first alignment, another with a smaller diaphragm aperture was essential to ensure the detector's fine alignment with the beam. 
    The second alignment was conducted at a pressure of 4.29\,bar, where the proton ring had a radius of 100\,mm at the diaphragm with a diaphragm aperture of 1.3\,mm. For which the alignment results are shown in Figure \ref{fig:alignment_res2} and

    \begin{equation}\label{eq:Lamb1}
        \lambda= \ln \left[ 1+\frac{8}{\eta_{7}/\eta_{8}-1} \right]
    \end{equation}
    and

        \lambda= \ln \left[ 1 + \frac{14}{ \sqrt{4 - 7(1-\eta_{6}/\eta_{8})}-2} \right]
    \end{equation}

    \begin{equation}\label{eq:sup-gaus}
        f(X) = a \cdot e^{-\left(\frac{(x - \mu)^2}{2 \sigma^2}\right)^p}
    \end{equation}
    with $a$, $\mu$, $\sigma$ and $p$ being parameters found using a minimised chi squared fit.

A photon that hit the centre of the spherical mirror was selected, and the positions

A target representing the PMT array, shown in Figure \ref{fig:mirrortarget}, was placed at the correct distance from the spherical mirror. The laser was placed to simulate the direction of light exiting the CEDAR-H and 
    the spherical mirror was adjusted so that the laser light hit the centre of the target, ensuring that the light would hit the centre of the PMT array. This process was then repeated for all eight spherical mirrors.

    The mirror support cylinder was then attached to the nose of the CEDAR-H, the KTAG photon-detection system was reassembled at the upstream end of the vessel, and all connections were reconnected and tested.

    Compared to the setup of the CEDAR-W, several modifications were made due to the requirements for the use of \HH\ within the ECN3 experimental hall. It was expected that some negligible amount of gas would leak from the CEDAR-H 
    , so all precautions had to be taken in order to prevent the concentration of gas from reaching dangerous levels between the Lower Explosive Limit (LEL) of 4\,\% and Upper Explosive Limit of 75\,\% in air. Any area where there was a potential 
    for a \HH\ leak was designated as an explosive atmosphere (ATEX) zone 2. In the ATEX zones, there are no electrical connectors, and all metal parts of the CEDAR-H are grounded to prevent any ignition sources. 
    During \HH\ operation, the diaphragm end switches are manually isolated through a key located by the KTAG electronics rack, as these are not ATEX rated and have the potential to create a spark. The ATEX zones extended 80\,cm from the CEDAR-H vessel and required 
    experts had to have ATEX training in order to access.
    Any confined space where the \HH\ can acuminate, such as the KTAG enclosure and the box containing the diaphragm potentiometer and motor, was flushed with a constant supply of nitrogen to prevent the build-up of \HH. Safety interlocks were installed to turn off the KTAG electronics 
    in the event the supply of \NN is interrupted. A second interlock was in place to turn off the KTAG electronics if there was a sizeable pressure drop in the CEDAR-H vessel. The pressure sensor precision limits the defined pressure drop for the interlock. A small leak was defined to be a measured drop in pressure of 0.02\,mbar per minute 
    measured by a rolling average across an hour. A major leak interlock, which would represent a rupture of the aluminium windows, would be triggered if the pressure dropped by 0.1\ mbar per second. 
    This major leak interlock would cut power to the CEDAR and neighbouring areas, turning off vacuum pumps and the GTK. To avoid the possibility of the window rupture, the CEDAR-H has a safety valve attached to the exhaust on top of a CEDAR vessel, which limits the pressure in the vessel to a maximum value of 5\,bar. 
    The aluminium windows have been tested to withstand a pressure of 7.5\,bar before installation so that the safety valve would prevent any damage to the windows.

    \section{Commissioning at CERN}
    The commissioning of the \CH in the NA62 beam-line was completed at the start of the 2023 run. After the CERN beam experts conducted the start-of-run activities and the beam was steered through the CEDAR-H vessel, the beam intensity was set at 10\,\% of the NA62 nominal value, corresponding to a particle rate 
    of 60\,Hz. Periodic triggers were used during the \CH commissioning in order to select all beam particles and measure the beam composition. The periodic triggers are where a period is selected between the start of burst (SoB) and end of burst (EoB) at which data was collected. At the beam intensity selected, the period was set so that $2 \times 10^{5}$ triggers per spill was collected. 
    For each triggered event, the KTAG data acquisition system recorded the time and the channel ID of each PMT signal within a 100\,ns time window defined by the trigger. The data collected was then reconstructed using the NA62 framework with the individual PMT signals clustered using an algorithm that selects the signals within a 4\,ns time window into candidates. 
    As each individual PMT signal was recorded, the light yield was defined using a different method when compared to the test beam. In the test beam, the ratios of the coincidences were used to calculate the light yield using statistical equations. However, at NA62, the light yield can be measured precisely due to the high granularity of the KTAG photon-detection system and can be defined as the number of photo-electrons per beam particle candidate.
    The identification of the beam particle utilised a 5-fold coincidence, compared to the 6-fold used in the test beam, due to the availability of data and the reduced noise level with the KTAG photon-detection system.

    As the CEDAR had been removed from H6, then moved to ECN3 and installed into the beam line, the first step of the commissioning was to validate that the detector was still functional using \NN\ before filling the vessel with \HH.

    This would allow to confirm the basic functionality of the detector without the complications added by \HH in case a hardware intervention was needed.

The data was reconstructed using a 4\,ns time window, with the results shown in Figure \ref{fig:cedarH_1}. Compared to the test beam, the y-axis of Figure \ref{fig:cedarH_1} is 
    arbitrary as the total number of triggers was not recorded. Instead, the number of coincidences was normalised to the beam intensity, which in NA62 can be measured through counts in argonion chamber detector. This made the direct beam composition difficult to calculate, but the ratios of the three peaks were to be used to check if the composition was what was expected. Figure \ref{fig:cedarH_1} shows the proton pressure at 4.3\,bar and the kaon 
    pressure at 3.88\,bar. At the kaon pressure the max light yield corresponded to $21.71 \pm 0.02$ photo-electrons per kaon candidate.  The kaon pressure in NA62 was higher than what was found at the test-beam, 3.88\,bar compared to 3.85\,bar due to a 2\,K temperature difference of the radiator gas in ECN3 compared to H6. The pion pressure was less clear due to the large flux of pions in the beam which overwhelmed the NA62 readout system, producing inconsistent results between 3.68 and 3.76\,bar. However, the falling edge of the pion peak was the important measurable, allowing the estimation of the pion miss-identification probability. At the kaon pressure of 3.88\,bar the measured normalised 5-fold coincidence was 0.0956. The pion contribution was then estimated using the fit of a super-Gaussian on the falling edge and extrapolated to the kaon pressure.
    The estimated pion contribution to the normalised 5-fold coincidence was $5.36 \times 10^{-06}$, which resulted in a pion miss-identification probability of $0.6\times 10^{-5}$ at the kaon pressure.
    
    The light yield obtained with a 1.8\,mm diaphragm aperture compared with the results from the test beam at a 2\,mm aperture shown in Figure \ref{fig:testbeamPresures}, showed an increase of just over 10\,\%. This increase was produced by adding the KTAG photon-detection system compared to the single PMTs used in the test-beam, which was predicted from the simulations.

    The photo-electrons per candidate and pion miss-identification probability suggested that a diaphragm aperture of 1.8\,mm was an excellent candidate for the working point as it passed all the requirements for operation at NA62

    However, even after the updated alignment, the number of photo-electrons per kaon candidate was $21.47 \pm 0.04$ suggesting a slightly lower light yield than the previous pressure scan completed with a 1.8\ mm diaphragm aperture. 
    
    The results show that the CEDAR-H was made operational in NA62 and was able to separate the kaon and pion peaks with a high level of confidence. It was decided that the pressure scan from the 1.8\,mm diaphragm aperture produced the best results for use in the run due to the superior light yield, resulting in a better kaon identification efficiency and pion miss-identification probability that was better than what was required at NA62.

        \item Kaon Pressure: 3.88\,bar
        \item Diaphragm Aperture: 1.8\,mm

    The commissioning procedure described above was extensive but was required due to the new detector installation in 2023. At the start of each data taking run, the commissioning has to be repeated with alignment and pressure scans to ensure the detector is still operational and to find the working point for the run. Though the detector does not move between years, the beam position can move compared to the previous years, as well as 
    slight movements of the vessel can be possible at each \HH filling due to possible deformation of the beam-pipe when the detector is brought in and out of vacuum. Also, it was possible that the pressure set points for the beam particles could differ due to the environmental changes in ECN3.

    Due to the changes to the working point pressure between the test beam and between the 2023 and 2024 runs, a new method was developed to monitor the CEDAR-H and to take into account the environmental variables to ensure the refractive index of the radiator gas did not change by large amounts which would result in lower kaon detection efficiency. The pressure of the vessel and temperature at both the front and
    back of the CEDAR were recorded and shown in Figure \ref{fig:PoverT_23} for the 2023 run and Figure \ref{fig:PoverT_24} for the 2024 run. The pressure was divided by temperature, as the ratio should be consistent for a sealed vessel like the CEDAR, and the ratio was multiplied by a room temperature of 293.15\,K, resulting in the pressure at normalised room temperature. 
    During previous runs at NA62, the CEDAR would be kept at a fixed pressure and regulated to stay at the desired pressure, which would be correct if the CEDAR vessel did not change in temperature. If the temperature of the gas changed with the pressure regulated to a fixed value, the resultant refractive index of the radiator gas would diverge from the desired value, reducing the detector efficiency. Even though the CEDAR is extensively insulated, it is designed to mitigate sharp temperature changes. It does not stop the CEDAR vessel from slowly changing temperature with the environment due to seasons. To mitigate this, the CEDAR
    was sealed with a fixed amount of gas and the pressure was allowed to vary.
    A good example of how this new method was utilised was in the final days of the 2023 run in Figure \ref{fig:PoverT_23} where the actual pressure increased by nearly 10\,mbar, whereas the normalised pressures stayed constant, suggesting that the temperature in ECN3 increased, reiterating that the pressure should not be automatically maintained at a specific pressure. 
    However, as CEDAR-H was not perfectly sealed, especially for Hydrogen, a small leak was expected, so there was a gradual decrease in the normalised pressure. The normalised pressure and the resultant number of photo-electrons per candidate were used to decide if the CEDAR required extra filling during the run due to the small loss of gas, which can be seen in Figures \ref{fig:PoverT_23} and \ref{fig:PoverT_23} as the large 
    spikes in pressure at various points.

    \section{CEDAR-H Performance at NA62} \label{sec:cedarHperformance}
    The performance of the CEDAR-H in standard NA62 run conditions was assessed using data collected in 2023 and compared to CEDAR-W with data collected in 2022. A sample of kaons was collected using a selection of $K^{+}\to\pi^{+}\pi^{+}\pi^{-}$ decays which were fully reconstructed using the STRAW spectrometer, described in Section \ref{sec:STRAW}. The KTAG was used to reconstruct the kaon candidates using 
    a nominal 2\,ns clustering time window. The number of photo-electrons per kaon was measured from events that had at least a five-sector coincidence, and the results are shown in Figure \ref{fig:NHitsCompare}. The light yield was calculated by fitting a Poissonian distribution between 4 and 24 photo-electrons. The smaller range was used to remove the contributions from the large number of 
    photo-electrons, produced from secondary kaons in coincidence, reconstructed as a single kaon. The average light yield for the CEDAR-H was 20.6 photo-electrons per kaon compared to 18.1 for CEDAR-W.
    \begin{figure}[H]
        \centering
        \includegraphics[width=0.7\textwidth]{plots/CEDAR_NHits.pdf}
        \caption{Comparison of the number of photo-electrons per kaon candidate of CEDAR-H in 2023 and CEDAR-W in 2022}%
        \label{fig:NHitsCompare}%
    \end{figure}    
    The time resolution of the KTAG + CEDAR-H was calculated to be 66\,ps using the equation,
    \begin{equation}
        \sigma_{t} = \frac{\sigma_{PMT}}{\sqrt{N_{PE}}}
    \end{equation}
    where $\sigma_{PMT}$ is the single photo-electron time resolution of the PMTs of 300\,ps, and $N_{PE}$ is the number of photo-electrons per candidate, compared to 71\,ps for CEDAR-W.
    
    The kaon tagging efficiency was calculated by comparing the number of N-Fold coincidences against the total number of events. The results of the computation are shown in Figure \ref{fig:Effciency}, with CEDAR-H having a kaon identification efficiency of 99.7\,\% compared to 99.5\,\% for CEDAR-W.
    The identification efficiency was compared to the analytical expectation with the photo-electron yield produced from Figure \ref{fig:NHitsCompare}, calculated assuming an ideal detector and uniform photo-electrons across all eight sectors. The results from the data showed that the eight sector efficiencies 
    were higher than the expectation due to the contribution from secondary kaons in coincidence. Due to the higher light yield, the experiment had the option of changing the identification classification to a 6-fold coincidence, which provides an identification greater than 95\,\% and would improve the pion miss-identification probability dramatically.
