    As described in Section~\ref{sec:na62}, until 2022 NA62, has utilised a \gls{cw} filled with \gls{n2} at a nominal gas pressure of 1.71\,bar (absolute pressure) to identify kaons from the unseparated hadron beam.
    For the searches for ultra-rare decays it is essential to reduce backgrounds from dominant kaon decays and from upstream sources produced by scattered beam particles. Minimising the amount of material in the path of the beam is required to reduce the upstream backgrounds. 
    It is possible to design a new \gls{CEDAR} with hydrogen as the radiator gas to reduce the amount of multiple scattering of beam particles within the vessel. The design, testing and commissioning of this new detector is detailed in this chapter.

    

    \glspl{CEDAR} were designed and built at CERN to identify secondary beam particles at the \gls{SPS} \cite{Bovet:1982xf}. Two types of \gls{CEDAR} were developed at CERN:
    \gls{cw} and \gls{cn}, corresponding to the areas of use. The two designs differ in terms of the momentum range in which they can identify beam particles, \gls{cn} 
    are able to separate kaons and pions up to \SI{300}{\giga\electronvolt\per\clight} and detect protons down to \SI{60}{\giga\electronvolt\per\clight}. \gls{cw} can detect protons down to \SI{12}{\giga\electronvolt\per\clight}
    and separate kaons and pions up to \SI{150}{{\giga\electronvolt\per\clight}}.

    The \gls{cw} with \gls{n2}, contributes the most significant amount of material in the path of the beam, corresponding to $39 \times 10^{-3}\,X_0$, 
    where $X_0$ is one radiation length. The \gls{CEDAR} vessel contributes $3.9 \times 10^{-3}\,X_0$ and the \gls{n2} gas $35 \times 10^{-3}\,X_0$.
    The material from the \gls{cw} with \gls{n2} results in the multiple scattering of beam particles, introducing an angular divergence in the beam of \SI{32}{\micro\radian}. 
    It is proposed that a \gls{CEDAR} filled with \gls{h2} would reduce the material due to the radiator gas's lower density. Using \gls{h2} as a radiator gas would require a pressure of 3.8\,bar 
    to produce a similar Cherenkov angle as \gls{n2} gas at \SI{1.71}{\bar}. \gls{h2} at this pressure introduces $3.4 \times 10^{-3}\,X_0$ of material, which would result in a factor of five reduction in material compared to \gls{n2}, resulting in a total 
    material of $7.3 \times 10^{-3}\,X_0$. The expected angular beam divergence is \SI{13}{\micro\radian} in both the horizontal and vertical dimensions. A summary of the material budget of each detector is shown in Table~\ref{tab:cedar_material_comparison}.

    \section{\gls{CEDAR} Design}\label{sec:cedar_desc}
    A \gls{CEDAR} comprises a thermally insulated gas vessel separated into two sections: a 4.3\,m long cylinder with an inner diameter of 53\,cm and a vessel cap 
    which differs in length, depending on the \gls{CEDAR} variant. The \gls{cw} has a vessel cap length of 34\,cm compared to 28\,cm for the \gls{cn} variant. The vessel cap is attached to the main cylinder 
    at the upstream end. The gas vessel is separated from the vacuum of the beam pipe by two aluminium windows at the upstream and downstream ends. A description of the \gls{CEDAR} vessel, along with the optics enclosed, is shown in Figure~\ref{fig:CEDAR_DIA}.

    The beam is designed to pass through the \gls{CEDAR} vessel along the longitudinal axis through the radiator gas and aluminium windows. When the beam particle's pass through the vessel at a velocity $v$ between
    %When charged particles travel faster than the speed of light in a medium, Cherenkov radiation is produced at an angle of,
    %When the beam particles pass through the vessel Cherenkov at a velocity $v$ between,
    \begin{equation}
        \frac{c}{n} < v < c
    \end{equation}
    where $c$ is the speed of light in a vacuum, and $n$ is the refractive index of the radiator gas, Cherenkov light is produced. The Cherenkov light from the beam particles is emitted as cones with a semi-aperture angle dependent on the speed of the beam particle,
    \begin{equation}\label{eq:Cherenkov2}
    \cos{\theta_c} = \frac{1}{\beta n}
    \end{equation}
    with $n$ being the refractive index of the radiator gas, $\theta_c$ is the semi-aperture angle and $\beta$ the beam particle velocity defined as $\beta = \frac{p}{\sqrt{p^2 + M^2}}$ where $M$ is the particles mass and $p$ is the momentum. A differential counter is designed to focus the Cherenkov light to a ring image in order to distinguish particles of different masses. Particles of different masses 
    produce ring images of different radii.

    The \gls{CEDAR} contains optical elements inside the vessel to allow the reflection and focusing of the produced Cherenkov light to reach the diaphragm at a radius of 100\,mm, as displayed in Figure~\ref{fig:CEDAR_DIA} as the orange lines. 
    The light produced is reflected back upstream by a Mangin mirror \cite{Riedl:74}. This is a lens comprising two spherical surfaces, with the downstream surface cemented onto a concave mirror having an identical radius of curvature.
    The radius at which the light is focused at the focal point is defined as, 
    \begin{equation}
        R_{focal} = f \theta_c
    \end{equation}
    where $f_m$ is the focal length for the entire system. The focal length of a Mangin mirror with two spherical surfaces is defined as, 
    \begin{equation} 
        \frac{1}{f_m} = \frac{2n_m}{r_2} - \frac{2(n_m - 1)}{r_1} 
    \end{equation}
    where $n_m$ is the refractive index of the Mangin mirror, $r_1$ and $r_2$ are the radii of curvature of the refracting and reflecting surfaces. 
    %A mirror comprising two spherical surfaces with the downstream lens cemented onto a concave mirror. 
    The mirror is designed to reduce spherical aberrations, an effect intrinsic to lenses and curved mirrors. This is where light that strikes the spherical surface off-centre is refracted/reflected
    to different points compared to the light that would strike the centre of the mirror. After the mirror, the light goes through a plano-convex lens named the Chromatic Corrector, which is designed in such a way that it corrects for the chromatic dispersion of the radiator gas
    and directs the light towards the diaphragm. 
    Chromatic dispersion occurs due to different wavelengths of light refracting differently in a medium, causing the Cherenkov ring to disperse, which would result in some of the light being lost when it reaches the small diaphragm opening. For a plano-convex lens the focal distance is defined as 
    \begin{equation}
        \frac{1}{f_{cc}} = \frac{(n_{cc}-1)}{r_{cc}}
    \end{equation}
    where $n_{cc}$ is the refractive index of the Chromatic Corrector and $r_{cc}$ is the radius of curvature of the convex side. Combined with the Mangin mirror the final focal distance for the system is calculated as
    \begin{equation}
        \frac{1}{f} = \frac{1}{f_m} + \frac{1}{f_{cc}} - \frac{d}{f_{m}f_{cc}}
    \end{equation}
    where $d$ is the distance between the Mangin mirror and the corrector lens. Both the Mangin mirror and the Chromatic corrector have a hole in the centre to allow for the beam to pass through without impacting the optics. The optical parameters for the \gls{cw} are found in Table \ref{table:CedarParameterValues}.
    
    \newline\indent The diaphragm is shown in Figure~\ref{fig:cedarpics}, as a disk with eight separate apertures and is located at the focal distance of the Mangin mirror. Each aperture has an inner and outer element which allows the aperture to open to  
    20\,mm and close to 0\,mm symmetrically about a radius of 100\,mm. These elements can be seen in Figure~\ref{fig:cedarpics} (right), where the aperture is set at a small value, used only to select a single Cherenkov ring of radius 100\,mm.  
    The mechanism is operated by a stepper motor connected to the shaft shown in Figure~\ref{fig:cedarpics} (left). Turning the shaft clockwise opens the diaphragm, and counter-clockwise closes it. The 
    shaft is connected to a potentiometer that measures the rotation, which is used to convert to an aperture in millimetres after calibration. A pair of end switches are in place at the extreme ends of the rotation to alert the user if the aperture 
    is fully open or fully closed, which is essential to avoid any damage to the mechanical system.

    In the original \gls{CEDAR} design \cite{Bovet:1982xf}, light exiting the \gls{CEDAR} through the exit windows is detected by eight ET-9820QB \glspl{PMT}, with one attached to each window. 
    A particle is identified when there is a coincidence of signals in at least six \glspl{PMT}.
    However, as original \glspl{CEDAR} are designed for beam fluxes of kHz, they would not be applicable for NA62 which operates with beam fluxes of 10s of MHz. This implies strict timing requirements on the selection of kaons of less than 100\,ps. % a kaon identification efficiency greater than 95\,\% and a pion miss-identification probability of less than $10^{-4}$. 
    To achieve these strict requirements, a photon detection system called the KTAG was purpose-built \cite{GOUDZOVSKI201586} and attached to the 
    upstream end of the \gls{CEDAR}. The light exiting the quartz windows traverses through optical caps attached to the exit windows, which focuses the light onto spherical mirrors. The eight spherical mirrors, one for each exit 
    window, reflect the light 90 degrees onto eight \gls{PMT} arrays and broaden the light to increase the resultant light spot. The spherical mirrors are manufactured from off-the-shelf optical lenses, then coated in aluminium and are shown in Figure~\ref{fig:spherical-mirror}. Each light spot is defined as a sector. 
    Each sector contains a light guide before a \gls{PMT} array equipped with 48 Hamamatsu \glspl{PMT} (32 of type R9880U-110 and 16 of type R7400U-03). 
    The light guide consists of a matrix of cones coated by aluminised mylar to maximise their reflectivity and are used to direct light onto the active area of each \gls{PMT}.

    The \gls{CEDAR} is designed to allow the light emitted at a specific angle to pass through a diaphragm aperture by selecting the pressure of the radiator gas. 
    When the pressure of the gas is raised the density increases which results in the refractive index increasing. The increase in refractive index results in an increase in the Cherenkov production angle, as defined in Equation~\ref{eq:Cherenkov2} and the final Cherenkov ring image radius. For an ideal gas this is defined as
    \begin{equation}
        (n-1) \propto P / T,
    \end{equation}
    where $n$ is the refractive index, $P$ is the pressure and $T$ is the temperature of the gas.
    This allows a pressure to be selected where a kaon at \SI{75}{\giga\electronvolt\per\clight} produces a Cherenkov ring radius of 100\,mm at the diaphragm, matching the centre of the \gls{CEDAR} aperture. Selecting an aperture of less than 2.5\,mm allows only the light from the kaon to pass through the diaphragm, with the 
    central radius of the pion ring being 2\,mm larger and the central radius of the proton ring being 6\,mm smaller, shown in Figure~\ref{fig:WestNitrogen}. The selection of a single particle Cherenkov ring depends on the beam divergence being less than \SI{100}{\micro\meter} and the axis of the \gls{CEDAR} vessel being aligned parallel
    to the beam. This is to ensure that the divergence of the Cherenkov light is minimised. The \gls{CEDAR} alignment to the beam is performed using a pair of motors at the downstream end of the detector. The motors displace the downstream end by $\pm 5$\,mm in both the X and Y direction, pivoting the \gls{CEDAR} vessel around the fixed upstream end. 
    
    At a temperature of 293\,K, the nominal gas pressure to select kaons at \SI{75}{\giga\electronvolt\per\clight} is defined at \SI{1.71}{\bar} for \gls{n2} in \gls{cw} and \SI{3.85}{\bar} for \gls{h2} in \gls{ch}, with the values changing by 0.34\,\% for every 1\,K of temperature difference.
    The \gls{CEDAR} vessel is sealed and insulated in order to operate as a closed volume with a fixed amount of gas. It is also expected to be insensitive to fluctuations in the environmental temperature due to the extensive insulation around the pressure vessel.

    \section{Development of \gls{ch}}
    
    %Due to the different chromatic dispersion effects in Hydrogen, it was not possible to use the \gls{cw} using \gls{h2}. From simulations (Figure~\ref{fig:WestHyrogen}), it was apparent the light was distorted 
    %when compared to the \gls{cw} with \gls{n2}\ shown in Figure~\ref{fig:WestNitrogen}. 
    In Figure~\ref{fig:WestHyrogen}, simulations of the \gls{cw} with \gls{h2} radiator gas show that the correction of the chromatic dispersion is inefficient, and photons of different wavelengths still display different radii at the diaphragm after the correction.
    In this configuration, it is not feasible to only select the light from kaons using the \gls{CEDAR} diaphragm aperture without sacrificing light yield and operating at a lower detection efficiency. 
    It is decided that a new vessel has to be developed with updated optics optimised for use with \gls{h2}. The vessel is called \gls{ch}.

    An available \gls{cn} vessel is chosen as the \gls{ch} structure, which reduces the costs as most of the mechanical components, such as the diaphragm and mirror supports, are not modified due to their complexity. This 
    reduces the degrees of freedom for optimisation to three: the radii of curvature of the chromatic corrector lens and the two surfaces of the Mangin mirror. Initial parameters of the optical components are found using an analytical ray-tracing 
    procedure \cite{Bache:2845388, Henshaw:2915320}. The parameters are iterated to achieve a ring radius of 100\,mm and to minimise the width of the ring at the focal distance of the Mangin mirror. 
    Due to the pressure-dependent effects of the chromatic dispersion and spherical aberration, each computation for the optical elements is completed at pressures between 3.8 to 4.1\,bar in steps of 0.1\,bar.

    Once the initial parameters have been found, a full GEANT4 \cite{Agostinelli2003Geant4} simulation is employed to validate and optimise the results from the analytical computation. The GEANT4 simulation further traced the light coming out of the \gls{CEDAR} vessel through the diaphragm aperture, 
    and then reflected by the spherical mirrors onto the \gls{PMT} arrays, as shown in Figure~\ref{fig:CEDAR_DIA}. The simulation is then used to modify the optical parameters 
    to maximise the number of photons reaching the KTAG \glspl{PMT}. The radius of the beam hole of the Mangin mirror is reduced with respect to the original \gls{CEDAR} design, resulting in a larger reflective surface and increasing number of 
    photons reaching the \glspl{PMT}. The spherical mirrors outside the vessel are upgraded to improve the light distribution on the \gls{PMT} arrays. It is found that using the condenser lenses from the \gls{cw} as opposed to the \gls{cn} resulted in more significant photon yield. 

    The optimisation studies produce results with negligible differences in the number of photons reaching the \gls{PMT} array between 3.8 and 4.1\,bar as the increase of Cherenkov photons at high pressure is counteracted by the improved light distribution on the array at low pressures.
    Due to \gls{h2} safety considerations, the lower pressure of 3.8\,bar is selected. The optics sufficiently correct the chromatic dispersion at this pressure setting, producing a Gaussian width of 0.4\,mm for the kaon and pion rings at the diaphragm (Figure~\ref{fig:cedarHsim}). This configuration allows for the selection of 
    kaons with a 2\,mm \gls{CEDAR} diaphragm with minimal light from the pion passing through.

    \section{\gls{ch} Test-beam}
    After construction of the \gls{ch} detector, it was tested and characterised in 2022 during a test at the H6 beamline at CERN, shown in Figure~\ref{fig:CEDARHTest}.
    The test-beam has three main aims:
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item Understand the safety procedures related to \gls{h2} gas operations.
        \item To ensure that the optics have been installed and aligned correctly;
        \item To measure the kaon identification efficiency and pion misidentification probability at several different diaphragm apertures.
    \end{enumerate}
    \begin{figure}[t]
        \centering 
        \includegraphics[width=0.7\textwidth]{imgs/test_beamInstall.png}
        \linespread{1.0}
        \caption{Image of \gls{ch} being installed on the H6 beamline at CERN in 2022.}
        \label{fig:CEDARHTest}
    \end{figure}

    The H6 beamline is located at the CERN north area and is produced from \SI{400}{\giga\electronvolt\per\clight} primary protons from the SPS interacting with a target consisting of a beryllium rod, producing an unseparated \SI{75}{\giga\electronvolt\per\clight} hadron beam similar to the NA62 beam. 
    \gls{ch} is installed 440\,m downstream of the target, compared to 70\,m when installed in NA62, resulting in a similar beam composition when compared to NA62, with 4\,\% kaons, 25\,\% protons, and 71\,\% pions. 
    The beam divergence at the entrance of the \gls{CEDAR} is \SI{80}{\micro\meter} in the H6 beamline compared to \SI{70}{\micro\meter} in NA62, well within the operating divergence of \SI{100}{\micro\meter} for the \gls{CEDAR}. 

    For the test-beam, an ET 9820QB \gls{PMT} is installed directly onto each exit window without the optical caps and is operated at a voltage of 2\,kV with a 30\,mV discriminator threshold voltage to achieve single photo-electron sensitivity. The test setup is illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:CEDARHTest_dia} where a pair of scintillating 
    counters are installed at each end of the detector to provide a trigger when each beam particle passed through the vessel. For a typical \gls{spill}, there are $2.8\times 10^{5}$ particles. For each triggered particle within the \gls{spill}, the six, seven and eight-fold 
    coincidences are recorded for later analysis. The N-fold coincidences are defined where N \glspl{PMT} are in coincidence within a given time window.

        \includegraphics[width=0.7\textwidth]{imgs/test_beam_diagram_jack.pdf}

        \caption{Diagram showing the \gls{CEDAR} test-beam setup in H6 from the side view.}

    Due to the flammable nature of \gls{h2} gas, some safety considerations have to be made compared to running with \gls{n2}. An area of 1\,m radius around the ends of the \gls{ch} is designated as an explosive atmosphere (ATEX) zone where extra care has to be taken to avoid the chance of a spark forming. The setup is certified by CERN before the start of the test-beam. A number of changes are implemented by CERN on the gas systems in H6 to accommodate the safety systems for operation with \gls{h2}. 
    The pressure is monitored by the gas control software and alarms are implemented if drops in pressure are observed. Also, some operational changes compared to \gls{n2} are modified for the pressure scans, where a maximum pressure is set at 4.4\,bar to reduce the chance of a vessel rupture.

    \subsection{Pressure and Diaphragm Scans}
    The remaining goals of the test-beam are to determine the parameters for operation at NA62. This includes the determination of the kaon pressure to maximise kaon yield, the optimal diaphragm aperture to maximise light from the kaon and minimising the impact of light from the pion. This is completed through a number of pressure and diaphragm scans described below.

    To determine the kaon operating pressure, a pressure scan is performed. Before the scan, the pressure of the \gls{ch} is set at the highest pressure (4.4\,bar), where there are no selected beam particles. A selected beam particle is defined when there is signal in at least 6 \glspl{PMT} in coincidence.
    The scan consists of reducing the pressure in small steps and collecting data at each step. This is completed at a diaphragm width of 2.3\,mm, larger than the standard operating setting used with the \gls{cw} at NA62. The aperture is chosen to maximise the light yield of the detector. This is to 
    compare the computed beam composition to the expected for the H6 beamline. The results of the pressure scan are found in Figure~\ref{fig:1presscan_testbeam}, with each beam component visible in the plots. The protons produce a maximum light yield at 4.26\,bar with 0.232 coincidences per trigger, with the build-up in coincidences caused by the gradual entrance of 
    the Cherenkov ring into the aperture of the diaphragm. The kaon has a maximum light yield at 3.844, with 0.037 coincidences per trigger, and the pion has a maximum light yield at 3.69\,bar, with 0.721 coincidences per trigger. The fraction of triggers is within 1--2\,\% of the expected beam composition, resulting in the selection of a particle in 99.1\,\% of the triggers.
    It is expected that not all light from the proton ring would pass through the 2.3\,mm diaphragm aperture due to its large Gaussian width and distorted shape, shown in Figure~\ref{fig:cedarHsim}.

    \begin{equation}\label{eq:Lamb1}
        \lambda= \ln \left[ 1+\frac{8}{\eta_{7}/\eta_{8}-1} \right]
    \end{equation}
    and
    \begin{equation}\label{eq:Lamb2}
        \lambda= \ln \left[ 1 + \frac{14}{ \sqrt{4 - 7(1-\eta_{6}/\eta_{8})}-2} \right],

At the kaon pressure (3.844\,bar), the ratios of $\eta_{8}/\eta_{6} = 0.500$ and $\eta_{8}/\eta_{7} = 0.535$ produce an average yield of $\lambda = 2.45$, which
    results in a total of 19.63 photo-electrons per kaon. This suggests that the optics have been correctly manufactured and installed. However, the diaphragm is wider with respect to the normal NA62 setting. It is clear that the 2.3\,mm diaphragm can not be used 
    in NA62 due to the unsatisfactory kaon-pion separation seen in Figure~\ref{fig:1presscan_testbeam}. This would result in a non-zero probability for misidentification of a pion at the kaon pressure.

    \subsection{Hydrogen Safety}
    Compared to the setup of the \gls{cw}, several modifications are made due to the requirements for the use of \gls{h2} within the ECN3 experimental hall. It is expected that some amount of gas would leak from the \gls{ch}, 
    so all precautions have to be taken in order to prevent the concentration of gas from reaching dangerous levels between the Lower Explosive Limit (LEL) of 4\,\% and Upper Explosive Limit of 75\,\% volume in air for \gls{h2}. Any area where there is a potential 
    for a \gls{h2} leak is designated as an ATEX zone 2. In the ATEX zones, there are no electrical connectors, and metal parts of the \gls{ch} are grounded to prevent any ignition sources. 
    During \gls{h2} operation, the diaphragm end switches are manually isolated through a key located by the KTAG electronics rack, as these are not ATEX rated and have the potential to create a spark. The ATEX zones extend 80\,cm from the \gls{ch} vessel and require 
    experts to have ATEX training in order to access.
    Any confined space where the \gls{h2} can accumulate, such as the KTAG enclosure and the box containing the diaphragm potentiometer and motor is flushed with a constant supply of \gls{n2} to prevent the build-up of \gls{h2}. A safety interlock is installed to turn off the KTAG electronics 
    in the event the supply of \gls{n2} is interrupted. Another interlock is in place to turn off the KTAG electronics if there is a sizeable pressure drop in the \gls{ch} vessel. The pressure sensor precision limits the defined pressure drop for the interlock. A small leak is defined to be a measured drop in pressure of 0.02\,mbar per minute 
    measured by a rolling average across an hour. A major leak interlock, which would represent a rupture of the aluminium windows, would be triggered if the pressure drops by 0.1\ mbar per second. 
    This major leak interlock would cut power to the \gls{CEDAR} and neighbouring areas, turning off vacuum pumps and the GTK. To avoid the possibility of the window rupture, the \gls{ch} has a safety valve attached to the exhaust on top of a \gls{CEDAR} vessel, which limits the pressure in the vessel to a maximum value of 5\,bar. 
    The aluminium windows have been tested to withstand a pressure of 7.5\,bar before installation so that the safety valve would prevent any damage to the windows.

    Dedicated flammable gas detectors are installed on the top of the metal pavilions at both upstream and downstream ends of the \gls{ch} vessel, shown in Figure~\ref{fig:cedarpics_ns62}. The detectors monitor the concentration of \gls{h2} levels outside the vessel 
    and result in a warning if the concentration exceeds 10\,\% of the LEL. The gas detectors are connected to the \gls{ch} \gls{DSS}. A concentration of 20\,\% of the LEL generates a level 3 alarm, which would cut the power in the area if the alarm is triggered, and the CERN fire brigade would be informed.
    The main metal pavilion covers the upstream end of the \gls{ch} vessel, the KTAG photon-detection system, and the gas distribution panel, and it is designed to guide any leaking gas to the \gls{h2} sensor. Another smaller metal pavilion is installed at the downstream end of the vessel.

    During the installation, the \gls{ch} is leak tested at 3.85\,bar for 10 days, where the leak rate is measured to be 0.2\,litres per day at standard pressure and temperature. This satisfies the safety requirements for use in ECN3, given the measured airflow from the ventilation of the experimental hall.

    \section{Commissioning at CERN}
    The commissioning of the \gls{ch} in the NA62 beamline was completed at the start of the 2023 run. The goals of the commissioning are to ascertain the operational working point for kaon detection at NA62 and to ensure that the detector is operational after its transport and installation. 

    After the CERN beam experts completed the start-of-run activities and the beam is steered through the \gls{ch} vessel, the beam intensity is set at 10\,\% of the NA62 nominal value, corresponding to a particle rate 
    of 60\,MHz. Periodic triggers are used during the \gls{ch} commissioning in order to select all beam particles and measure the beam composition. This trigger is where a period is selected between the start of burst (SoB) and end of burst (EoB) at which data are collected. At the beam intensity selected, the period is set so that $2 \times 10^{5}$ triggers per spill are collected. 
    For each triggered event, the KTAG data acquisition system records the time and the channel ID of each \gls{PMT} signal within a 100\,ns time window defined by the trigger. Individual \gls{PMT} signals are clustered into candidates by an algorithm that selects the hits within a 4\,ns time window. 
    As each individual \gls{PMT} signal is recorded, the light yield is defined using a different method when compared to the test-beam. In the test-beam, the ratios of the coincidences are used to calculate the light yield using Equations~\ref{eq:Lamb1} \&~\ref{eq:Lamb2}. However, at NA62, the exact light yield is measured precisely due to the high granularity of the KTAG photon-detection system and is defined as the number of photo-electrons per beam particle candidate.
    The identification of the beam particle utilised a 5-fold coincidence, compared to the 6-fold used in the test-beam, due to the reduced noise level with the KTAG photon-detection system.

    As the \gls{CEDAR} has been removed from H6, then moved to ECN3 and installed into the beamline, the first step of the commissioning is to validate that the detector is functional using \gls{n2} before filling the vessel with \gls{h2}. 
    %This was because any work that could have been conducted due to any issues caused by the transport to ECN3, would have been complicated by the presence of \gls{h2}. 
    This allows for the confirmation of the basic functionality of the detector without the complications added by \gls{h2}, in case a hardware intervention is needed.
    It is important to note that any results from this phase are preliminary due to the distorted nature of the Cherenkov light in the \gls{ch} with a \gls{n2} radiator gas.

This pressure scan is used to determine the pressure at which each beam element can be selected.

The pion-kaon peaks are not separated, which is not expected with a small diaphragm, so it is assumed that the detector alignment with the beam is not correct. %as the procedure might have been affected by other particles Cherenkov ring passing through the diaphragm aperture.

    \section{\gls{ch} Performance at NA62} \label{sec:cedarHperformance}
    The performance of the \gls{ch} in standard NA62 run conditions is assessed using data collected in 2023 and compared to \gls{cw} with data collected in 2022. A sample of kaons is collected using a selection of $K^{+}\to\pi^{+}\pi^{+}\pi^{-}$ decays fully reconstructed using the STRAW spectrometer, described in Section~\ref{sec:STRAW}. The KTAG is used to reconstruct the kaon candidates using 
    a nominal 2\,ns clustering time window. The number of photo-electrons per kaon is measured from events that have at least a five-sector coincidence, and the results are shown in Figure~\ref{fig:NHitsCompare}. The light yield is calculated by fitting a Poissonian distribution between 4 and 24 photo-electrons. The fitting range is selected to remove the contributions from the large number of 
    photo-electrons, produced from secondary kaons in coincidence, reconstructed as a single kaon. The average light yield for the \gls{ch} is 20.6 photo-electrons per kaon compared to 18.1 for \gls{cw}.
    \begin{figure}[t]
        \centering
        \includegraphics[width=0.7\textwidth]{plots/CEDAR_NHits.pdf}
        \caption{Comparison of the number of photo-electrons per kaon candidate of \gls{ch} in 2023 and \gls{cw} in 2022.}%
        \label{fig:NHitsCompare}%
    \end{figure}    
    The estimated time resolution of the KTAG + \gls{ch} is calculated to be 66\,ps using the equation,
    \begin{equation}
        \sigma_{t} = \frac{\sigma_{PMT}}{\sqrt{N_{PE}}}
    \end{equation}
    where $\sigma_{PMT}$ is the single photo-electron time resolution of the \glspl{PMT} of 300\,ps, and $N_{PE}$ is the number of photo-electrons per candidate, compared to 71\,ps for \gls{cw}.
    
    The kaon tagging efficiency is calculated by comparing the number of N-Fold coincidences against the total number of expected kaons. The results of the computation are shown in Figure~\ref{fig:Effciency}, with \gls{ch} having a kaon identification efficiency of 99.7\,\% compared to 99.5\,\% for \gls{cw}.
    The identification efficiency is compared to the analytical expectation with the photo-electron yield produced from Figure~\ref{fig:NHitsCompare}, calculated assuming an ideal detector and uniform photo-electrons across all eight sectors. The results for both detectors show that the eight sector efficiencies 
    are higher than the expectation due to the contribution from secondary kaons in coincidence. %Due to the higher light yield, the experiment had the option of changing the identification classification to a 6-fold coincidence, which provides an identification greater than 95\,\% and would improve the pion miss-identification probability dramatically.

    \section{Conclusions}
    The \gls{ch} detector was successfully tested at a test-beam at CERN in 2022 and was approved for use at NA62. 
    The detector was installed at NA62 in 2023 where it was commissioned and an operational working point for kaon detection was set with the following parameters,
    \begin{itemize}
        %\item Alignment: $X = -2.258$\,mm and $Y = -0.591$\,mm,
        \item Kaon pressure: 3.88\,bar,
        \item Diaphragm aperture: 1.8\,mm.
    \end{itemize}
    At this working point the \gls{ch} obtained an average number of 20.6 photo-electrons per kaon candidate at NA62, a 14\,\% increase with respect to \gls{cw} with an estimated pion misidentification probability of at least $10^{-4}$.
    The light yield represented a kaon identification efficiency of 99.7\,\% and a kaon time resolution of 66\,ps. The improvement of \gls{ch} compared to \gls{cw} represented a successful design, testing and commissioning of the detector for use at NA62.
    
    The \gls{ch} has been in operation since 2023 and has performed reliably and has exceeded the requirements for NA62.
