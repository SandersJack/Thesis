    CEDARs were designed and built at CERN to detect secondary beam particles at the Super Proton Synchrotron (SPS). Two types of CEDAR were developed at CERN:
    CEDAR-W and CEDAR-N, corresponding to the areas of use. The two designs differ in terms of energy range in which they can detect beam particles, with CEDAR-N 
    able to separate kaons and pions up to $300 GeV / c$ but not detect protons below $60 GeV / c$. Whereas CEDAR-W can detect protons down to $12 GeV / c$ 
    and separate kaons and pions up to $150 GeV / c$. 
    %% Describe the previous performance of CEDAR-W with Nitrogen
    \newline\indent Described in Section \ref{sec:na62}, since 2016 NA62 has utilised a CEDAR-W filled with Nitrogen ( \NN\ ) at a pressure of 1.71\,bar (absolute pressure).
    In this configuration, the CEDAR contributed to the most significant amount of material in the path of the beam, introducing $39 \times 10^{-3}\,X_0$ of material, 
    where $X_0$ is one radiation length. The aluminum windows of the CEDAR vessel contributed $3.9 \times 10^{-3}\,X_0$ and the \NN\ gas contributed $35 \times 10^{-3}\,X_0$.
    The material from the \CW\ with \NN\ resulted in the multiple scattering of beam particles, which introduced an angular divergence of $32\,\mu rad$. Scattered beam particles 
    contribute to the backgrounds for the searches for ultra-rare decays, so it was imperative to reduce the amount of material in the beam to control these backgrounds. 
    It was proposed that a CEDAR filled with Hydrogen (\HH) could reduce the material due to the radiator gas's lower mass. Using \HH\ as a radiator gas would have required a pressure of 3.8\,bar 
    to produce a similar Cherenkov angle as \NN\ at 1.71\bar. Hydrogen at this pressure would instead introduce $3.4 \times 10^{-3}\,X_0$ of material, which would result in a $21\,\%$ reduction compared to \NN\, which resulted in a total 
    material of $7.3 \times 10^{-3}\,X_0$. Resulting in an expected angular beam divergence of $13\,\mu rad$ in both the horizontal and vertical dimensions. 

    \newline\indent Taking into account that the four GTk stations contribute $20 \times 10^{-3}\,X_0$, the total beam material along the beam-line reduces from $59 \times 10^{-3}\,X_0$ 
    to $27 \times 10^{-3}\,X_0$. Using the full NA62 simulation based on GEANT4 \cite{Agostinelli2003Geant4}, it was possible to estimate the number of particles interacting inelastically and elastically upstream 
    of the decay volume would reduce from $2.1\,\%$ to $0.9\,\%$. The reduction in scattering would result in higher kaon transmission (number of kaons reaching the
    decay volume) and reduce the signal rates in downstream detectors. %%% May be we can add things about trigger rates but I think we should do a quick study

    \section{CEDAR description}\label{sec:cedar_desc}
    
    \begin{figure}[H]
        \centering 
        \includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth]{imgs/cedar_diagram_jack.pdf}
        \linespread{1.0}

    A CEDAR comprises of a thermally insulated gas vessel separated into two sections: a 4.3\,m long cylinder with an inner diameter of 53\,cm and a vessel cap 
    which can differ in length, depending on the CEDAR variant. The CEDAR-W had a vessel cap of 34\,cm compared to 28\,cm for the CEDAR-N variant and is attached to the main cylinder 
    at the upstream end. The gas vessel is separated from the vacuum of the beam-pipe by two aluminum windows at the upstream and downstream end. A diagram of the CEDAR vessel along with the optics enclosed is depicted in Figure \ref{fig:CEDAR_DIA} 
    
    The beam is designed to pass through the CEDAR vessel along the longitudinal axis, passing through the radiator gas and the aluminum windows. When the beam passed through the vessel Cherenkov at a velocity between,
    \begin{equation}
        \frac{c}{n} < v < c
    \end{equation}
    where $c$ is the speed of light in a vacuum and $n$ is the refractive index of the radiator gas, Cherenkov light is produced.

    %light is produced with an angle dependent on the mass and momentum of the beam particle and is defined as equation \ref{eq:Cherenkov2}.
    %\begin{equation}\label{eq:Cherenkov2}
    %cos(\theta) = \frac{1}{\beta n}

    The CEDAR contains optics inside the vessel connected to supports to allow the reflection and focusing of the produced Cherenkov light, in order to reach the diaphragm at a radius of 100\,mm. 
    The light produced was reflected back upstream by a Mangin mirror \cite{Riedl:74}, a mirror comprising of two spherical surfaces with the downstream lens cemented onto a concave mirror. The 
    mirror was designed to reduce any spherical aberrations, an effect intrinsic to lenses and curved mirrors where the light that strikes the spherical surface off-center is refracted/reflected
    to different points compared to the light that would strike the center of the mirror. After the mirror is a plano-convex lens named the Chromatic Corrector, which is designed in such a way that it corrects for the chromatic dispersion of the radiator gas
    and directs the light towards the diaphragm.
    Chromatic dispersion is where the different wavelengths of light refract differently in a medium, causing the ring to disperse, which would result in issues when the Cherenkov light ring reached the small diaphragm opening. Both the Mangin mirror and the 
    Chromatic corrector have a hole in the center to allow for the beam to pass through without impacting the optics. 
    
    \newline\indent The diaphragm shown in figure \ref{fig:cedarpics}, is a disk with eight separate apertures. Each aperture has an inner and outer element which allows the aperture to open as wide as 
    20\,mm and closed to 0\,mm. These elements can be seen in Figure \ref{fig:cedarpics} (right), where the aperture is set at a small value, used only to select a single Cherenkov ring of radius 100\,mm.  
    The mechanism is operated by a stepper motor connected to the shaft shown in figure \ref{fig:cedarpics} (left), turning the shaft clockwise opens the diaphragm and counter-clockwise closes it. The 
    shaft was connected to a potentiometer that would measure the rotation, which would then be used to convert to an aperture in millimetres. A pair of end switches are in place at the extreme ends of the rotation to alert the user if the aperture 
    is fully open or fully closed, which is essential so as not to cause any damage to the mechanical system.

    The light exits the CEDAR through eight quartz exit windows placed around the beam pipe at the upstream end of the CEDAR, shown in Figure \ref{fig:exit-windows}. Attached to the exit windows are glass filters, designed to 
    attenuate light below 240\,nm to remove light from unwanted particles.

    In the original CEDAR design \cite{Bovet:1982xf}, light exiting the CEDAR through the exit windows were detected by eight ET-9820QB photomultiplier tubes (PMTs), with one attached to each window. 
    A particle was identified when there was a coincidence in at least six PMTs.
    However, this setup would not be applicable for NA62, which requires strict timing requirements on the selection of kaons of less than 100\,ps, a kaon identification efficiency of greater than 95\,\% 
    and a pion miss-identification probability of less than $10^{-4}$. To achieve these strict requirements, a purpose-built photon detection system was designed \cite{GOUDZOVSKI201586} and attached to the 
    upstream end of the CEDAR. The light exiting the quartz windows traversed through optical caps attached to the exit windows, which focused the light onto spherical mirrors instead of directly after the window. The spherical mirrors, one for each exit 
    window reflect the light 90 degrees into eight light boxes and broaden the light to increase the resultant light spot. The spherical mirrors were manufactured from off the shelf optical lenses, then coated in aluminum and are shown in Figure \ref{fig:spherical-mirror}. Each light-box is defined as a sector 
    that will be used from now on. Each sector contains a light guide before a PMT array equipped with 48 Hamamatsu PMTs (32 of type R9880U-110 and 16 of type R7400U-03). 
    The light guide consists of a matrix of cones coated by aluminised mylar to maximise their reflectivity and direct the light onto the active area of each PMT.

    The Cherenkov light from the beam particles is produced with an angle dependent on the mass and momentum of the beam particle and is defined as equation \ref{eq:Cherenkov2}.
    \begin{equation}\label{eq:Cherenkov2}
    cos(\theta) = \frac{1}{\beta n}
    \end{equation}
    with $n$ being the refractive index of the radiator gas and $\beta = p / E$. As the momentum ($p$) is constant in the NA62 beam, at a constant refractive index each particle will produce slightly different Cherenkov angles due to their differing mass. As the CEDAR was designed to allow the light from a specific angle to pass through the 
    diaphragm aperture the refractive index of the gas can be modified by altering the pressure of the radiator gas. As the pressure of the radiator gas is raised the refractive index increases which in resulted in the increase of the Cherenkov angle and the ring radius, allowing a pressure to be
    selected where a kaon at $75\,Gev/c$ produces a ring radius of 100\,mm at the diaphragm, matching the center of the aperture. By selecting a small aperture of less than 2.5\,mm allowed for only the light from the kaon to pass through, with the 
    center of the pion ring being 2\,mm larger and the center of the proton ring being 6\,mm smaller, shown in Figure \ref{fig:WestNitrogen}. Selection of a single particle's Cherenkov ring depends on the beam divergence being less than $100\,\mu m$ and the CEDAR vessel being aligned parallel
    to the beam. The alignment is performed using a pair of motors at the downstream end of the detector, which displaces the downstream end by $\pm 5\,mm$ in both the XY dimension, pivoting the CEDAR vessel around the fixed upstream end. 
    At a temperature of 293\,K, the gas pressure to select kaons at $75\,Gev/c$ was defined at $3.71\,bar$ for \NN\ in CEDAR-W and $3.85\,bar$ for \HH\ in CEDAR-H, with the values fluctuating by 0.34\,\% for every 1\,K of temperature difference.
    The CEDAR vessel was sealed and insulated, allowing it to operate with a fixed amount of gas. It was also insensitive to any small fluctuations in environmental temperature due to the extensive insulation around the pressure vessel.

    \section{Development of CEDAR-H}

    Due to the different chromatic dispersion effects in Hydrogen, it was not possible to use the CEDAR-W using \HH\. From simulations it was apparent in Figure \ref{fig:WestHyrogen}, where the light is distorted 
    when compared to the CEDAR-W with \NN\ shown in Figure \ref{fig:WestNitrogen}. It was not feasible to only select the light of the kaon with the CEDAR diaphragm without sacrificing light yield, which would result in a lower detection efficiency. 
    It was decided that new vessel had to be developed with updated optics designed for use with \HH\ .
    
    An available CEDAR-N vessel was chosen to be used as the CEDAR-H structure, which reduced the costs when most of the mechanical components, such as the diaphragm and mirror supports, were not envisioned to change due to their complexity. This 
    reduced the degrees of freedom for optimisation to two: the radius of curvature of the corrector lens and the two surfaces of the mirror. Initial parameters of the optical components were found using an analytical ray-tracing 
    procedure that considered the point of origin of the Cherenkov light and the wavelength spectrum (REF JACKS THESIS). The parameters were iterated to achieve a ring radius of 100\,mm at the diaphragm and to minimise the divergence of the light. 
    Due to the pressure-dependent effects of the chromatic dispersion and spherical aberration, each computation was completed at pressures between 3.8 to 4.1\,bar in steps of 0.1\,bar.

    Once the initial parameters had been found, a full GEANT4 \cite{Agostinelli2003Geant4} simulation was employed to validate the results from the analytical computation. After some more optimisation, the simulation was then used to modify the optical parameters 
    to maximise the number of photons reaching the KTAG PMTs. The radius of the beam hole of the Mangin mirror was reduced with respect to the original CEDAR design, resulting in a greater reflective surface and increasing the number of 
    photons reaching the PMTs. The spherical mirrors were updated to improve the light distribution on the PMT arrays. It was found that using the condenser lenses from the CEDAR-W compared to the CEDAR-N resulted in more significant photon yield. 

    During the optimization process, it was found that the results showed negligible differences between 3.8 and 4.1\,bar as the increase of Cherenkov photons at high pressure was offset by the improved light distribution on the PMT array at low pressures.
    Due to \HH\ safety considerations, the lower pressure of 3.8\,bar was selected. The optics sufficiently correct the chromatic dispersion at this pressure setting, producing a Gaussian width of 0.4\,mm for the kaon and pion rings. Allowing for the selection of 
    the kaon with a 2\,mm diaphragm, shown in Figure \ref{fig:cedarHsim} with minimal light from the pion passing through.

    the requirements set for operation at NA62.

    \section{Test-beam at CERN}
    \begin{figure}[H]
        \centering 
        \includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth]{imgs/test_beamInstall.png}
        \linespread{1.0}
        \caption{Image of CEDAR-H being installed into H6 beam-line in 2022}

    After construction of the CEDAR-H, the detector was tested and characterised in 2022 at a test beam at the H6 beam-line at CERN, shown in Figure \ref{fig:CEDARHTest}.
    There were three main aims of the test beam: to ensure that the optics had been installed and aligned correctly, to measure the kaon identification probably and the pion miss-identification probability.
    The H6 beam-line is located at CERN's north area and is derived from $400\,GeV/c$ primary protons from the SPS interacting with a target consisting of a beryllium rod, producing an unseparated $75\,GeV/c$ hadron beam similar to NA62. 
    CEDAR-H was installed 440\,m downstream of the target, compared to 70\,m when installed in NA62, resulting in a different beam composition when compared to NA62, with 4\,\% kaons, 25\,\% protons, and 71\,\% pions. 
    The beam divergence at the entrance of the CEDAR was $80\,\mu m$ in the H6 beam-line compared to $70\,\mu m$ in NA62, well within the operating divergence of the CEDAR. 

    In the test beam, an ET 9820QB PMT was installed onto each exit window without the optical caps described previously and operated at a voltage of 2\,kV with a 30\,mV discriminator voltage to achieve single-photoelectron sensitivity. The setup is described in Figure \ref{fig:CEDARHTest_dia} where a pair of scintillating 
    counters were installed at either end of the detector (one downstream and one upstream) to provide a trigger when each beam particle passed through the detector vessel. For a typical spill there were $2.8\times 10^{5}$ particles and for each triggered particle within the spill the six, seven and eight-fold 
    coincidences were recorded in a database for later analysis.

    After the installation, the first step of the test-beam was to conduct an alignment of the CEDAR-H vessel. As described in Section \ref{sec:cedar_desc}, the CEDAR is attached to XY motors which are used to align the vessel to the beam. The diagram in Figure \ref{fig:alignment_dia}
    depicts how moving the CEDAR vessel would have impacted the position of the Cherenkov ring at the diaphragm. The alignment aims to center the ring directly within the diaphragm aperture to maximise the light yield.

    It is possible to quantify the alignment in two ways: to maximise the number of photo-electrons per beam particle and to balance the number of photo-electrons per trigger on each PMT. For the first alignment, the pressure was set at 4.2\,bar and the diaphragm was set at a wide aperture at 19\,mm to 
    allow the light from the kaon, pion and proton to pass through the diaphragm, with the results shown in Figure \ref{fig:alignment_res1}. The motors were moved until the counts on the PMT per trigger were around 100 and were balanced (shown on the right of Figure \ref{fig:alignment_res1}).
    A large aperture allowed for the rough calculation of the PMT efficiency, as a fully efficient detector would expect the number of hits on each PMT to equal the number of triggers. From Figure \ref{fig:alignment_res1}
    it is clear that the PMTs are at least 95\,\% efficiency with the average being 98.3\,\%.

With the hits per PMT for the 2\,mm aperture corresponding to the actual beam composition with a few percent.

    The next stage of the test beam was to conduct a pressure scan. Before the scan, the pressure of the CEDAR-H was set at the highest pressure where there were no selected beam particles. A selected beam particle was defined when there are at least 6 PMTs in coincidence, which was 4.4\,bar.
    The scan consisted of reducing the pressure in small steps and collecting a number of spills of data at each step. This was completed at a diaphragm width of 2.3\,mm, which was slightly larger than the standard operating setting used with the CEDAR-W at NA62. The aperture was chosen to maximise the light yield of the detector to 
    compare the computed beam composition to the expected at the H6 beam line.     The resultant light yield of CEDAR-H can be computed from the 6, 7, and 8 coincidences per trigger shown in Figure \ref{fig:1presscan_testbeam}. The light yield can be computed using either of the two equations below, 
    \begin{equation}\label{eq:Lamb1}
        \lambda= \ln \left[ 1+\frac{8}{\eta_{7}/\eta_{8}-1} \right]
    \end{equation}
    and
    \begin{equation}\label{eq:Lamb2}
        \lambda= \ln \left[ 1 + \frac{14}{ \sqrt{4 - 7(1-\eta_{6}/\eta_{8})}-2} \right]
    \end{equation}
    with $\eta_{N}$ defined as the probability of an N-fold coincidence and $\lambda$ being the number of photo-electrons per PMT. The ratios $\eta_{7}/\eta_{8}$ and $\eta_{6}/\eta_{8}$ are shown in Figure \ref{fig:alignment_res2_ratio}. 
    The two equations \ref{eq:Lamb1} and \ref{eq:Lamb2} were used to cross-check the result and then averaged. At the pion pressure (4.26\,bar), $\eta_{7}/\eta_{8} = 0.856$ and $\eta_{6}/\eta_{8} = 0.550$, which produced an average light yield of $\lambda = 2.372$. 
    The proton yielded slightly less light with $\eta_{7}/\eta_{8} = 0.840$ and $\eta_{6}/\eta_{8} = 0.528$ producing an average yield of $\lambda = 2.293$. At the kaon pressure (3.844\,bar), the ratios of $\eta_{7}/\eta_{8} = 0.869$ and $\eta_{6}/\eta_{8} = 0.574$ producing an average yield of $\lambda = 2.454$ which
    would result in a total of 19.63 photo-electrons per kaon. This was a excellent result, suggesting that the optics had been correctly manufactured and installed, however the diagram was wide with respect to the normal NA62 setting. It is clear that the 2.3\,mm diaphragm could not be used 
    in NA62 due to the un-satisfactory kaon-pion separation seen in Figure \ref{fig:1presscan_testbeam}, where the probability of misidentification of a pion at the kaon pressure was too high. The actual miss-identification will be quantified later on in this section, but it was clear with the peaks of the pion and kaon having no 
    clear separation that the result would of been unsatisfactory.

    \begin{equation}\label{eq:sup-gaus}
        f(X) = a \cdot e^{-\left(\frac{(x - \mu)^2}{2 \sigma^2}\right)^p}
    \end{equation}
    with $a$, $\mu$, $\sigma$ and $p$ being parameters found using a minimised chi squared fit.

A target representing the PMT array, shown in Figure \ref{fig:mirrortarget}, was placed at the correct distance away from the spherical mirror. The laser was placed to simulate the direction of light exiting the CEDAR-H and 
    the spherical mirror was adjusted so that the laser light hit the center of the target, ensuring that the light would hit the center of the PMT array. This process was then repeated for all eight spherical mirrors.

    The mirror support cylinder was then attached to the nose of the CEDAR-H, and the KTAG photon-detection system was reassembled at the upstream end of the vessel and all connections were reconnected and tested.

    Compared to the setup of the CEDAR-W, several modifications were made due to the requirements for use of \HH\ within the ECN3 experimental hall. It was expected that some small amount of gas would leak from the CEDAR-H 
    so all precautions had to be taken in order to prevent the concentration of gas from reaching dab levels, between the Lower Explosive Limit (LEL) of 4\,\% and Upper Explosive Limit of 75\,\% in air. Any area where there was a potential 
    for the \HH\ to leak was designated as an explosive atmosphere (ATEX) zone 2. In the ATEX zones, there are no electrical connectors, and all metal parts of the CEDAR-H are grounded to prevent any ignition sources. 
    During \HH\ operation the diaphragm end switches are manually isolated though a switch located by the KTAG electronics barack, as these are not ATEX rated and have the potential to create a spark. The ATEX zone extended 80\,cm from the CEDAR-H vessel and required that 
    experts had specific training in order to enter.
    Any confined space where the \HH\ can acuminate, such as the KTAG enclosure and the box containing the diagram potentiometer and motor, was flushed with a constant supply of nitrogen to prevent the build-up of \HH. Interlocks were installed to turn off the electronics 
    if this supply of nitrogen was interrupted. A second interlock was in place to turn off the electronics if there was a measured pressure drop in the CEDAR-H vessel. The sensor precision limits the defined pressure drop for the interlock. A small leak was defined to be a measured drop in pressure of 0.02\,mbar per minute 
    measured by a rolling average across an hour. A major leak interlock, which would represent a rupture of the aluminum windows, would be triggered if the pressure dropped by 0.1\,mbar per second. 
    This major leak interlock would cut power to the area, turning off vacuum pumps and the GTk. To avoid the possibility of the window rupture, the CEDAR-H has a safety valve attached the exhaust on top of the CEDAR vessel which limits the pressure in the vessel to 5\,bar. 
    The aluminum windows have been tested to withstand a pressure of 7.5\,bar before installation, so the safety valve would prevent any damage to the windows.

    \section{Commissioning at CERN}
    The commissioning of the cedar in the NA62 beam-line was completed at the start of the 2023 run. After the beam group conducted the start of run activities and the beam was aligned through the CEDAR-H vessel, the beam intensity was set a 10\,\% of NA62 nominal value, corresponding to a particle rate 
    of 60\,Hz. Periodic triggers were used during the commissioning, where a range of time is set between the start of burst (SoB) and End of Burst (EoB) and a period at which data is collected. This period was then set so that $2 \times 10^{5}$ triggers per spill were collected from the data acquisition system, which was dependent on the intensity at the time. 
    For each triggered event, the KTAG data acquisition system recorded the time and the channel ID of each PMT signal within a 100\,ns time window defined by the trigger. The data collected was then reconstructed using the NA62 framework with the individual PMT signals clustered using an algorithm that selects the signals within a 4\,ns time window into candidates. 
    As each individual PMT signal was recorded, the light yield was defined using a different method when compared to the test beam. In the test beam, the ratios of the coincidences were used to calculate the light yield using statistical equations; however, at NA62, the light yield was defined as the number of photo-electrons per beam particle candidate.
    The identification of the beam particle requires a 5-fold coincidence compared to 6-fold used in the test beam due to the availability of data and the reduced noise level with the KTAG photon-detection system.

    As the CEDAR had been removed from H6, then moved to ECN3 and installed into the beam line, it was essential to validate that the detector was still functional using Nitrogen before filling the vessel with Hydrogen. This was because any work that could have been conducted due to any issues caused by the transport to ECN3, would have been complicated by the presence of \HH\ . It was important to note 
    that any results would be preliminary due to the distorted nature of the Cherenkov light in the CEDAR-H with a \NN\ radiator gas. The smaller diagram allowed for a tighter alignment, as the diaphragm aperture was only wide enough for the single ring from the kaon to pass through. 
    The results of the alignment are shown in Figure \ref{fig:cedarAlign2}, where the vessel was moved in both X and Y to produce a 2D map of motor positions and the corresponding average number of photo-electrons per candidate. The 
    best position was found to be at $X = -2.258\,mm$ and $Y = -0.591\,mm$ with an average of $21.68 \pm 0.01$ photo-electrons per kaon candidate.

    \end{figure}
    After the final alignment, a pressure scan was completed across the pion and kaon peak with a diaphragm aperture of 1.5\,mm with the results shown in Figure \ref{fig:cedarH_2}. The separation compared to the previous pressure scan in Figure \ref{fig:cedarH_1} was much greater with the pion contribution to the 5-fold coincidence estimated to be order of $10^-90$ which corresponded to a almost zero miss-identification probability.
    However even after the updated alignment the number of photo-electrons per kaon candidate was $21.47 \pm 0.04$ suggesting a slightly lower light yield than the previous pressure scan completed with a 1.8\,mm diaphragm aperture. 
    
    The results show that the CEDAR-H was made operational in NA62 and was able to separate the kaon and pion peaks with a high level of confidence. It was decided that the pressure scan from the 1.8\,mm diaphragm aperture produced the best results for use in the run due to the superior light yield, resulting in a better kaon identification efficiency and pion miss-identification probability than was better than what was required at NA62.

    \begin{itemize}
        \item Alignment: $X = -2.258\,mm$ and $Y = -0.591\,mm$
        \item Kaon Pressure: 3.88\,bar
        \item Diaphragm Aperture: 1.8\,mm
      \end{itemize}
    The commissioning procedure described above was extensive but was required due to the new detector installation in 2023. At the start of each data taking run, the commissioning must be repeated with alignment and pressure scans to ensure the detector is still operational. Though the detector does not move between years, the beam's position can move compared to the previous years, as well as 
    slight movements of the vessel due to the deformation of the beam-pipe when it is brought in and out of vacuum. Also, it is possible that the pressure set points for the particles can differ due to the environmental changes in ECN3.

    \section{CEDAR-H Performance at NA62} \label{sec:cedarHperformance}
    The performance of the CEDAR-H in standard NA62 run conditions was assessed using data collected in 2023 and compared to CEDAR-W with data collected in 2022. A sample of kaons was collected using a selection of $K^{+}\to\pi^{+}\pi^{+}\pi^{-}$ decays which were fully reconstructed using the STRAW spectrometer, described in Section \ref{sec:STRAW}. The KTAG was used to reconstruct the kaon candidates using 
    the nominal 2\,ns clustering time window. The number of photo-electrons per kaon was measured from events that had at least a five sector coincidence and the results are shown in Figure \ref{fig:NHitsCompare}. The light yield was calculated by fitting a poissonian distribution on the distributions between 4 and 24 photo-electrons. The smaller range is used to remove the contributions from the large number of 
    photo-electrons tails, produced from secondary kaons in coincidence, reconstructed as a single kaon. The average light yield for the CEDAR-H was 20.6 photo-electrons per kaon compared to 18.1 for CEDAR-W.
    \begin{figure}[H]
        \centering
        \includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth]{plots/CEDAR_NHits.pdf}
        \caption{Comparison of the number of photo-electrons per kaon candidate of CEDAR-H in 2023 and CEDAR-W in 2022}%
        \label{fig:NHitsCompare}%
    \end{figure}    
    The time resolution of the KTAG + CEDAR-H was calculated at 66\,ps using the equation,
    \begin{equation}
        \sigma_{t} = \frac{\sigma_{PMT}}{\sqrt{N_{PE}}}
    \end{equation}
    where $\sigma_{PMT}$ is the single photo-electron time resolution of the PMTs of 300\,ps, and $N_{PE}$ is the number of photo-electrons per candidate, compared to 71\,ps for CEDAR-W.
    
    The kaon tagging efficiency was calculated by comparing the number of N-Fold coincidences against the total number of events. The results of the computation is shown in Figure \ref{fig:Effciency}, with CEDAR-H having a kaon identification efficiency of 99.7\,\% compared to 99.5\,\% for CEDAR-W.
    The identification efficiency was compared to the analytical expectation with the photo-electron yield produced from Figure \ref{fig:NHitsCompare}, calculated assuming an ideal detector and uniform photo-electrons across all eight sectors. The results from data showed that the eight sector efficiencies 
    were higher than the expectation due to the contribution from secondary kaons in coincidence. Due to the higher light yield, the experiment had the option of changing the identification classification to a 6-fold coincidence, which provides an identification greater than 95\,\% and would improve the pion miss-identification probability dramatically.

        \caption{Comparison of the number of kaon identification efficiency of CEDAR-H in 2023 and CEDAR-W in 2022 with the expectation for 
        a given photo-electron yield.}%
        \label{fig:Effciency}%
    \end{figure}
