    CEDARs were designed and built at CERN to detect secondary beam particles at the Super Proton Synchrotron (SPS). Two types of CEDAR were developed at CERN:
    CEDAR-W and CEDAR-N, corresponding to the areas of use. The two designs differ in terms of energy range in which they can detect beam particles, with CEDAR-N 
    able to separate kaons and pions up to $300 GeV / c$ but not detect protons below $60 GeV / c$. Whereas CEDAR-W can detect protons down to $12 GeV / c$ 
    and separate kaons and pions up to $150 GeV / c$.

    \newline\indent Described in section \_\_, NA62 has run with a CEDAR-W since 2016 filled with Nitrogen (\NN\) at a pressure of 1.71\,bar (absolute pressure).
    In this configuration, the CEDAR contributed to the most significant amount of material in the path of the beam, introducing $39 \times 10^{-3}\,X_0$ of material, 
    where $X_0$ is one radiation length. The aluminum windows of the CEDAR vessel contribute $3.9 \times 10^{-3}\,X_0$ and the \NN\ gas contributes $35 \times 10^{-3}\,X_0$.
    The material from the \CW\ with \NN\ results in multiple scattering of beam particles, which introduces an angular divergence of $32\,\mu rad$. Scattered beam particles 
    contribute to the backgrounds for the searches for ultra-rare decays, so it is imperative to reduce the amount of material in the beam to control these backgrounds. 
    It was proposed that a CEDAR filled with Hydrogen (\HH) could reduce the material due to the radiator gas. Using \HH\ as a radiator gas would require a pressure of 3.8\,bar 
    to produce a similar Cherenkov angle as \NN at 1.71\bar, which would instead introduce $3.4 \times 10^{-3}\,X_0$ of material. This would result in a $21\,\%$ reduction in total 
    material to $7.3 \times 10^{-3}\,X_0$. Resulting in an anglar beam divergence of $13\,\mu rad$ in both the horizontal and vertical dimensions.

    \section{CEDAR description}\label{sec:cedar_desc}
    
    \begin{figure}[H]
        \centering 
        \includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth]{imgs/cedar_diagram.png}
        \linespread{1.0}

    A CEDAR comprises a thermally insulated gas vessel comprising of two sections: a 4.3\,m long cylinder with an inner diameter of 53\,cm and a vessel cap 
    which can differ in length, depending on the CEDAR variant. The CEDAR-W has a vessel cap of 34\,cm long, with the CEDAR-N's being 28\,cm long and is attached to the main cylinder 
    at the upstream end. The gas vessel is separated from the vacuum of the beam-pipe by two aluminum windows at the upstream and downstream end. 
    \newline\indent The beam is designed to pass through the CEDAR vessel along the longitudinal access, passing through the radiator gas. When the beam passes through the vessel Cherenkov
    light is produced with an angle dependent on the mass and momentum of the beam particle and is defined as equation \ref{eq:Cherenkov2}.
    \begin{equation}\label{eq:Cherenkov2}
        cos(\theta) = \frac{1}{\beta n}
    \end{equation}
    The CEDAR contains optics inside the vessel connected to supports to allow the focusing of the produced Cherenkov light to reach the diaphragm at a radius of 100\,mm. 
    The light produced is reflected back upstream by a Mangin mirror \cite{Riedl:74}, which is a mirror comprising of two spherical surfaces with the dowstream lense cemented onto a concave mirror. The 
    mirror was designed to reduce any spheical aberrations, which is an effect intrinsic to lenses and curved mirrors where the light that strikes the spherical surface off-center is refracted/reflected
    vary compared to the light that strikes the center. After the mirror is a plano-convex lens called the Chromatic Corrector, which is designed in such a way that it corrects for the chromatic dispersion of the radiator gas
    and directs the light towards the diaphragm.
    This is where the different wavelengths of light refract differently in a medium, causing the ring to disperse, which would cause issues when it reaches the small diaphragm opening. Both the Mangin mirror and the 
    Chromatic corrector have a hole in the center to allow for the beam to pass through without impacting the optics. 
    
    \newline\indent The diaphragm shown in figure \ref{fig:cedarpics}, is a disk with eight seperate aperatures. Each aperature has an inner and outer element which allows the aperture to open as wide as 
    20\,mm and closed to 0\,mm. These elements can be seen in Figure \ref{fig:cedarpics} (right), where the aperture is set at a small value only to select a single Cherenkov ring of radius 100\,mm.  
    The mechanism is operated by a stepper motor connected to the shaft shown in figure \ref{fig:cedarpics} (left), where turning the shaft clockwise opens the diaphragm and the opposite closes it. The 
    shaft is also connected to a potentiometer that measures the rotation, which can then be converted to an aperture in millimeters. A pair of end switches are in place to alert the user if the aperture 
    is fully open or fully closed, which is essential so as not to cause any damage to the mechanical system.

    In the original CEDAR design \cite{Bovet:1982xf}, light exiting the CEDAR through the exit windows was detected by eight ET-9820QB photomultiplier tubes (PMTs), with one attached to each window. 
    A particle was identified when there was a coincidence in at least six PMTs.
    However, this setup would not be applicable for NA62, which requires strict timming requirements on the selection of kaons of less than 100\,ps, a kaon identification efficecy of greater than 95\,\% 
    and a pion miss-identification probability of less than $10^{-4}$. To achieve these strict requirements, a purpose-built photon detection system was designed \cite{GOUDZOVSKI201586} and attached to the 
    upstream end of the CEDAR. The light exiting the quartz windows traverses through optical caps attached to the windows, which focus the light onto spherical mirrors. The spherical mirrors, one for each exit 
    window reflect the light 90 degrees into eight light boxes and broaden the light to increase the resultant light spot and are shown in Figure \ref{fig:spherical-mirror}. Each lightbox is defined as a sector 
    that will be used from now on. Each sector contains a light guid before a PMT array equipped with 48 Hamamatsu PMTs (32 of type R9880U-110 and 16 of type R7400U-03). 
    The light guide consists of a matrix of cones coated by aluminised mylar to maximise their reflectivity and direct the light onto the active area of each PMT.

    As mentioned above, the angle of the produced Cherenkov light is defined by equation \ref{eq:Cherenkov2}, which is related to the refractive index and the velocity of the particle. As $\beta$ is constant the 
    radius of the Cherenkov rings is dependent on the density of the gas. As the pressure of the radiator gas is increased the refractive index increases which in turn increases the ring radius, allowing a pressure to be
    selected where a kaon at $75\,Gev/c$ produces a ring radius of 100\,mm at the diaphragm, matching the center of the aperture. Selecting a small aperture, less than 2.5\,mm allows for only the light from the kaon to pass through, with the 
    center of the pion ring being 2\,mm larger and the center of the proton ring being 6\,mm smaller, shown in Figure \ref{fig:WestNitrogen}. Selection of a single particle's Cherenkov ring depends on the beam divergence being less than $100\,\mu m$ and the CEDAR vessel being aligned parallel
    to the beam. The alignment is performed using a pair of motors at the downstream end of the detector, which displaces the downstream end by $\pm 5\,mm$ in both the XY dimension and the fact the CEDAR is fixed at the upstream end. 
    At a temperature of 293\,K, the gas pressure to select kaons at $75\,Gev/c$ is $3.71\,bar$ for \NN\ in CEDAR-W and $3.85\,bar$ for \HH\ in CEDAR-H, with these values fluctuating by 0.34\,\% for every 1\,K of temperature difference.
    The CEDAR vessel is sealed and insulated, allowing it to operate with a fixed amount of gas. It is also insensitive to any small fluctuations in environmental temperature.

    Due to the different chromatic dispersion effects in Hydrogen, it was not possible to use the CEDAR-W using \HH\. This is apparent in Figure \ref{fig:WestHyrogen}, where the light is distorted 
    when compared to the CEDAR-W with \NN\ shown in Figure \ref{fig:WestNitrogen}, where it is not feasible to only select the light of the kaon without sacrificing light yield. It was decided that new vessel would 
    have to be developed with updated optics.
    
    An available CEDAR-N vessel was chosen to be used as the CEDAR-H structure. Most of the mechanical components, such as the diaphragm and mirror supports, were not envisioned to change due to their complexity. This 
    reduced the degrees of freedom for optimisation to two: the radius of curvature of the corrector lense and the two surfaces of the mirror. Initial parameters of the optical components were found using an analytical ray-tracing 
    procedure that considered the point of origin of the Cherenkov light and the wavelength spectrum. The parameters were iterated to achieve a ring radius of 100\,mm at the diapghram and to minimise the divergence of the light. 
    Due to the pressure-dependent effects of the chromatic dispersion and spherical aberration, each computation was completed at pressures between 3.8 to 4.1\,bar in steps of 0.1\,bar.

    Once the inital parameters had been found, a full GEANT4 simulation was employed to validate the results from the analytical computation. After some more optimisation, the simulation was then used to modify the optical parameters 
    to maximise the number of photons reaching the KTAG PMTs. The radius of the beam hole of the Mangin mirror was reduced with respect to the original CEDAR design, resulting in a greater reflective surface and increasing the number of 
    photons reaching the PMTs. The spherical mirrors were updated to improve the light distribution on the PMT arrays. It was found that using the condenser lenses from the CEDAR-W compared to the CEDAR-N resulted in more significant photon yield. 

    During the optimization process, it was found that the results showed negligible differences between 3.8 and 4.1\,bar as the increase of Cherenkov photons at high pressure was offset by the improved light distribution on the PMT array at low pressures.
    Due to \HH\ safety considerations, the lower pressure of 3.8\,bar was selected. The optics sufficiently correct the chromatic dispersion at this pressure setting, producing a Gaussian width of 0.4\,mm for the kaon and pion rings. Allowing for the selection of 
    the kaon with a 2,\mm diaphragm shown in Figure \ref{fig:cedarHsim} with minimal light from the pion passing through.

    \section{Test-beam at CERN}
    \begin{figure}[H]

    After construction of the CEDAR-H, the detector was tested and characterised in 2022 at a test beam at the H6 beam-line at CERN, shown in Figure \ref{fig:CEDARHTest}.
    There were two main aims of the test beam: to ensure that the optics had been installed and aligned correctly, to measure the kaon identification probably and the pion missidentication probability.
    The H6 beamline is located at CERN's north area and is derived from $400\,GeV/c$ primary protons from the SPS interacting with a target consisting of a beryllium rod, producing an unseparated $75\,GeV/c$ hadron beam similar to NA62. 
    CEDAR-H was installed 440\,m downstream of the target, compared to 70\,m when installed in NA62. Resulting in a slightly different beam composition when compared to NA62, with 4\,\% kaons, 25\,\% protons, and 71\,\% pions. 
    The beam divergence at the entrance of the CEDAR was $80\,\mu m$ in the H6 beamline compared to $70\,\mu m$ in NA62, well within the operating divergence of the CEDAR. 

    In the test beam, an ET 9820QB PMT was installed onto each exit window and operated at a voltage of 2\,kV with a 30\,mV discriminator voltage to achieve single-photoelectron sensitivity. The setup is described in Figure \ref{fig:CEDARHTest_dia} where a pair of scintilating 
    counters were installed at either end of the detector (one downstream and one upstream) to provide a trigger when each beam particle passed through the vessel. For a typical spill there were $2.8\times 10^{5}$ particles and for each spill the six, seven and eight-fold 
    coincidences were recorded in a database for later analysis.

    The light yield of CEDAR-H can be computed from the 6, 7, and 8 coincidences per trigger shown in Figure \ref{fig:1presscan_testbeam}. The light yeild can be computed using either of the two equations below, 
    \begin{equation}
        \lambda= \ln \left[ 1+\frac{8}{\eta_{7}/\eta_{8}-1} \right]
    \end{equation}
    and
    \begin{equation}\label{eq:lamb2}
        \lambda= \ln \left[ 1 + \frac{14}{ \sqrt{4 - 7(1-\eta_{6}/\eta_{8})}-2} \right]
    \end{equation}
    with $\eta_{N}$ defined as the probability of an N-fold coincidence and $\lambda$ being the number of photo-electrons per PMT. The ratios $\eta_{7}/\eta_{8}$ and $\eta_{6}/\eta_{8}$ are shown in Figure \ref{fig:alignment_res2_ratio}. 
    The two equations \ref{eq:Lamb1} and \ref{eq:Lamb2} are used to cross-check the result and then averaged. At the pion pressure (4.26\,bar), $\eta_{7}/\eta_{8} = 0.856$ and $\eta_{6}/\eta_{8} = 0.550$ producing an average light yield of $\lambda = 2.372$. 
    The proton yeilded slightly less light with $\eta_{7}/\eta_{8} = 0.840$ and $\eta_{6}/\eta_{8} = 0.528$ producing an average yeild of $\lambda = 2.293$. At the kaon pressure (3.844\,bar), the ratios of $\eta_{7}/\eta_{8} = 0.869$ and $\eta_{6}/\eta_{8} = 0.574$ producing an average yield of $\lambda = 2.454$ which
    would result in a total of 19.63 photo-electrons per kaon. This was a positive sign that the optics had been correctly manufactured and installed, however the diagram was wide with respect to the normal NA62 setting. It is clear that the 2.3\,mm diapgrham could not be used 
    in NA62 due to the un-satisfactory kaon-pion separation seen in Figure \ref{fig:1presscan_testbeam}, where the probability of misidentification of a pion at the kaon pressure is too high.

    Several pressure scans were completed at different diaphragm apertures to select a working point with great light yield and low pion misidentification (pion-kaon separation). The results are shown in Figure \ref{testbeamPresures} and the corresponding ratios and average number of photo-electrons be beam particle is 
    described in Table \ref{tab:testbeam_val}. 
    The pressure scan at 2\,mm is shown in the top left plot of Figure \ref{fig:testbeamPresures}; this provides a similar light yield as the 2.3,\mm but with greater separation between the pion and kaon peaks, however, there is no clear seperation in the six-fold coincidences. This is important as in 
    NA62 was expected to continue the particle selection with at least five sectors, but unfortunately, this was unavailable at the test beam. The top right of \ref{fig:testbeamPresures} was at a similar diaphragm aperture as used at NA62 of 1.7\,mm. Compared to the 2,\mm pressure scan, the light yield decreased only by 1.7\,\% but the 
    separation of the kaon-pion peak has increased dramatically, with the estimated six sector coincidences of the pion peak falling well below $10^{-6}$ at the kaon pressure.

    The final pressure scans at 1.2\,mm and 1\,mm, shown in the lower plots in Figure \ref{fig:testbeamPresures}, show complete separation between the kaon-pion peaks, however it is possible to see in the figure that the light yeild is being reduced at the particles pressures as the peaks no longer have a flat top. This suggests that at 
    1.3,\mm the diagram aperture is narrower than the width of the Cherenkov ring. This is also clear in Table \ref{tab:testbeam_val} where at 1\,mm the average photo-electrons per kaon was 16.03, which would result in less than 95\,\% identification efficiency at five sectors so would not be acceptable for use in NA62.
