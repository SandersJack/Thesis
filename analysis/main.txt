3.1 Overview
The NA62 experiment is a fixed target experiment located in the north area of
CERN with a high energy particle beam provided by the Super Proton Synchrotron
(SPS). The main goal of the experiment is to measure the branching fraction of the
ultra-rare charged kaon decay K+→π+ν¯ν. Alongside there is a expansive physics
program of precision and rare decay measurements as well as searches for forbidden
processes and exotic particles.
The experiment employs a decay-in-flight technique to obtain a large number of
charged kaon decays, in the order of 1013within the short time frame of a few years.
The experimental setup was designed to fulfil all the requirements for the challenging
measurement of K+→π+ν¯ν, which involves the detection of the K+upstream and
the detection of the product π+, with a missing energy-momentum carried away by
the undetected neutrino-antineutrino pair. The measurement of the missing mass
is used for kinematic rejection of the main background K+decays; K+→π+π0,
K+→π+π−andK+→µ+νµby selecting two regions shown in figure 3.1. The
missing mass calculation requires precise timing and position measurement of the
K+andπ+candidates, provided by beam (section 3.3.2) and secondary (section
3.3.4) particle spectrometers. The measured 3-momenta of the candidates allows for
the reconstruction of the 4-momenta of the Kaon PK+and the pion Pπ+which is

3.2 The NA62 Beam Line
The NA62 experiment at CERN uses a hadron beam extracted from the SPS with
a nominal momentum of 400 GeV /c, directed onto a 400 mm long, 2 mm diameter
beryllium rod that forms the fixed target (T10) located at 0 m in figure 3.3. The
beam derived from the T10 target, referred to as K12, is a high-intensity unsepa-
rated hadron beam with a nominal momentum of 75 GeV/c . The beam momenta
was chosen to maximise the fraction of kaons with respect to the other beam com-
ponents at the entrance to the detector.

3.3.5 Ring Imaging Cherenkov counter (RICH)

and muons within a momentum range of 15 - 35 GeV /c. The ability to separate the

continues through the 4 mm thick, 1.4 m radius aluminum exit window. The vessel

3.4 Trigger and Data Acquisition System (TDAQ)
The high intensity of the NA62 experiment requires a triggering system that can
limit the eventual data that is stored while keeping useful data to satisfy the ex-
periments physics goals. The NA62 trigger system is comprised of two stages: a
hardware trigger (L0) and a software trigger (L1) which is also known as the high
level trigger (HLT). Each stage of the trigger system is designed to reduce the in-
coming rate by a factor of 10. With a rate of O(10 MHz) kaon decays within the
fiducial volume the L0 was designed to reduce this to O(1 MHz) and the L1 then
reducing the rate written to storage to O(100 kHz).

Differential Cherenkov counters with achromatic ring focus (CEDAR) were designed
and built at CERN to detect secondary beam particles at the Super Proton Syn-
chrotron (SPS) [2]. Two types of CEDAR were developed at CERN: CEDAR-West
(CEDAR-W) and CEDAR-North (CEDAR-N), corresponding to the areas of use.
The two designs differ in terms of the energy range in which they can detect beam
particles, with CEDAR-N able to separate kaons and pions up to 300 GeV /c but
not detect protons below 60 GeV /c. Whereas CEDAR-W can detect protons down
to 12 GeV/c and separate kaons and pions up to 150 GeV /c.
As described in Section 3, since 2016 NA62 has utilised a CEDAR-W filled with
Nitrogen (N 2) at a nominal gas pressure of 1.71 bar (absolute pressure). In this
configuration, the CEDAR contributed to the most significant amount of material
in the path of the beam, corresponding to 39 ×10−3X0of material, where X0is
one radiation length. The CEDAR vessel contributed 3 .9×10−3X0and the N 2gas
contributed 35 ×10−3X0. From simulations, the material from the CEDAR-W with
N2resulted in the multiple scattering of beam particles, introducing an angular
divergence in the beam of 32 µrad. Scattered beam particles contribute to the back-
grounds for the searches for ultra-rare decays. In order to reduce these backgrounds,
it was possible to minimise the amount of material in the path of the beam. It was
proposed that a CEDAR filled with Hydrogen (H 2) could reduce the material due to
the radiator gas’s lower mass. Using H 2as a radiator gas would require a pressure
of 3.8 bar to produce a similar Cherenkov angle as N 2gas at 1 .71 bar. Hydrogen at
this pressure would introduce 3 .4×10−3X0of material, which would result in a

factor of five reduction in material compared to N 2, resulting in a total material
of 7.3×10−3X0. The expected angular beam divergence is 13 µrad in both the
horizontal and vertical dimensions.
Taking into account that the four GTK stations contribute 20 ×10−3X0, the
total beam material along the beam-line reduces from 59 ×10−3X0to 27×10−3X0.
Using the full NA62 simulation based on GEANT4 [12], it was possible to estimate
that the number of particles interacting inelastically and elastically upstream of the
decay volume would reduce from 2 .1 % to 0 .9 %. The reduction in scattering would
result in higher kaon transmission (number of kaons reaching the decay volume) and
reduced signal rates in the downstream detectors.

mirror. The mirror is designed to reduce spherical aberrations, an effect intrinsic
to lenses and curved mirrors where the light that strikes the spherical surface off-
centre is refracted/reflected to different points compared to the light that would
strike the centre of the mirror. After the mirror, the light goes through a plano-
convex lens named the Chromatic Corrector, which is designed in such a way that it
corrects for the chromatic dispersion of the radiator gas and directs the light towards
the diaphragm. Chromatic dispersion happens because different wavelengths of light
refract differently in a medium, causing the Cherenkov ring to disperse, which would
result in some of the light being lost when it reaches the small diaphragm opening.
Both the Mangin mirror and the Chromatic corrector have a hole in the centre to
allow for the beam to pass through without impacting the optics.

Figure 4.22: Image of CEDAR-H being installed on the NA62 beam-line in 2022
CEDAR-H was installed on the NA62 beam-line in 2023, involving several steps.
First, the KTAG photon-detection system had to be disassembled from the CEDAR-
W and set to one side, with the CEDAR-W vessel then removed from the beam-line.
The CEDAR-H was brought to the NA62 underground cavern and installed into the
beam-line, shown in Figure 4.22. The new spherical mirrors were aligned with a
laser beam and secured to a support cylinder, shown in Figure 4.23. The process
of alignment started with the positioning of the laser. Data from the NA62 Monte
Carlo simulation provided info about the direction of light exiting the CEDAR-H
vessel. A photon that hit the centre of the spherical mirror was selected, and the
positions at the exit window and spherical mirror were recorded in XYZ. The vector
was then calculated from the mirror’s centre to the exit window and used to set up
the laser for the mirror alignment. A target representing the PMT array, shown in
Figure 4.24, was placed at the correct distance from the spherical mirror. The laser
was placed to simulate the direction of light exiting the CEDAR-H and the spherical
mirror was adjusted so that the laser light hit the centre of the target, ensuring that
the light would hit the centre of the PMT array. This process was then repeated for
all eight spherical mirrors.

The mirror support cylinder was then attached to the nose of the CEDAR-H, the
KTAG photon-detection system was reassembled at the upstream end of the vessel,

Compared to the setup of the CEDAR-W, several modifications were made due
to the requirements for the use of H 2within the ECN3 experimental hall. It was
expected that some negligible amount of gas would leak from the CEDAR-H , so
all precautions had to be taken in order to prevent the concentration of gas from
reaching dangerous levels between the Lower Explosive Limit (LEL) of 4 % and
Upper Explosive Limit of 75 % in air. Any area where there was a potential for a
H2leak was designated as an explosive atmosphere (ATEX) zone 2. In the ATEX
zones, there are no electrical connectors, and all metal parts of the CEDAR-H are
694.4 Installation at NA62 CEDARH
grounded to prevent any ignition sources. During H 2operation, the diaphragm end
switches are manually isolated through a key located by the KTAG electronics rack,
as these are not ATEX rated and have the potential to create a spark. The ATEX
zones extended 80 cm from the CEDAR-H vessel and required experts had to have
ATEX training in order to access. Any confined space where the H 2can acuminate,
such as the KTAG enclosure and the box containing the diaphragm potentiometer
and motor, was flushed with a constant supply of nitrogen to prevent the build-
up of H 2. Safety interlocks were installed to turn off the KTAG electronics in the
event the supply of N 2is interrupted. A second interlock was in place to turn off
the KTAG electronics if there was a sizeable pressure drop in the CEDAR-H vessel.
The pressure sensor precision limits the defined pressure drop for the interlock. A
small leak was defined to be a measured drop in pressure of 0.02 mbar per minute
measured by a rolling average across an hour. A major leak interlock, which would
represent a rupture of the aluminium windows, would be triggered if the pressure
dropped by 0.1 mbar per second. This major leak interlock would cut power to the
CEDAR and neighbouring areas, turning off vacuum pumps and the GTK. To avoid
the possibility of the window rupture, the CEDAR-H has a safety valve attached to
the exhaust on top of a CEDAR vessel, which limits the pressure in the vessel to
a maximum value of 5 bar. The aluminium windows have been tested to withstand
a pressure of 7.5 bar before installation so that the safety valve would prevent any
damage to the windows.

4.5 Commissioning at CERN
The commissioning of the CEDAR-Hin the NA62 beam-line was completed at the
start of the 2023 run. After the CERN beam experts conducted the start-of-run
activities and the beam was steered through the CEDAR-H vessel, the beam inten-
sity was set at 10 % of the NA62 nominal value, corresponding to a particle rate
of 60 Hz. Periodic triggers were used during the CEDAR-Hcommissioning in or-
der to select all beam particles and measure the beam composition. The periodic
triggers are where a period is selected between the start of burst (SoB) and end of
burst (EoB) at which data was collected. At the beam intensity selected, the period
was set so that 2 ×105triggers per spill was collected. For each triggered event,
the KTAG data acquisition system recorded the time and the channel ID of each
PMT signal within a 100 ns time window defined by the trigger. The data collected
was then reconstructed using the NA62 framework with the individual PMT signals
clustered using an algorithm that selects the signals within a 4 ns time window into
candidates. As each individual PMT signal was recorded, the light yield was de-
fined using a different method when compared to the test beam. In the test beam,
the ratios of the coincidences were used to calculate the light yield using statistical
equations. However, at NA62, the light yield can be measured precisely due to the
high granularity of the KTAG photon-detection system and can be defined as the
number of photo-electrons per beam particle candidate. The identification of the
beam particle utilised a 5-fold coincidence, compared to the 6-fold used in the test
beam, due to the availability of data and the reduced noise level with the KTAG
photon-detection system.

Due to the changes to the working point pressure between the test beam and
between the 2023 and 2024 runs, a new method was developed to monitor the
CEDAR-H and to take into account the environmental variables to ensure the re-
fractive index of the radiator gas did not change by large amounts which would
result in lower kaon detection efficiency. The pressure of the vessel and temperature
at both the front and back of the CEDAR were recorded and shown in Figure 4.34
for the 2023 run and Figure 4.35 for the 2024 run. The pressure was divided by tem-
perature, as the ratio should be consistent for a sealed vessel like the CEDAR, and
814.5 Commissioning at CERN CEDARH
the ratio was multiplied by a room temperature of 293.15 K, resulting in the pres-
sure at normalised room temperature. During previous runs at NA62, the CEDAR
would be kept at a fixed pressure and regulated to stay at the desired pressure,
which would be correct if the CEDAR vessel did not change in temperature. If the
temperature of the gas changed with the pressure regulated to a fixed value, the
resultant refractive index of the radiator gas would diverge from the desired value,
reducing the detector efficiency. Even though the CEDAR is extensively insulated,
it is designed to mitigate sharp temperature changes. It does not stop the CEDAR
vessel from slowly changing temperature with the environment due to seasons. To
mitigate this, the CEDAR was sealed with a fixed amount of gas and the pressure
was allowed to vary. A good example of how this new method was utilised was in
the final days of the 2023 run in Figure 4.34 where the actual pressure increased
by nearly 10 mbar, whereas the normalised pressures stayed constant, suggesting
that the temperature in ECN3 increased, reiterating that the pressure should not
be automatically maintained at a specific pressure. However, as CEDAR-H was not
perfectly sealed, especially for Hydrogen, a small leak was expected, so there was a
gradual decrease in the normalised pressure. The normalised pressure and the re-
sultant number of photo-electrons per candidate were used to decide if the CEDAR
required extra filling during the run due to the small loss of gas, which can be seen
in Figures 4.34 and 4.34 as the large spikes in pressure at various points.

Contributions to NA62 Framework
The software for NA62 is split into three main C++ packages: NA62MC,
NA62Reconstruction and NA62Analysis. NA62MC is a GEANT4 based Monte
Carlo simulation package that simulates the NA62 experiment including the de-
tector geometry and the full simulation of kaon decays, used as a comparison to the
data collected by the experiment. NA62Reconstruction is a package that is used to
reconstruct either the Monte Carlo data or collected data from the experiment for
all detectors. NA62Analysis is used by the physicists to analyse the data produced
from the reconstruction to produce all the physics studies that are conducted at
NA62.
The NA62 software was designed for use only with the NA62 experiment resulting
in in minimal configurability and flexibility, with the only option for users to change
the software being to enable/disable sub-detectors. With the experiment forseen to
run up to CERNs Long shutdown 3 (LS3), an upgraded software package was needed
to allow for the development of new detectors for the future kaon experiments. This
software had to be flexible and configurable to allow for the simulatinous use of the
software for NA62 data taking and physics analysis, as well as the development of
new experiements.

Future Kaon Experiments
Over the past 75 years the study of kaon physics has been integral to the develop-
ment of the standard model. Since 2016, the NA62 experiment has been the leading
experiment in the field of kaon physics. However, with the NA62 experiment sched-
uled up to CERN Long Shutdown 3 (LS3), which is expected in autumn 2026, it
is important to investigate future experiments. It was then required to investigate
the future of kaon physics and the potential experiments that could succeed NA62.
This chapter will discuss the potential future of kaon physics, the experiments that
could be used to further the field, and the work I contributed to these experiments.
6.1 High Intensity Kaon Experiments (HIKE)
The High-intensity kaon experiments (HIKE) was a proposed project to continue
kaon physics at CERN in the ECN3 experimental hall and represented a long-phased
plan after LS3. HIKE would have profited from a four to six-fold increase in beam
intensity compared to NA62 and included cutting-edge detector technologies that
will be described in this section. The beam intensity requirements for HIKE are
described in Table 6.1. To accommodate the higher beam intensities, upgrades
were needed to the beam-lines that provided the beam to ECN3. A number of work
groups such as the ECN3 beam delivery group, Physics Beyond Colliders was already
conducting efforts to consolidate all the work required to upgrade infrastructure.
There were three phases within the HIKE program, the first being a continuation of
NA62’s charged kaon physics with the high-intensity beam and detector upgrades

6.2 KTAG Photon Detection System Upgrade
To sustain the increased kaon rate, the KTAG required upgrades to the photon-
detection system and data acquisition system, with an expected time resolution
of 15 - 20 ps and a kaon identification efficiency of greater than 95 %. The ex-
pected kaon rate was 200 MHz which corresponds to a maximum particle rate of
10 MHz/c m2for the photons at the PMT array. This took into account the pro-
duction of Cherenkov photons in CEDAR-H and the resultant acceptance of the
photons on the PMT plane. It was proposed that the NA62 PMT arrays would be
replaced with arrays of micro-channel plate photomultipliers (MCP-PMTs). The
MCP-PMTs would provide a single photon time resolution of 50 - 70 ps depending
on the model, a low dark rate of 1 kHz/c m2and the ability to sustain the required
photon rate. A downside of the MCP-PMTs is their limited lifetime from the ageing
of the Photo-Cathode caused by heavy feedback ions from the residual gas. This
results in the rapid degradation of PMT quantum efficiency with the increasing in-
tegrated anode charge. MCP-PMTs are similar to normal PMTs except that the
dynode section is replaced by a micro-channel plate. An example diagram is shown
in Figure ??, where a photon hits the photocathode window, producing a single
electron, which is then accelerated towards the MCP by an electric field provided
by a voltage difference across the gap. The electron then collides with the walls of
the MCP, producing secondary electrons, which results in a cascade which is then
collected by the anodes below. The duel layer design, or chevron, adds additional
benefits compared to a single layer design: better gains due to the increased surface
for secondary electrons to be produced and improved lifetime due to the suppression
of ions produced in the amplification from reaching the photocathode.

6.3 RICH Upgrade
The overall design of the NA62 RICH described in section 3.3.5 was well suited for
re-use within HIKE with an upgrade to the photon-detection system. The NA62
RICH photon detection system comprises two arrays on either side of the beam pipe,
equipped with Hamamatsu R7400-U03 PMTs placed in a pattern described in Figure
6.11. The PMTs have a single hit time resolution of 240 ps and a quantum efficiency
of 20 %. Due to the placement of the PMTs, the distance between the centre of
each PMT is 18 mm, which is constrained by the sensor size and the geometry of the
PMT itself. This distance between sensors was the main contributor to the single
hit position resolution of 4.7 mm, resulting in an overall ring radius resolution of
1.5 mm. The number of hits per ring candidate and the single hit time resolution
produced a time resolution of 80 ps. Due to the increased beam intensity, the main
requirement of the RICH for HIKE was a time resolution of 20 - 30 ps.

After the new GTk and RICH detectors were developed, it was necessary to deter-
mine the expected particle rate in the upgraded and non-upgraded detectors. The
upgraded STRAW detector utilising 5 mm diameter straws was also included in the
HIKE simulations. It was possible to estimate the expected rate for Phase 1 by
scaling the known NA62 rates by the expected increase in beam intensity by a fac-
tor of 4 compared to NA62. The rates for Phase 2 were calculated using the new
simulation software described in the previous sections.
An array of input files were produced in BDSIM [29] by the CERN beam group
which contained the simulated HIKE beam components at a Z of 120 m, which
was the start of the fiducial volume. The files were then used as an input for the
NA62MC software and used as the primary beam instead of the turtle beam, which
is the default for NA62. Each file contained 10000 events, equaling 10000 protons on
target, and there were 18K files produced in total, which was equivalent to 1 .8×108
protons on target. As this wouldn’t be enough to get a reasonable estimate of the
rates, each file was used 11 times as an input to the simulation with random seeds to
increase the statistics. The simulation was run with the unforced flag which allows
for four main KLdecays: KL→π±e∓νe(K0
e3),KL→π±µ∓νµ(K0
µ3),KL→π0π0π0
andKL→π+π−π0. After the simulation, the files were then reconstructed using
the NA62 Reconstruction software, and the rates were calculated by scaling the
numbers in the output by the expected HIKE Phase 2 beam conditions, a spill time
of 4.8 seconds and a beam intensity of 2 ×1013protons per spill.

expwas computed from the propaga-
