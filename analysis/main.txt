iiAbstract
The 2022-24 NA62 data has been used to search for the dark photon ( A′) from the
π0→A′γand the subsequent prompt decay A′→e+e−. Within the mass range
s 6≤mA′≤121.6 MeV /c2no signal was observed and an exclusion of the mixing
parameter ϵ2across the mass range is obtained.
The NA62 beam consists of only 6 % kaons which require identification which is
conducted by the KTAG. The KTAG consists of a Differential Cherenkov counter
with Achromatic Ring focus (CEDAR) and a purpose built photon-detection sys-
tem. Since 2016 NA62 has employed a CEDAR with a Nitrogen gas (N 2) radiator
gas. To reduce the amount of material in the path of the beam a new CEDAR
utilising Hydrogen gas (H 2) as a radiator gas can be used. Its design, testing and
commissioning at NA62 is presented.Acknowledgements

iDeclaration of author’s
contributions
Chapter 1 sets out the theoretical framework for the thesis. Topics include the
background on the Standard Model and kaon physics to provide context for the
NA62 experiment and future kaon experiments. The Chapter also include theoretical
foundations for the Dark Photon. The NA62 experiment is described in Chapter
2 with descriptions of the sub-detectors and the latest result for the K+→π+ν¯ν
decay. During the periods 2021-2024 I completed data taking shifts as well as expert
shifts for the KTAG and HLT to assist with the collection of data.
Chapter 3 describes the development and commissioning of the CEDAR-H de-
tector at CERN. Section 3.1 describes the theoretical background of the CEDAR
as well as the basic construction of the vessel, of which is not my own. The design
of the CEDAR-H is described in Section 3.1 which was completed prior to my in-
volvement. The characterisation of the optical elements was completed by CERN
engineers but was consolidated by myself and documented. The simulation data
required for the alignment of the spherical mirror was completed by me. I was in-
volved in the test-beam in Section 3.3 by completing a number of shifts through out
the period. I conducted the analysis of the data from the test-beam shown in this
Section. I was also involved in the installation described in Section 3.4 which was
conducted at the start of my Long Term Attachment in 2023. I was also involved
in the commissioning in 2023 described in Section 3.5 and produced all the data
analysis shown in this Section. The commissioning in 2024 was also completed by
myself and other members of the Birmingham group. I was also involved in the
monitoring of the KTAG performance in the periods 2021-2024 by conducing shifts
as the detector expert and is briefly described in Section 3.5
iiThe search of the Dark Photon described in Chapter 4 is entirely my work except
for the results referenced from other experiments.
Chapter 5 set outs the possibility for future kaon experiments. Section 5.1 de-
scribes the aims for a High Intensity Kaon Experiments (HIKE) and the planned
detector setups for three separate phases. Section 5.2 describes my contributions to
the NA62 software were I developed and tested new flexible features to allow the de-
velopment of new detectors. Section 5.3 follow on with development of an upgraded
KTAG which I helped implement into the new software framework. In Section 5.4
the upgraded RICH is described which I completed from design to implementation
in the software framework. Section 5.5 provides the detector rates for HIKE Phase
2 which I completed.
No other qualifications were obtained from the work completed within this thesis.

xxIntroduction
The understanding of the fundamental components and interactions of the universe
has cumulated in to the formation of the Standard Model (SM). The SM stands out
as one of the most successful models nature produced by science. However, it does
not encapsulate all the observations of the universe such as neutrino masses, dark
matter, matter-antimatter asymmetry and the fourth fundamental force of gravity.
The aim of most modern particle physics experiments is to test the SM as pre-
cisely as possible and to search for new physics Beyond the Standard Model (BSM).
To methods of experimental methods are employed to reach these aims. One is to
reach ever increasing energies to produce and observer new heavier particles. An
example of this method are the experiments at the Large Hadron Collider at CERN
such as ATLAS and CMS with the successful discovery of the Higgs boson in 2012.
The second method involves collecting large data sets allowing for the search for rare
processes where any deviations from the SM can provide evidence of BSM physics.
An example of this method is the search for K+→πν¯νby NA62 at CERNs Super
Proton Synchrotron (SPS) with its goal to measure the branching ratio which is
precisely predicted by the SM.
A more detailed description of the SM and then the involvement of kaon physics
can be found in Chapter 1.
The work completed within this thesis was undertaken within the NA62 collab-
oration. This thesis includes the theoretical foundations for the work completed
throughout found in Chapter 1. Chapter 2 describes the NA62 experimental setup
and the latest results published by the collaboration for the search for K+→πν¯ν.
Chapter 3 describes the development of a new CEDAR with a hydrogen radiator
gas for use within the NA62 kaon identification system. A analysis project for the
search for the DP at NA62 is set out in Chapter 4 where two methods are presented.

1.1 Standard Model of Particle Physics
The SM of particle physics is a framework which contains our current understanding
of the matter and three of the four four forces found in the universe. Within the SM
there are 12 elementary fermions, 5 vector bosons which mediate the three funda-
mental forces and a scalar boson responsible for particle masses (Figure 1.1). It is
important to note that the SM is not a complete model of the universe. A great ex-
ample of this is the gravitational force which is not described by the theory, however
at the small scales of of particle physics this force is neglected due to it’s insignifi-
cant strength when compared to the other three forces (strong, electromagnetic and
weak).

1.2 Kaon Physics
The first possible observation a heavy meson was in 1943 by Leprince-Ringuet and
L’heritier [7, 8] and predated even the discovery of the pion in 1947 [9]. Utilising
a cloud chamber to measure cosmic rays they found the first evidence of a particle
with a mass of 990 times the mass of the electron (0 .511 MeV [10]). However, it was
possible that the upper limit on the measured mass could reach the proton mass so
they were not credited with the discovery. In 1947 while similarly studying cosmic
rays with cloud chambers G. D. Rochester and C. C. Butler published a result which
included two pictures showing forked tracks [11]. One of the tracks proved to show
the decay of a neutral particle into two charged daughter particles, which turned
out to be decay of the neutral kaon to two pions. The other track showed the decay
of a charged particle into charged and neutral daughter particles. This result was
later confirmed in 1950 by a group in Cal-Tech who observed multiple instances of
these decays [12]. However, between these publications a group in Bristol which
included physicist Rosemary Brown, used emulsion chambers to observe the decay
of a charged particle into three charged particles [13], known then as the tau decay.
This resulted in what was known as the tau-theta problem, where the seemingly
the same particle was decaying into two different final states with different parities:
Theta to two pions (+1) and Tau to three pions (-1). This eventually led to the
discovery that parity is not conserved in weak interactions [14].
In the early 1950s these particles would be described as K-mesons, for which
there are four types ( K+,K−,K0and ¯K0). Experiments continued to measure
the characteristics of the K mesons and found features such as long lifetimes (10−10
seconds) when compared to the fact there production was much faster [15]. These
deviations from the other mesons, mainly the pion suggested the existence of a
new quantum number named strangeness [16, 17]. The strange quantum number
applies to all strongly interacting particle and is conserved in strong interactions
but violated in weak interactions.
Into the latter 1950s and early 1960s the neutral kaon became the center of
number of studies. Despite the fact that parity is violated in weak interactions
it was still believed that the combination of CP would be conserved. Through
experiment the two weak eigenstates of the neutral kaon showed to have different
81.2 Theoretical foundations Kaon Physics
lifetimes, which resulted in their names KLandKS[18]. Each of these eigenstates
had different CP values with the KSwith CP= +1 decaying to two pions and
theKLwith CP=−1 observed decaying to three pions. However, in 1964 it was
observed that the KLdecayed to two pions [19] suggesting that CP was indeed
violated in weak decays. This decay is an example of indirect CP violation as it
occurs through neural kaon mixing. The first evidence of direct CP violation, where
the violation occurs within the decay was provided by the NA31 experiment in 1993
[20]. The process was eventually discovered by KTeV and NA48 collaborations in
the latter 1990s [21, 22].

2.1 Overview
The NA62 experiment is a fixed target experiment located in the ECN3 experimen-
tal hall in the North Area of CERN where a 400 GeV /cparticle beam is provided
by the SPS. The main goal of the experiment is to measure the branching fraction
of the ultra-rare charged kaon decay K+→π+ν¯ν. Alongside, there is an expansive
physics program of precision and rare decay measurements, as well as searches for
forbidden processes and exotic particles.
The experiment employs a decay-in-flight technique to obtain a large number
of charged kaon decays, in the order of 1013within the time frame of a few years.
The experimental setup was designed to fulfil the requirements for the challenging
measurement of K+→π+ν¯ν, which involves the detection of the K+upstream and
the detection of the product π+, with a missing energy-momentum carried away by
the undetected neutrino-antineutrino pair. The missing mass calculation, defined
by equation 2.1, requires precise spacial measurement of the K+andπ+candidates,
provided by the beam (section 2.3.2) and secondary (section 2.3.4) particle spec-
trometers. The measurement of the missing mass is used for kinematic rejection of
the main background K+decays; K+→π+π0,K+→π+π+π−andK+→µ+νµby
selecting two regions shown in figure 2.1. The measured 3-momenta of the candi-

2.2 The NA62 Beam-Line
The NA62 experiment at CERN uses a hadron beam extracted from the SPS with
a nominal momentum of 400 GeV /c, directed onto a 400 mm long, 2 mm diameter
beryllium rod that forms the fixed target (T10) located at 0 m in Figure 2.2. The
beam derived from the T10 target, referred to as K12, is a high-intensity unsepa-
rated hadron beam with a nominal momentum of 75 GeV /c. The beam momentum
was chosen to maximise the fraction of kaons decaying within the FV with respect
to the other beam components.
The elements of the K12 beam-line are shown in Figure 2.2 and comprise a num-
ber of components. First are three quadrupole magnets (Q1, Q2 and Q3), used to

focus the beam with a large solid angle acceptance of ±2.7 mrad horizontally and
±1.5 mrad vertically at a 75 GeV /ccentral momentum. Next follows an achromat
(A1), which selects a beam of a 75 GeV /cmomentum with a 1 % rms momentum.
The A1 achromat comprises four dipole magnets that vertically deflect the beam,
the first two deflect the beam 110 mm downwards, while the final two deflect the
beam upwards towards the original axis. Between these pairs of magnets, there are
a pair of water-cooled beam-dump units (TAX1 and TAX2) with a set of holes to
select the required 75 GeV /cmomentum beam while absorbing unwanted secondary
beam particles and any remaining primary protons. TAX1&2 can be closed to act
as a secondary target for use in dump mode or for safety when access is required
downstream. Between the TAX1&2, a radiator consisting of tungsten plates with a
thickness of up to 5 mm is introduced into the path of the beam. The radiator is
optimised to reduce positron energy while minimising the loss of energy to hadrons.
A triplet of quadrupoles (Q4, Q5, Q6) follows, which refocuses the beam in the
vertical direction and produces a parallel beam in the horizontal direction. The
space between the quadrupoles is occupied by a pair of collimators (C1, C2), which
redefines the horizontal and vertical acceptance of the beam. The collimator after
the Q6 quadrupole (C3) is employed to absorb positrons that had their momenta
degraded by the radiator and again redefines the acceptance in the vertical direc-
tion.
The beam next passes through a 40 mm field-free hole in a set of iron plates,
placed between three 2 m dipole magnets (B3). The vertical magnetic field in the
iron plates is used to deflect muons. Any deviation in the beam due to stray fields
in the beam hole is cancelled out by a pair of steering dipoles (TRIM2 and TRIM3).
Two quadrupoles (Q7, Q8) follow and ensure that the beam is parallel before the
KTAG, which comprises of a CEDAR and a purpose-built photon detection system,
described in Section 2.3.1. Before the KTAG, a pair of collimators (C4 and C5) are
used to absorb particles in the tails of the spacial distribution of the beam.
For beam monitoring, two pairs of filament scintillator counters (FISC 1, 3 and
FISC 2, 4) are installed at either end of the KTAG. Used in coincidence the FISCs
can measure the beam divergence which is tuned to ensure the condition of paral-
lelism is satisfied through the CEDAR vessel.

2.3.5 Ring Imaging Cherenkov counter (RICH)
The RICH is designed to separate secondary pion and muons within a momentum
range of 15 - 35 GeV /cas required by the necessity to suppress the K+→µ+νµ

diameters decreasing from 4.2 m at the upstream section to accommodate the PMT

at the upstream end, allowing minimal material in the path of particles entering
the detector. A lightweight aluminium beam pipe connected to the vacuum tube

window. The vessel contains Neon gas at atmospheric pressure with a refractive in-

2.4 Trigger and Data Acquisition System (TDAQ)
The high intensity of the beam necessitates a triggering system capable of reduc-

physics objectives. The NA62 trigger system is comprised of two stages: a hardware

trigger (L0) and a software trigger (L1), known as the high-level trigger (HLT).

was designed to reduce this to O(1MHz ) and the L1 to reduce the rate written to
storage to O(100kHz).
To fit with NA62 broad physics goals, the trigger system is designed to be flexible.

CEDARs were designed and built at CERN to detect secondary beam particles at

As described in Section 2, since 2016 NA62 has utilised a CEDAR-W filled with

lost when it reaches the small diaphragm opening. Both the Mangin mirror and the
Chromatic corrector have a hole in the centre to allow for the beam to pass through

Simulations of the CEDAR-W filled with H 2radiator gas show that the correction

updated optics optimised for use with H 2. The vessel was called CEDAR-H.
An available CEDAR-N vessel was chosen to be used as the CEDAR-H struc-

diaphragm and mirror supports, were not envisioned to change due to their com-

of curvature of the chromatic corrector lens and the two surfaces of the Mangin
mirror. Initial parameters of the optical components were found using an analytical

ray-tracing procedure that considered the point of origin of the Cherenkov light and
the wavelength spectrum [60, 61]. The parameters were iterated to achieve a ring
radius of 100 mm at the diaphragm and to minimise the width of the ring at the
diaphragm. Due to the pressure-dependent effects of the chromatic dispersion and
spherical aberration, each computation was completed at pressures between 3.8 to
4.1 bar in steps of 0.1 bar.
Once the initial parameters had been found, a full GEANT4 [57] simulation was
employed to validate and optimise the results from the analytical computation. The
GEANT4 simulation allowed to further trace the light coming out of the CEDAR
vessel through the diaphragm aperture , and then reflected by the spherical mirrors
onto the PMT arrays, as shown by the light path in Figure 3.1. The simulation
was then used to modify the optical parameters to maximise the number of photons
reaching the KTAG PMTs. The radius of the beam hole of the Mangin mirror was
reduced with respect to the original CEDAR design, resulting in a greater reflective
surface and increasing number of photons reaching the PMTs. The spherical mirrors
outside the vessel were updated to improve the light distribution on the PMT arrays.
It was found that using the condenser lenses from the CEDAR-W as opposed to the
CEDAR-N resulted in more significant photon yield.
The optimisation studies produced results with negligible differences between 3.8
and 4.1 bar as the increase of Cherenkov photons at high pressure was counteracted
by the improved light distribution on the PMT array at low pressures. Due to
H2safety considerations, the lower pressure of 3.8 bar was selected. The optics
sufficiently correct the chromatic dispersion at this pressure setting, producing a
Gaussian width of 0.4 mm for the kaon and pion rings at the diaphragm (see left
Figure 3.8). This configuration allowed for the selection of kaons with a 2 mm
CEDAR diaphragm, shown in Figure 3.8 with minimal light from the pion passing
through.

3.4.2 Hydrogen Safety
Compared to the setup of the CEDAR-W, several modifications were made due
to the requirements for the use of H 2within the ECN3 experimental hall. It was
expected that some negligible amount of gas would leak from the CEDAR-H , so
all precautions had to be taken in order to prevent the concentration of gas from
reaching dangerous levels between the Lower Explosive Limit (LEL) of 4 % and
Upper Explosive Limit of 75 % in air. Any area where there was a potential for
a H 2leak was designated as an ATEX zone 2. In the ATEX zones, there are
no electrical connectors, and all metal parts of the CEDAR-H are grounded to
prevent any ignition sources. During H 2operation, the diaphragm end switches are
manually isolated through a key located by the KTAG electronics rack, as these
are not ATEX rated and have the potential to create a spark. The ATEX zones
extended 80 cm from the CEDAR-H vessel and required experts had to have ATEX

3.5 Commissioning at CERN
The commissioning of the CEDAR-H in the NA62 beam-line was completed at
the start of the 2023 run. The goals of the commissioning were to ascertain the
operational woking point for kaon detection at NA62 and to ensure that the detector
was still operations after its transport and installation.
After the CERN beam experts conducted the start-of-run activities and the beam
was steered through the CEDAR-H vessel, the beam intensity was set at 10 % of
the NA62 nominal value, corresponding to a particle rate of 60 Hz. Periodic triggers
were used during the CEDAR-H commissioning in order to select all beam particles
and measure the beam composition. The periodic triggers are where a period is
selected between the start of burst (SoB) and end of burst (EoB) at which data was
collected. At the beam intensity selected, the period was set so that 2 ×105triggers
per spill was collected. For each triggered event, the KTAG data acquisition system
recorded the time and the channel ID of each PMT signal within a 100 ns time
window defined by the trigger. The data collected was then reconstructed using the
NA62 framework with the individual PMT signals clustered using an algorithm that
selects the signals within a 4 ns time window into candidates. As each individual
PMT signal was recorded, the light yield was defined using a different method when
compared to the test beam. In the test beam, the ratios of the coincidences were
used to calculate the light yield using statistical equations. However, at NA62,

data and the reduced noise level with the KTAG photon-detection system.

4.4 Conclusions
The search for DP at NA62 has continued and expanded from the study conducted
at NA48/2. Two methods for the background estimation have been presented: one
utilising MC samples and the other utilising the data itself. It was found that us-
ing the MC background estimation method produced uncompetitive results due to
the limited size of the samples produced. The data driven background estimation
however provided excellent results producing competitive limits for the mixing pa-
rameter across the DP mass search range. The result covers an area which is yet
to be covered by any other experiment at the time of writing. A final result will be
available when NA62 completes data taking in 2026 and the full dataset is used for
the analysis.

Over the past 75 years the study of kaon physics has been integral to the develop-
ment of the standard model. Since 2016, the NA62 experiment has been the leading
experiment in the field of kaon physics. However, with the NA62 experiment sched-

This chapter will discuss the potential future of kaon physics, the experiments that
could be used to further the field, and the work I contributed to these experiments.
5.1 High Intensity Kaon Experiments (HIKE)

5.2 Flexible Software
As briefly described in Section 2.5, the software for NA62 is split into three main
C++packages. NA62MC is a GEANT4-based Monte Carlo simulation package that
simulates the NA62 experiment. The MC utilises functions within GEANT4 to
construct the sub-detectors of NA62, including all their materials and structures. It
is possible to select a number of kaon decays to simulate in order to create signals
similar to what are produced in reality at the experiment. After the simulation is
complete, the software saves the signals in each detector in a ROOT file for use in
reconstruction or analysis. NA62Reconstruction is then used to reconstruct either
the Monte Carlo data or collected data from the experiment, producing hits within
the detectors and clustering them into candidates. NA62Analysis is used to analyse
the data produced from the reconstruction to conduct all the physics studies that
are performed at NA62.
The NA62FW was designed for use only with the NA62 experimental setup,

option for users to modify was to enable/disable sub-detectors. With the experiment

to be flexible and configurable enough to allow for the simultaneous use for NA62
data taking and physics analysis, as well as the development of new experiments.

5.2.1 Testing: Geometry and Magnetic Fields

validated to ensure that they were still correct with respect to the previous version.

completed using a virtual particle within the GEANT4 framework called a Geantino.

fields. A large square beam of these particles was sent through the simulation, and

was saved. Using this information it was possible to reconstruct the entire geometry
of each detector in order to check the placement and geometry to mm precision to

5.3 The KTAG upgrades for HIKE
To sustain the increased kaon rate, the KTAG required upgrades to the photon-
detection and data acquisition systems, allowing to reach the required time resolu-
tion of 15-20 ps and kaon identification efficiency of greater than 95 %. The expected
kaon rate in HIKE Phase 1 was 200 MHz, which corresponds to a maximum rate
of 10 MHz /cm2for the detected photons at the PMT array. This rate takes into
account the production of Cherenkov photons in CEDAR-H and the resultant ac-
ceptance of the photons on the PMT plane. It was proposed that the NA62 PMT
arrays were replaced with arrays of Micro-Channel Plate Photomultipliers (MCP-
PMTs). The MCP-PMTs would provide a single photon time resolution of 50-70 ps
depending on the model, a low dark rate of 1 kHz /cm2and the ability to sustain
the required photon rate. A downside of the MCP-PMTs is their limited lifetime
from the ageing of the photocathode caused by feedback ions from the residual gas
resulting in a rapid reduction in the QE [88]. This results in the rapid degradation of
PMT quantum efficiency with the increasing integrated anode charge. MCP-PMTs
are similar to normal PMTs except that the dynode section is replaced by a micro-
channel plate. An example diagram is shown in Figure 5.10, where a photon hits
the photocathode window, producing a single electron, which is then accelerated
towards the MCP by an electric field provided by a voltage difference across the
gap. The electron then collides with the walls of the MCP, producing secondary
electrons, which results in a cascade collected by the anodes below. The dual layer
design, or chevron, adds additional benefits compared to a single layer design such
as better gains due to the increased surface for secondary electrons to be produced

5.4 RICH Upgrade
The overall design of the NA62 RICH described in section 2.3.5 was well suited for
re-use within HIKE with an upgrade to the photon-detection system. The NA62
RICH photon detection system comprises two arrays on either side of the beam pipe,
equipped with Hamamatsu R7400-U03 PMTs placed in a pattern described in Figure
5.19. The PMTs have a single hit time resolution of 240 ps and a quantum efficiency
of 20 %. Due to the placement of the PMTs, the distance between the centre of
each PMT is 18 mm, which is constrained by the sensor size and the geometry of the
PMT itself. This distance between sensors was the main contributor to the single
hit position resolution of 4.7 mm, resulting in an overall ring radius resolution of
1.5 mm. The number of hits per ring candidate and the single hit time resolution
produced a time resolution of 80 ps. Due to the increased beam intensity, the main
requirement of the RICH for HIKE was a time resolution of 20 - 30 ps.

The proposed RICH photon detection system upgrade would replace the current
PMTs with a smaller sensor size, allowing for a smaller distance between sensor
centres and reducing the hit position resolution. An excellent candidate for a sensor
replacement was silicon photomultipliers (SiPMs). SiPMs with small sensor sizes,
high quantum efficiency of 40 % and a single hit time resolution of at least 100 ps
are available from major manufacturers. Three sensor sizes were selected for further
investigation, 9 ×9mm, 6×6mm, and 3 ×3mm. Each SiPM sensor size had apparent
benefits and downsides, and a balance between performance and complexity/price
had to be taken into account. The smaller sensors would produce a better hit
position resolution but at an increased cost and complexity. Whereas the larger
sensors would have worse hit position resolution but at reduced complexity and
cost. A study was conducted to determine the optimal sensor size for the SiPMs,
balancing overall detector performance with cost and complexity.

simulation software described in the previous sections.

increase the statistics. The simulation was run with the unforced flag which allows

158Chapter 6
Conclusions
The CEDAR-H was designed by The University of Birmingham and built at CERN
where it was characterised and tested at a test-beam in 2022. CEDAR-H was in-
stalled and commissioned in the NA62 beam-line for the 2023 run where a number
of pressure scans and other tests were completed to ensure that the detector was
ready for data taking. The detector produced excellent results with 13 % greater
light yield compared with CEDAR-W with N 2. CEDAR-H has been in operation
since 2023 and has perform flawlessly through out.
The search for DP at NA62 was conducted with two studies with different back-
ground estimation methods: MC and data-driven. The MC study did not produce
competitive results when compared to previous experiments in the search region so
the data-driven background method was used. Utilising the data-driven method the
UL of the mixing parameter squared was set and provides coverage across a region
which has not been covered by any other experiments. It is planned that this search
will continue by implementing data collected in the two final years of data taking
at NA62 to further enhance the result.
The NA62 software package was overhauled to allow for more flexibility. Thi
allowed for the implementation and testing of new detectors for future experiments
such as HIKE. The upgrades planned for the KTAG and RICH were presented
for use within HIKE Phase 1 and the detector rates for the expected positions
for HIKE Phase 2 were presented. The results from the simulations were used in
the HIKE proposal submitted in 2023 to the SPS committee. Unfortunately the
HIKE experiment was not approved. However, the upgraded software and studies
