The matter content of the SM comprises elementary fermions, Five vector bosons mediate the three fundamental forces, and a scalar boson is responsible for particle masses (Figure 1.1). There are six quark flavours: up, down, charm, strange, top, and bot- tom. Depending on their charge, they can be characterised into three generations or two types. There are also three lepton flavours, each with a corresponding neutrino. It is important to note that the SM is not a complete model of the universe. Any deviations from theoretical understanding could be a hint of physics BSM. 1.2 Kaon Physics The first possible observation of a heavy meson, a particle composed of a quark- antiquark pair, was in by Leprince-Ringuet and L’heritier [9, 10] and predated even the discovery of the pion in [11]. Utilising a cloud chamber to measure cosmic rays, they found the first evidence of a particle with a mass of times the mass of the electron (0 .511 MeV [12]). However, it was possible that the upper limit on the measured mass could reach the proton mass, so they were not credited for the discovery. In 1947, while similarly studying cosmic rays with cloud chambers, G. D. Rochester and C. C. Butler published a result which included two pictures showing forked tracks [13]. One of the tracks proved to show the decay of a neutral particle into two charged daughter particles, which turned out to be a decay of the neutral kaon to two charged pions. The other track showed the decay of a charged particle into charged and neutral daughter particles. This result was later confirmed in by a group in Cal-Tech who observed multiple instances of these decays [14]. However, between these publications, a group in Bristol used emulsion chambers to observe the decay of a charged particle into three charged particles [15], known then as the tau decay. This resulted in what was known as the tau-theta problem, where seemingly the same particle was decaying into two different final states with different parities: Theta to two pions (+1) and Tau to three pions (-1). This eventually led to the discovery that parity is not conserved in weak interactions [16]. In the early 1950s, these particles would be described as K-mesons, for which there are four interaction (weak) eigenstates ( K+,K−,K0and ¯K0). Experiments continued to measure the characteristics of the K mesons and found features such as unusually long lifetimes (10−10seconds) despite their rapid production [17]. These features arise from the fact the output of Kmesons occurs through the strong interaction, whereas their decays proceed through the weak interaction. These de- 81.2 Theoretical foundations Kaon Physics viations from the other previously discovered mesons, such as the pion, suggested the existence of a new quantum number named strangeness [18, 19]. The strange quantum number applies to all strongly interacting particles and is conserved in strong interactions but violated in weak ones. Into the late 1950s and early 1960s, the neutral kaon became the centre of a number of studies because their relative ease of production and long lifetimes made them easy to study. Despite the fact that parity is violated in weak interactions, it was still believed that the combination of CP would be conserved. Through experiment, the two mass (strong) eigenstates of the neutral kaon showed to have different lifetimes, which resulted in their names KLfor long and KSfor short [20]. Each of these eigenstates had different CP values with the KSwith CP= +1 decaying to two pions and the KLwith CP=−1 observed decaying to three pions. However, in 1964, it was observed that the KLdecayed to two pions [21] proving that the CP symmetry was indeed violated in weak decays. This decay is an example of indirect CP violation as it occurs through neural kaon mixing. The first evidence of direct CP violation, where the violation occurs within the decay, was provided by the NA31 experiment in [22]. The process was conclusively observed by KTeV and NA48 collaborations in the latter 1990s [23, 24]. The beam next passes through a mm field-free hole in a set of iron plates, placed between three m dipole magnets (B3). The vertical magnetic field in the iron plates is used to deflect muons. Any deviation in the beam due to stray fields in the beam hole is cancelled out by a pair of steering dipoles (TRIM2 and TRIM3). Two quadrupoles (Q7, Q8) follow and ensure that the beam is parallel before the KTAG, which comprises of a CEDAR and a purpose-built photon detection system, described in Section 2.3.1. Any area where there is a potential for a H 2leak is designated as an ATEX zone 2. In the ATEX zones, there are no electrical connectors, and metal parts of the CEDAR-H are grounded to prevent any ignition sources. During H operation, the diaphragm end switches are manually isolated through a key located by the KTAG electronics rack, as these are not ATEX rated and have the potential to create a spark. The ATEX zones extend cm from the CEDAR-H vessel and require experts to have ATEX training in order to access. Any confined space where the H can accumulate, such as the KTAG enclosure and the box containing the diaphragm potentiometer and motor is flushed with a constant supply of nitrogen to prevent the build-up of H 2. A safety interlock is installed to turn off the KTAG electronics in the event the supply of N 2is interrupted. Another interlock is in place to turn off the KTAG electronics if there is a sizeable pressure drop in the CEDAR-H vessel. The pressure sensor precision limits the defined pressure drop for the interlock. A small leak is defined to be a measured drop in pressure of 0.02 mbar per minute measured by a rolling average across an hour. A major leak interlock, which would represent a rupture of the aluminium windows, would be triggered if the pressure dropped by 0.1 mbar per second. This major leak interlock would cut power to the CEDAR and neighbouring areas, turning off vacuum pumps and the GTK. The aluminium windows have been tested to withstand a pressure of 7.5 bar before installation so that the safety valve would prevent any damage to the windows. NA62MC is a GEANT4-based Monte Carlo simulation package that simulates the NA62 experiment. The MC utilises functions within GEANT4 to construct the sub-detectors of NA62, including all their materials, volumes and structures. It is possible to select a number of kaon decays to simulate in order to create signals similar to what are produced in reality at the experiment. After the simulation is complete, the software saves the signals in each detector in a ROOT file for use in reconstruction or analysis. NA62Reconstruction is then used to reconstruct either the Monte Carlo data or collected data from the experiment, producing hits within the detectors and clustering them into candidates. NA62Analysis is used to analyse the data produced from the reconstruction to conduct all the physics studies that are performed at NA62. The NA62FW was designed for use only with the NA62 experimental setup, resulting in minimal configurability and flexibility. Within the simulation, the only option for users to modify was to enable/disable sub-detectors. With the experiment foreseen to run up to LS3, an upgraded software package is required to allow for the development of new detectors for future kaon experiments. The software had to be flexible and configurable enough to allow for the simultaneous use for NA62 data taking and physics analysis, as well as for the development of new experiments. To implement a flexible framework, all detector classes are refactored to inherit from a common class, which contained the essential functions and variables for all detectors. This allowed for all classes of the detectors to be parsed using the same machinery. The search for DP at NA62 was conducted with two studies with different back- ground estimation methods: MC and data-driven. The MC study did not produce competitive results when compared to previous experiments in the search region so the data-driven background method was used. Utilising the data-driven method the UL of the mixing parameter squared was set and provides coverage across a region which has not been covered by any other experiments. It is planned that this search will continue by implementing data collected in the two final years of data taking at NA62 to further enhance the result. The NA62 software package was overhauled to allow for more flexibility. Thi allowed for the implementation and testing of new detectors for future experiments such as HIKE. The upgrades planned for the KTAG and RICH were presented for use within HIKE Phase and the detector rates for the expected positions for HIKE Phase were presented.
